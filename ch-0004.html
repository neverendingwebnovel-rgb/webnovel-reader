The Tallied City · Chapter 4

One Day, Flat

Wren posted the chit before light on the eighth, while the Loss Room still held the cold of the night and nobody else had come up the stairs. It went through the slot without a sound. One hour of the seventh, paid for and gone.

She had counted the tin before she left her room, kneeling on the boards with her coat already buttoned. Eight quiet hours were left in it. She said the number aloud, to no one, the way she would have read a total back to Teague across the desks, and then put the lid on.

At desk nineteen the wardens' return on the cooper was waiting in the tray.

She knew the case before she had the sheet unfolded: the man from the lower town who had woken on the fifth without his wedding day, and the lucky-catch charms sold on the docks the night before. She had matched the loss to the charm herself and sent it down. It had come back up with more in it than she had sent.

Seller of charms (knotted cord and scale), taken near the fish stairs on the night of the fourth, gives up the three buyers. Buyer the first: a net-mender, did not use it. Buyer the second: a fisherman of the lower docks, out on the night tide of the fourth, in at first light with a full net. Buyer the third: a carter, did not use it. The fisherman is named as caster against the loss of the cooper. Award to be entered by the clerk of the line, under the Schedule of Restitution.

The clerk of the line was Wren.

She read it twice. Then she went down the row to desk twelve.

Pell had the booklet out of his drawer before she reached him. He held it up between two fingers, grey cover and all, and did not ask what she wanted it for.

"The cooper," he said. "I heard the runner. You'll want memory. It's near the back, after the senses."

It was near the back. The older type ran in two columns down the page, the loss on the left and the rate on the right, and the memory entries were short, because there was not much to say about them. A season. A month. A week. And at the foot, on a line to itself:

Memory, one day, flat.

She looked for the rest of it and there was no rest. The Schedule priced a year of life by trade and age; it priced a sense by which one. A day was a day. It did not ask whether the day had been spent hauling staves or standing up in church. Whatever the cooper had promised that morning, the Schedule had already decided what it was worth, generations ago, in type nobody had reset.

Wren took the booklet back to her desk and worked it the way she would have worked any line: the rate, no adjustment for trade, no adjustment for age, nothing carried. Then she dipped her pen and wrote the award out in words in the box at the foot of the return, as the form required, spelling the sum out so that no one downstairs could add a stroke to it. It took less than a line.

Somewhere on the docks a fisherman would be made to pay that. Somewhere in the lower town a man who didn't remember his own wedding would be handed it, and would have to take someone's word for what it was for.

That was how a payer got anything. A loss, a caster with a name, the Schedule. Three steps, and the middle one was the only hard one.

She thought of the Varleys' table, and of Nell Varley asking what they had bought. This was what they would get, a line like this one in words at the foot of a return, if there were a name to put in the box. There was a name on her district sheet, in her own hand. It had a question mark after it, and a question mark was not a name.

When she returned the booklet, Pell had his pen in his mouth. He took it out.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske," he said, "it's the same rate for a wedding as for a wet Thursday. I've always found that very even-handed of it." He put the booklet in his drawer and shut the drawer gently. "I'm sorry. For him, I mean. Not for the Schedule. The Schedule doesn't mind."

* * *

Her slip came back countersigned after the noon bell: one hour, purpose the inquiry. Once its chit went in tomorrow there would be seven.

She went out by the front steps, round the side of the Office to the top of Mill Steps, and down. She passed the fifth door without slowing and did not look at its chimney. At the foot of the lane the stone began to dip under her boots, and the river stairs dropped away to the brown water.

Jory Dace was on his step halfway down with the tray across his knees. He saw her coming a long way off, which was the trouble with stairs.

"Miss," he said. He did not say penny a knot.

She stopped two steps above him. "I wanted to ask about Rook. Where he sleeps. When he's likely to be about."

Jory looked at the tray. He straightened a red-thread knot that did not need straightening.

"You said yes to up top," he said. "You never said the Office."

"You never asked."

"No." He turned the brass pin of the knot round and round. "Somebody told me after. A clerk's coat and asking after Rook by name: that's not just up top. That's the Office." He glanced up the hill, though from the stairs there was nothing to see of it but roofs. "They had him once. Took him in the act, right here. Everybody on the water knows that."

She waited. Waiting was the one thing Teague had taught her that she was sure of.

Jory put the knot down and held out his left hand, palm up, then turned it over so she could see the back. Two of the fingers sat a little wrong, the way a mended chair leg sits a little wrong, straight enough to use and not quite in line with the others.

"Boat-winch," he said. "I was nine. Caught them in the gear, and my dad had to run it backwards to get them out. They were flat." He flexed the hand. The two fingers came along after the rest, but they came. "Rook set them. Here. On this stair, about where you're standing. He held my wrist and said something I didn't catch and I felt them go round again, like a sock pulled right way out."

"Did it hurt?"

"Yes." He didn't smile. "Nobody on this stair lost a thing for it. I asked round after, every door. My mam made me. Nobody went blind, nobody lost a year, nobody's babby forgot its name. Nothing."

Wren looked at the hand, and then up the stairs, to where the lane began and the bakehouse doors went climbing toward the Office's back wall.

"On the stair," she said. She said it gently, as if checking a figure.

Jory heard what she meant. She watched him hear it: that he had asked down here, on the stairs, and nobody had asked up there, on the lane, where a widow had gone blind and a lamplighter had lost a winter and a baker had lost forty years. His face shut.

"I shouldn't have said his name to you," he said.

"Jory."

"I shouldn't. He fixed my hand and I gave him to the Office for a penny." He looked at the penny knots on his tray as if they had done it. "I'm not telling you where he lives. I don't know it anyway, not proper. And I wouldn't."

He did not offer her anything from the tray, and she did not ask. After a moment she said thank you, which was absurd, and climbed back up the stairs, then the lane. At the top she went round to the front steps, and she was at desk nineteen with eleven minutes of her hour unspent.

* * *

The floor emptied at six. Wren's time after that was her own and cost her nothing.

She wanted to see the lane lit, the way it would have been on the night of the fifth, and so she went out by the front steps and down the street, and round the foot of the hill to the river side, the long way she had taken the first time. She came to Mill Steps from the bottom as the lamps were going on.

The lamplighter was three lamps up from the river stairs, reaching. The pole was taller than he was by half again, and he handled it as if he had built it.

"Evening," he said, before she had decided whether to speak. "You'll want to keep left on the greasy bit, miss. Everyone goes right and everyone goes over."

She kept left. He lit the lamp and swung the pole down and fell in beside her without being asked, the way a dog does on a long road.

"Kit Morrow," he said. "That's me. This is my round. Well, it's the parish's round, but it's mine to walk." He lifted the pole a little higher. "Hook's new. Brass. I bought that out of my first quarter."

He was about sixteen, she thought, with a voice still breaking and a cap pushed back to show he wasn't afraid of the weather. He lit the next lamp in one movement, and she understood he wanted her to see that he could.

"How long have you had the round?"

"Since old Abel lost his winter." He said it plainly, the way people on the lane said such things. "He woke up one morning near the thaw and the whole winter was gone out of him. Every day of it, where he'd been, what he'd lit, all gone. His knees weren't up to the steps after that anyway. So they gave me the pole." He considered the lamp ahead. "I'd have rather got it some other way."

Wren had entered old Abel's winter in her own hand. She had not known his name. She did not tell Kit that.

They climbed. Kit talked, and she let him: the cost of oil, which doors left their slops on the steps, a gull that sat on the third lamp from the top and wouldn't be moved. Near the middle of the lane, where the stairs gave way to the slope, he stopped to trim a wick.

"I go round twice," he said. "Once to light, and once late, before midnight, to see to the ones that smoke. This one smokes." He worked at it. "I saw him that night, you know. The night the baker went old."

She did not ask who. The floor had taught her to let a talker talk.

"The fifth," Kit said. "Late. I was on the middle lamps, and a man came up out of the river stairs with a hooded lantern and went by me. Not hurrying. He had his hands held careful, like this." He held his own out in front of him, a little stiff, as if they were full of something that might spill. "He went in at a door near the top of the lane and he came out again before midnight, because I was still on the last lamps by then, and he went back down the way he came."

"Which door?"

"Couldn't say." Kit frowned at the wick. "Up by the bakehouse end. It was dark, miss, that's why they pay me." He looked at her sideways, pleased. "Rook, I'd say. Everybody knows his walk."

Under his lamp, Wren took out her notebook and wrote it down. Witness: lamplighter, Mill Steps (K. Morrow). Night of the fifth, late. Man with hooded lantern up from the river stairs, in at a door near the top, out before midnight. Rook (?) seen on the lane.

She underlined nothing. It was Kit's word, and a boy's word at that, about a dark lane and a walk he thought everybody knew. But it was a man on Mill Steps on the night the baker aged, coming up from the stairs where Rook worked, and it was more than she'd had that morning. A warden would take it. She knew that as she wrote it. She knew, too, how much she had wanted something to write.

Kit lit the last lamp near the top and wished her goodnight, and went whistling round the side of the hill with the pole over his shoulder like a pike.

* * *

On the ninth she posted the chit for the eighth's hour before light. That left seven.

She went down the hill the other way, toward the city and the river, while the lamps were still lit and the fish market was only beginning to shout. Saltmarket Yard lay behind it, through an arch where the gulls stood in a row and watched the carts. The yard was a parish ground and an old one, so crowded that the stones leaned on each other for company, and the newer ones stood in the gaps between the older ones wherever a spade would go in. The smell of the market came over the wall and sat on everything.

Teague's hole was near the back wall. Two of the undertaker's men stood by it with their caps in their hands and their breath going up. Dunmore stood at the head of it in grey, her keys gathered in one gloved hand so that they would not ring.

And at the foot, with a black band pinned crooked round his sleeve, stood Osric Pell.

Wren stopped on the path. Two days ago Pell had said there was not a soul to stand at the edge of a hole for Teague. He looked at her now as if daring her to mention it.

"Between ourselves," he said, when she came up beside him, "I don't like being wrong in public. But I like it better than the alternative."

It was short. Nobody read anything. The men let the box down on its ropes, and it went down crooked and was put right, and the ropes came up. Dunmore stood without moving. The gulls on the arch shifted and settled.

When the first earth went in, Dunmore spoke without turning her head.

"I had a desk on the same floor as him, my first year," she said. "A junior's desk. He went through my first quarter with me line by line, one evening, after the floor had gone." She watched the spade. "He made me do it again. Twice."

"Was it wrong?"

"The first time." Her mouth moved. "The second time he said it was untidy."

Dunmore said no more. Wren had worked opposite the man for four years and had never once imagined him young enough to correct anyone's first quarter, or Dunmore green enough to need it. She found she could see both of them, the lamp, the empty floor, the pen going down the column.

The men filled in. Dunmore did not look at the hole again until it was level. Then she turned to Wren.

"Have you found your line, Miss Aske?"

The district sheet was in Wren's coat, folded small, with Rook (?) in ink against the three Mill Steps lines. In her notebook, since last night, was a lamplighter's word.

"Not yet," Wren said.

Dunmore nodded as if that was the answer she had expected, and perhaps it was.

Wren counted it as she stood there, because counting was what she did. What Teague had really said, she had kept. The razor, she had kept. And now a name, asked for to her face, at his grave. Three.

At the arch, Dunmore stopped and unbuttoned her coat and took something out of the inside of it. It was the peppermint tin: the only thing, Wren understood, that had not gone into the box with him.

"He never offered anyone one," Dunmore said. She held it out, closed, the lid square on it. "I think he'd rather the clerk opposite had them than the ground."

Wren took it. The tin was warm from Dunmore's coat. Dunmore buttoned the coat again, nodded once to Pell, and went out under the arch toward the market without looking back.

Pell had watched the whole of it. He said nothing. His eyes stayed on the tin in Wren's hand a moment longer than was polite, and then he said his knees would take the long way up, and took it.

Wren walked back up the hill on her own. Halfway, where the street steepened and the clock over the Office's front steps came into sight above the roofs, she stopped and got her thumbnail under the lid.

The peppermints had gone soft and grey and stuck together in one lump, a dozen of them, the sugar sweated into a single slab. She tipped the lump up with one finger. It came away from the bottom of the tin in a piece.

Under it lay a small brass disc, no bigger than her thumbnail. One edge of it was notched, a row of little square bites, uneven. On its face was stamped a clock, tiny and exact, the hands at the hour, the numerals too small to read. She looked up the street at the clock over the front steps, and down again. The face was the same.

She turned it over. The back was plain. It was not a coin; it had no value stamped on it. It was not a button; it had no shank. It was not like anything she had seen in all her time on the floor, or in the seizure lists, or on the wardens' trays.

She put the peppermints back on top of nothing, closed the tin, and put the disc in her coat pocket, where the razor was, and the penny knot. Then she went on up the hill to work.

* * *

The day went by in lines. She entered two losses from the river parishes, both matched to charms before noon. The district sheet stayed folded in her coat, and she did not take it out once.

After the floor had emptied she went down to the river stairs on her own time, out by the front steps, round the side, and down the lane.

The lamps were lit on Mill Steps. Kit was nowhere about. From the foot of the lane the stairs went down into the blue of the evening, and Jory's step was empty, swept clean, no tray.

Halfway down she stopped.

At the bottom, where the stone went green and the water came up over it and went back, a man was kneeling. Beside him, on the last dry step, sat a boatman's boy with his hand held out, palm up, and the palm laid open across the middle by a hook. A skiff knocked at the stairs behind them.

The man had the boy's wrist in his left hand. His right hand was inside his coat. Wren watched for it to come out, and it did not.

He said something, too low to carry up the stairs, and short, and he did not say it twice.

The cut closed. It did not flash or glow. It closed the way a seam closes when someone draws the thread up from the far end: from one side of the palm to the other, the edges coming to meet each other, the red going in after them until there was a line, and then less than a line. The boy made a sound like someone stepping into cold water.

That was all. She had read four years of what it cost, and it had taken about as long as tying a knot.

The man let go of the boy's wrist. The boy looked at his own hand, and closed it, and opened it, and said what might have been thank you, and was down in the skiff and pushing off before the man had moved.

Then the man got up, and Wren saw his walk, the one Kit had said everybody knew. It was quick and sure on the wet stone, a young man's walk. It did not go with the rest of him. His hair was white and had been cut short by someone who didn't care, possibly himself, in the dark. His face, when it turned toward the river, had the look of a man who had had to stop and look up the boy's name before he could say it, and might have to look it up again tomorrow. He was older than his walk by a long way.

He sat down on the step above the water. His right hand stayed in his coat. He held his left out in front of him and opened it, and closed it, slowly, and opened it again, and looked at it each time, as if he were checking that it was still the one he had come down with.

Wren found she had already done the sum without meaning to. A palm's worth of someone's year. Or someone's eyes, a little. Or a day of someone's memory, up the lane, tonight, and in the morning a knock at a door and a line in her book. Or nobody's. She couldn't tell from here. Nobody could tell from here. That was the whole of the problem, and she was standing on it.

The man did not look round.

"You'll be the clerk from up top," he said.

The water came up over the green step and went back.

"Yes," said Wren.
