The Tallied City · Chapter 2

The Clerk's Own Hours

The runner came up the stairs at half past ten. Wren knew what the folded slip in his hand was before he'd found her desk, because paper went down from the Loss Room to the floor below, and only one kind ever came back up.

Deputy Registrar. At once.

She capped her ink, laid the baker's report on top of the seizure lists that had told her nothing, and went. Behind her the Loss Room went on scratching, every nib keeping its own pace. Nobody looked up, and nobody needed to. They had all seen the tray outside Dunmore's door that morning, and they could all count to two.

Downstairs, the door past the stairwell was unlocked for once. Dunmore sat behind a blotter the colour of old liver, and on it, squared to the edges, lay both sheets. The Office of the Tally, Vellmark: Quarterly Return of Losses. Wren's own, and Teague's beneath it. On each, BALANCED was struck through in black and signed in a small upright hand. W. Aske. W. Aske.

Wren had spent most of the night deciding what the cellar would smell like. She stood with her hands folded and waited to be told.

Dunmore pressed her lips together. The keys at her waist shifted on their chain.

"Do you know what happens," she said, "to a clerk who signs a return unbalanced?"

"I've heard the cellar, ma'am. Or a letter. Or docked hours."

"Those happen afterwards. First," Dunmore said, "paperwork."

It was Teague's old joke, near enough word for word. He used to say it the way other men said amen. Wren had nothing at all to do with her face.

Dunmore laid one hand flat beside the returns. "An unbalanced return opens an Inquiry of Imbalance. It's an old rule. I've seen it used twice. The signing clerk undertakes to find the line that won't close, and to show it. Or she withdraws her signature."

"Withdraws it."

"Strikes through the other word. Initials the change. Takes up her post in the cellar records, where nothing needs to balance." Dunmore tapped the top return. "The inquiry must be closed by the Office's year-end."

"Settling Day."

"Settling Day. The end of the coming quarter." Dunmore said it as she might have said Thursday. Outside, in a few months, the whole city would hang out its bunting and close its accounts, and not one person in it could have told you what was owed. The Office closed its books the same day. It always had. "The inquiry is conducted in the clerk's own quiet hours. The Office doesn't pay for a clerk's doubts."

Wren thought of the tin under her bed. Eleven hours. Four years of not spending them.

"Mr. Teague's quarter," she began.

"Is yours now. You signed it. One inquiry covers both." Dunmore bent, unlocked the deep drawer at her right knee and pulled it out for the warrant form.

The drawer was full. Wren saw the edge of the stack before she saw what it was: a block of forms as thick as a hall register, each one Report of Loss (Personal), Staff, each with its cause box stamped in red. UNATTRIBUTED. UNATTRIBUTED. The one on top was crisp, filled in yesterday afternoon in Dunmore's own hand. Teague, Halloran. Forty-one years. Beneath it the forms went down and down, the paper yellowing as it went, until the bottom ones were the colour of tea.

Once in a long while, Dunmore had said, standing over a dead man.

Dunmore took a single sheet from beside the stack, printed in a type nobody had used in years, the capitals spurred like old ironwork, and the drawer slid shut. She wrote her name on the warrant and blotted it, and for a moment she didn't hand it over.

"Did Mr. Teague say anything to you," she said, "before?"

Wren had the words ready. If you want to know who pays, look at who's first in the queue. She could feel the shape of them behind her teeth.

"He said the tea had gone off," she said.

Dunmore looked at her for a long moment. "The tea."

"He said it most days, ma'am. But he said it yesterday."

"Nothing about his work."

"He was always talking about his work. I mostly didn't listen." That was true, and Wren was sorry for it, and she could see Dunmore deciding it wasn't true at all.

Dunmore's lips pressed together again. She held out the warrant. "There we are."

Wren went back upstairs with it, and the whole way she was certain the Deputy Registrar knew exactly why forty-one years had ended at a desk, and was waiting to see how much Wren had guessed.

* * *

Osric Pell had heard by the time she reached the top step. Osric Pell heard everything; it was most of what he did at desk twelve.

"Inquiry of Imbalance," he said, swinging round on his stool with a cheerful creak. He was a soft, comfortable man of fifty or so, his waistcoat buttoned one hole wrong, as it had been every day Wren had known him. "Now there's a thing I haven't seen since I was your age. Between ourselves, Miss Aske, I'll take those hours off your hands."

"I haven't said how many I have."

"Eleven," said Pell kindly. "Everybody knows you've never spent one. It's a sort of landmark." He named his price, by the hour. It was more than Wren paid in rent for a week, and a good deal more than he'd given the clerk on the end desk last year, when the man's mother died and he'd needed the money more than the time. "Before you waste them on the street. The rate's gone up. Everyone wants an hour these days. Between ourselves, I couldn't tell you why."

"No," said Wren. "Thank you."

"Offer stands." He swung back to his ledger, untroubled. "It'll be lower by Settling Day. Or higher. That's the beauty of it."

She took a quiet-hour slip from the clerk at the end of the row, the first she had ever asked for, and filled it in at desk nineteen. One hour, against eleven held. Chits to be surrendered from the clerk's own keeping on the morning following. Purpose: Inquiry of Imbalance, warrant attached. She finished the morning's entries while it went round for a countersignature, because the Office did not pay for her doubts, and she would not give it the satisfaction of saying they had cost it an hour. It came back after the noon bell, initialled.

At half past one, for the first time in four years, Wren Aske put on her coat in the middle of a working day and walked out of the Loss Room.

Tuppence was sitting on the landing beside the tea urn, square-shouldered and grey, her notched ear turned to the stairwell. She watched Wren go down with the expression of a cat who has seen clerks leave before and expects to see this one come back.

* * *

The Office stood at the top of its hill the way a stopped clock stands on a mantel, and Wren had looked out from it for so long that she'd half forgotten it could be looked at.

Below the front steps the street fell away in a long, worn curve, and below that was Vellmark: the old river city, roof over roof, with the River Vell running brown and slow through the middle of it. From up here she could see the river's bends and the grey smudge of the far bank. Then the street turned, the houses closed in, and the river was only a smell of mud and coal smoke coming up the lanes.

Vellmark had never pulled anything down if it could build on top of it. Wren's lane ran over another lane. She could see it at the stair-turns, where a flight of steps went down past a row of doorways bricked up to the lintel, their old house numbers still painted on the brick in a style nobody painted in any more. A water pump stood at one corner, its handle long gone and its spout stopped with lead. The pump wore a little iron plate with a word chiselled off it, the stone around the plate scarred as if someone had worked hard to make sure. Over a cobbler's door hung an empty bracket, the sign taken down so long ago that ivy had grown along the iron where it used to swing.

Every door had its guild mark. Some were cut into the lintels, some pressed into lead plates, some only scratched into the wood and blacked in with soot: the cobblers' last, the coopers' hoop, the chandlers' wick and flame. A few of the oldest doors carried two marks, the first one worn nearly smooth and a second cut fresh beside it, as if the house had changed hands and nobody had cared to scrape off the past. Wren had seen all of these marks a thousand times at the foot of reports. She had never seen so many of them at once on doors that were still opening and shutting.

She looked back once. From down here the Office was a dusty roof among a hundred other dusty roofs, a little higher than most. Nobody on the street was looking at it.

The lanes took her round the foot of the hill to the river side, and so she came to Mill Steps from the bottom. It dropped from right behind the Office, down the back of the hill toward the Vell, so steep that the lane was stairs for half its length and a slope of greased cobbles for the rest. In four years of front steps she had never once asked what lay behind her desk. The bakehouses stood shoulder to shoulder all the way up, every chimney blackened and every door marked with the bakers' wheatsheaf. Most of them smelt of bread. One of them didn't.

The Varleys' door was the fifth from the top, which meant climbing nearly all of it. When Wren turned on the step to knock, she saw the Office's back wall right above her, close enough to count its windows. One row of them was the Loss Room's. She could have pointed to desk nineteen.

* * *

Nell Varley opened the door no wider than her own shoulders. She was a small woman with flour in the creases of her knuckles and a face that had made up its mind before Wren had said a word. She took the warrant and held it out at arm's length to read, her mouth moving on the long words.

"Inquiry," she said. "Is that better than a report?"

"It means somebody has to look."

Nell looked at her a while longer, then stood aside.

The bakehouse was cold. That was the first wrong thing, and the worst. A bakehouse oven is never let go out, and this one had been out since the night before. The long kneading trough under the window held the overnight dough, set on the fifth for the morning bake. It had risen and fallen again and gone grey and sour, and it gave off a smell like a cellar full of old beer.

Tobin Varley sat at the table in a shirt made for a younger man. It hung off his collarbones. His hair was white and thin, his face fallen in round the mouth, and the hands lying on the table were knotted at every joint, swollen and spotted and still. A dough-scraper lay between them. While Wren watched, he tried to close his fingers round its handle. They curled most of the way and stopped.

"Thirty-one," he said, in a voice that had gone thin too. "I'm thirty-one. I'd like that written down somewhere, if it's all the same to you."

"It's written down," said Wren. "I wrote it."

She sat across from him and did it properly, because doing it properly was the only thing she had brought. Had he wanted anything that night? Bought anything, from anyone, at any price? Wished aloud, or under his breath? Quarrelled with a neighbour, a customer, a rival, a stranger on the steps?

No, he said. And no. He had banked the oven and set the dough and gone up to bed at nine, as he did every night of his life. He hadn't wished for anything but sleep. He owed nobody money. He had argued with the miller in the spring about the weight of a sack, and they had shaken on it.

"And Nell," Tobin said. "I'll not say I've never quarrelled with Nell."

"You've never once won," said Nell from the doorway.

Wren wrote it all down. They were the same answers she had read that morning in the warden's hand. Caster: none known. Charm or article: none found. Witness: none. Only now there was a man attached to it, and a smell of sour dough.

"The guild's been," Nell said. "First thing. Very sorry. Very proper. A man who can't knead can't bake, and a man who can't bake comes off the working register at the end of the month. They'll let him keep the mark on the door." She laughed once, not pleasantly. "That's kind of them."

"Mrs. Varley—"

"He paid for something." Nell came in from the doorway and put both floury hands flat on the table, next to her husband's. "That's how it works, isn't it? That's what your Office is for. Somebody gets something and somebody pays. Well, he paid. Forty years, he paid. So tell me what we bought."

Wren looked at the four hands on the table, two of them seventy years old and two of them still young, and found she could not answer.

"I'll find out," she said, and was surprised to hear herself say it as though it were already on paper.

* * *

On the step outside, the cold went off her a little. Then she saw the doorframes.

The door to the left of the Varleys' and the door to the right had each had something nailed up beside the wheatsheaf since she'd knocked. On the left, three little bundles hung from a single nail. On the right there were five. Each was a twist of twine knotted round a bent pin, the sort of thing a child might make. On the doors farther down the step she could see more of them, pale against the dark wood.

"Debt charms," said a man on the next step down. He was sweeping flour off his threshold and had been watching her look. "Fellow's been selling them on the steps since first light. Penny a knot. Hang it by the door and the next one goes to somebody else." He leaned on the broom. "Don't suppose your Office has an opinion."

Wren had seen twenty such charms in seizure lists, and every one had been twine and nonsense. She didn't have the heart to tell him so. "Does it work?"

"Can't hurt." He looked up the steps toward the Varleys' cold chimney. "Mill Steps has always been unlucky. Ask anyone. The widow two doors up went blind in her sleep this spring. Sat up in bed in the morning and couldn't find the window. And the old fellow who lit the lamps along here, he lost a winter. Clean gone. Woke up thinking it was the autumn before and asked after a dog that had been dead for months." He went back to sweeping. "So you see. Unlucky."

Wren knew both losses before he had finished telling them. She had entered the widow's sight at her old desk, opposite Teague's, and the lamplighter's winter too, in her own upright hand, and she had written UNATTRIBUTED beside each of them and turned the page.

* * *

Going back was the short way: up the last of Mill Steps, and round the side of the Office to its front steps. By the time Wren reached them the clock over the door stood at a few minutes past half three, and the light was going long and yellow in the lanes. One quiet hour, she had put on the slip. Two, as it turned out. Nine left in the tin. She supposed the Office would be glad to know its hill had been charged to her account.

The Loss Room was as she had left it, minus her. Pell lifted two fingers from his pen in a friendly salute and didn't ask.

She fetched her own ledger from her old desk and opened it at desk nineteen: the entries behind the sheet she had signed last night, and the two quarters before them. She had always kept them by date, because the Office kept everything by date. This time she went through the lines one by one on a spare sheet, sorting by street instead.

It was slow. Half the entries gave only a district and a few named nothing but their side of the Vell. She went on anyway, through the noise of forty pens, until the windows had gone from grey to dark grey.

Of the forty-two in the quarter just signed, seven fell within a few streets of the Office's hill, and one of the seven was Teague, dead in the Loss Room itself at the top of it. The widow's sight was among them too; the lamplighter's winter was in the quarter before. With the baker pencilled in at the foot, though he belonged to the new quarter, Mill Steps came up three times, once a quarter. The two older quarters showed the same shape, only thinner.

Seven in forty-two. It was not nothing. It was not much, either. A clerk who brought seven lines to the Deputy Registrar and called them a pattern would be told, kindly, about chance and small numbers, and the clerk would deserve it. Wren knew that. She had told other people the same, about their own sevens.

But the hill and the lanes below it were a sliver of Vellmark, a few streets you could see from the Office's back windows, and the other thirty-five were scattered across the whole city, both banks of the Vell, hardly two in the same street. Seven was too small to prove anything. It was still too many for one hill.

* * *

She took one more sheet. It wasn't Office paper. It was a plain foolscap leaf from the back of her own drawer, the kind she meant to fold into her coat and take home, where nothing she wrote could be filed. She ruled a column down it with the back of Teague's closed razor and dipped her pen.

She knew what went at the top. She had known since last night, from the one heading left in his ledger: unmatched — by distr—, cut off where the blade went through. All afternoon she had been doing his sum without letting herself say so.

She wrote it in her small, upright hand, and this time she finished the word.

unmatched — by district

The nib was still wet. Someone had done this work before her. He had sat at this desk and sorted the city's losses by street instead of date, and then someone had cut it out of his book and left the word to end where it ended. She had carried it on past the edge, in ink. It was her column now, as much as his quarter was. Wren sat with the pen in her hand and tried to work out whether she was following Teague, or whoever had come after him with the razor.

Tuppence came across the desk without a sound, stepped over the inkwell and sat down on the sheet, squarely on the heading, as if to say that would do for one day.

Outside, below the back windows, the lamps were coming on along Mill Steps one by one. The man lighting them was young, and new to it.
