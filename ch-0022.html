The Tallied City · Chapter 22

The Inkstand

The twenty-ninth. The tea came up at ten, and Pell was at the urn before the cups were set out.

Wren had watched him on and off since the early bell. He had sat down at twelve without his word for anyone, opened his ledger at the morning's page and left it at that page, and three times she had seen his pen go to the inkwell and come back to the paper and lift off again with nothing written. Now he stood beside the urn with a cup in his hand and nothing in the cup, and the clerks were only beginning to push back their chairs along the row.

The first of them out onto the landing was the clerk from desk nine, who said "Morning, Mr. Pell" as he went by on Pell's right, reaching for a cup. Pell did not answer him. The clerk shrugged at the back of Pell's head and took his tea away.

Wren filled her own cup and came round to him. The tea was as black as it ever was by ten, and smelt of the urn.

"Mr. Pell," she said, from his right.

He did not turn. The tap ticked into the drip tray. She said it again, a little louder, and he turned to her, his whole head and his shoulders with it, his eyes staying where his head put them, the way a man turns who has had to learn it since breakfast.

"The right one," he said, low. Behind them the row was coming out onto the landing, two and three together, talking of nothing, and one or two of them looked at the pair of them by the urn and away again. "Gone when I woke. I lay a good while thinking the street had stopped. Then a pan went down in the yard below my window, and I heard it with the one side, and I knew." He looked past her at the stairhead. "Between ourselves, Miss Aske, you'll want to stand this side of me."

She went round to his left.

"You said the next one," he said. "I've had it since the bell. I thought it would keep till ten, and it has. Ten's the first time a man stands up on this floor without the row lifting its head."

He looked at the urn, and at the cups in their row on the tray, the white rims, the hands reaching past him for them.

"They'll have me with tomorrow's pot," he said.

Wren set her cup down on the tray, full, and did not drink from it.

"We'll go down now, Mr. Pell," she said. "Before it's poured round."

She went to the stairhead and started down, past the turn, and did not look back. Behind her, after a moment, his tread came onto the stair.

* * *

Dunmore's door was shut. Wren knocked and was told to come in.

The Deputy Registrar was at her desk with the lamp unlit and the window's grey on the blotter, and her inkstand still stood on the torn leaf, one corner of Wren's handwriting showing from under its foot. She looked at Wren and then past her at Pell, who had come in on her heels and shut the door behind him with great care, as a man shuts a door he can no longer hear close.

"Mr. Pell has a loss to bring in, ma'am."

Pell came forward to the desk and stood a little sideways to it, his left ear toward Dunmore and his hands behind his back. He did not say anything yet. He was waiting to be asked. Dunmore saw which side he had turned to her, and her eyes stayed on it a breath.

She unlocked the deep drawer and drew it out to its stop. The block lay inside, the stamped forms squared one on another, Teague's on top with the red word beside its box. She did not touch the block. She reached past it to the back of the drawer, took a blank from the few there and laid it on her blotter, and dipped her pen.

"Desk," she said.

"Twelve, ma'am."

"The loss."

"The hearing of the right ear, ma'am."

Her pen went down and stopped at the end of the word. "The right, Mr. Pell."

"The right, ma'am. The left's as it was."

"And the time."

"On waking, ma'am. This morning. I'd put an hour to it if I could, but I was asleep for the hour."

She wrote it out as he gave it, at her ordinary pace and no faster, and she asked him nothing the form did not ask. The nib was the only sound in the room. Wren read the line as it went down.

desk twelve · the hearing of the right ear · on waking, the twenty-ninth

Dunmore read it through once from the beginning, her pen held off the paper, and blotted it and took the stamp out of her pocket.

She had it over the box marked Cause, and she did not bring it down. She looked up at Wren. It was not a look Wren had had from her before: the Deputy Registrar asking a clerk of the Loss Room what she should write.

Wren looked at the form. There was the box for Cause, ruled heavier than everything round it, and nowhere on the whole sheet a place to write the Office. One word from her and the box would hold it, and Pell would carry it up the stair in his good ear, and the row would have it with the pot. The word was the Office's, and so was the form.

"There's no other word on the form, ma'am."

She said it in her ordinary voice. Her mouth was dry after, as if she had read out a whole quarter.

Dunmore brought the stamp down. When she lifted it, UNATTRIBUTED stood in red beside the empty box.

Pell bent an inch toward the desk and read it upside down. The Deputy, who could tell him what belonged in that box, sat a yard from him with the stamp still in her fingers. He straightened. He had only to turn his good ear to her and ask. He looked at his own cuff a moment, the button there, and then at Dunmore.

"Between ourselves, ma'am," he said, "I'll not ask what's in the box."

Dunmore held his look. Then she put the stamp back in her pocket.

"It goes in the Tally, Mr. Pell."

"Ma'am."

She took the form by its edges and laid it in the drawer on top of Teague's, square to it, and left the drawer open. None of them moved. The red on it was still wet.

* * *

A little after four Ruddock laid down his ruler and went down the stair, came back up a quarter of an hour later, sat, and took the ruler up again; Wren did not ask him.

At six she went down the front steps beside Pell, and at the foot of them, without being asked, she moved round to his left. He glanced down at her, and buttoned his coat to the neck, and said nothing about it.

The street was going home ahead of them, shutters coming across and a boy sweeping a step, and the smell of the bakehouses coming up from the turnings. A wind came up the hill off the river, and Pell turned his collar up against it on the left, and then, a step later, on the right. They were past the bakers' hall before Pell spoke.

"The boy went down at four," he said. "He'll have asked."

Wren did not answer. A cart came up the street toward them on Pell's right, and she watched him not hear it until its wheel was level with him, and then start, and step in toward her, and go on as if he had meant to.

"I'll tell you a thing about the urn, Miss Aske," he said, when it had gone by. "Thirty years, and never two inside one month. Not once." He walked on a few steps. "Tomorrow they'll have two."

"Why did you bring it in, Mr. Pell?" she said. "You kept the oranges nine years."

"You asked," Pell said.

Nobody else had. He did not add to it for a dozen steps, and she did not want him to.

"I'll be a week at the urn," he said then. "After that I'm only a man who turns his head. There's worse to be on that floor."

They went on down together, Wren on his left, a little slower than the street, until the corner where his way turned off, and there he stopped.

"Tomorrow, Miss Aske," he said. "Left side."

She saw him off along the turning, close in to the houses on his deaf side, until the bend took him. Desk nineteen on the fifth, and desk twelve on the twenty-ninth: the line had come for two desks before the month was out.

* * *

The thirtieth, the month's end. By eleven the morning's bundle was entered, and Wren had blotted the last line of it. Across from her Ruddock was ruling, as he had ruled every morning since he came, the finished sheets squared at his right hand and the quire at his left gone thin. He did not look up from it when Dunmore came up the row with nothing in her hands.

The card was inside her coat.

"Mr. Ruddock," Dunmore said.

Along the row pens stopped. At the far end Fenn looked up from his tray, and a little way down somebody's chair, half pushed back, stayed half pushed back.

Ruddock stood up. He looked once at Wren, across the gap, a plain look, asking her nothing, and then he went out after the Deputy Registrar to the stair.

Wren laid her pen down. Up the row Pell, who had not heard the pens stop, was the last to look up, and he went back to his ledger.

If she went up behind them, it would be the twenty-fifth again, a clerk on the landing who had not been sent for.

She went after them. The main stair went up flight by flight above the Loss Room's floor, a landing and a shut door to each, and what light there was came up the well behind her and thinned as she climbed. She kept a flight back, hearing Dunmore's step and the boy's heavier one over her head, and the boy's stopping once, on a landing, and going on. At the landing where it gave out she went to the back of it and up the last flight, steep and narrow, as far as its turn, out of sight of the landing above.

Her foot had already lifted for the next tread. She set it down on the one she stood on, and put her shoulder to the plaster. It was his to do; she had told him at his father's gate all she would, and the rest was on the card. The plaster was cold through her sleeve, and she let it be cold.

Above her two people's weight came onto the boards and stood still.

"I'd like to read it first, ma'am," Ruddock said. His was a lower-town voice, made for a yard, and it came down the flight whole.

Wren did not hear Dunmore answer. She heard the card come out of the coat, a stiff small sound, and then a board give under the boy's weight as he took it, and then nothing. Far down the well somebody on the Loss Room's floor coughed, and a door shut on a lower landing, and the nothing went on. Seven lines, read through once from head to foot, came to about nine of her breaths. She counted. She came to nine, and went on, and came to eighteen, and was some way past it before the boy spoke again.

"Read it me, ma'am," Ruddock said.

Dunmore read the first line. Wren could hear the shape of it and not the words, the Deputy's voice pitched for the landing and no further. Ruddock said it after her, louder, every word given its full weight. Dunmore read the second line, and Ruddock said it. Dunmore read the third, the fourth and the fifth, a line at a time, and after each one Ruddock said it, and each time he waited until she had quite finished before he began.

Dunmore read the sixth line.

Ruddock's voice did not come. She counted one breath, and two, and three, her eyes on the tread above her, the worn dip in the middle of it. On the fourth breath Ruddock said the sixth line. Dunmore read the last line, and Ruddock said that too.

Nothing came through the slot.

For a while nobody above her moved. Then Dunmore said something too low to carry down the flight, and Ruddock said, "Ma'am," and a single step crossed the boards.

Ruddock came down the narrow flight, not quickly, his hands at his sides, and round the turn. He found her there. He was not surprised. He stopped a tread above her, so that she looked up at him. His face was red along the cheekbones and otherwise quite still.

"You read it," Wren said.

"Every line, miss." He looked down at the stair, and back at her. "Somebody's to sit at that desk. It might as well be one that's read it."

* * *

From noon Ruddock had a ledger open on the desk opposite nineteen, and his mother's black pen in his hand, with ink on it at last. He entered slowly, a line and a look back up the page and the next line, and when the noon bell had long gone and the light had begun to leave the windows he was still at it, the lamp lit at his shoulder. Wren had her own afternoon entered by five, and sat with her ledger shut and listened to his nib across the two desks, slower than anyone's on the row, and not once stopping to scratch a word out.

At six Mr. Hesketh came up the row with the envelopes. He carried them stacked along his left forearm, in desk order, and he began at the end desk, where Fenn was locking the stores book, and came down the row laying one buff envelope on each blotter, the desk's number on the front, without a word and without looking at anyone's face. Chairs stopped scraping as he came. Ruddock had seen him from the end of the row; he laid his pen down across the ledger and put his hands in his lap. Pell's envelope went down on twelve, and Pell weighed it in his palm and set it down unopened. Hesketh laid one on Ruddock's desk, and one on nineteen, square to the edge of the blotter, and went on down to the end and out by the stair.

Ruddock opened his first. Wren heard the coin slide, and saw him draw out the slip and look into the envelope after it. There was no letter. Nobody on the row said anything about it. Across from her he let out a breath, a long one, through his nose, and pocketed the coin. Up the row Pell had his coat on already and the envelope in his coat pocket, unopened, and he was looking at nothing.

Wren's envelope had Nineteen on the front in Hesketh's hand, and the flap had been stuck down like every month's. She slit it with her thumb. The coin was inside, and the slip of hours, countersigned. With them, folded twice, was a plain white sheet, not the Office's buff.

She opened it out and read along the Registrar's hand to the thing a Deputy had always held on Settling morning, on the foot of the leaf.

Desk nineteen. W. Aske. Settling Day: the inkstand. L. Vane.
