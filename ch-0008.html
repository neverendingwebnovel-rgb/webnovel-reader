The Tallied City · Chapter 8

Drawn as Borne

The referral index and the river stairs went into the box together, two chits for the twelfth's two hours, and she did not hear either of them land. It was still dark. Three were left in the tin under her bed.

The stove caught late, and the floor filled round it in coats. Her own entries came first, because they were her post and the inquiry was not, and both of them closed. She blotted them and drew a slip out of the drawer.

One hour, against three held. Purpose: Inquiry of Imbalance, warrant attached.

The slip did not say when she meant to take it. The warrant asked for hours at whatever hour of the day or night, and after six there would be nobody on the stair to watch where she went.

Pell came back up the row from the urn with his cup held level in both hands, and slowed at her shoulder the way he slowed at everyone's. He read the slip without seeming to look at it.

"Three," he said. "Between ourselves, Miss Aske, I'd keep the last one."

He went on to desk twelve and sat down and blew on his tea.

She waited for the rest of it. The other times he had read her hours over her shoulder, the next thing out of him had been a price, or the offer standing. Nothing came. He turned a page of his ledger and dipped his pen, and she found she had been holding the slip a finger's width off the desk, as if he might still lean over and want it.

The runner took it round with the warrant pinned behind, and when it came back initialled she unpinned the warrant to fold it away. The back fell open at the last row: R., hedge-worker, of the ninth, and under it his short level stroke with the shorter one standing beneath its middle. Then it isn't anywhere. It was somewhere now, in his word and under his mark, and Dunmore could read it there whenever she liked.

The afternoon went on in losses that closed.

* * *

At six Wren let the room go down ahead of her, and followed it a few minutes behind, meaning at the turn of the stair to go on down.

The street door stood open on the dusk. Through it she could see the front steps, and on the top one, under the clock, a woman standing with her arms folded and her hands tucked in against her ribs. There was flour still worked into her knuckles. She had been waiting a while; she had the look of someone who had decided how long she would wait and had not reached it yet.

Wren went out to her.

"Mrs. Varley."

"Bench let me go," Nell said. She looked Wren up and down once, as if checking her against something. "Is it you that names him?"

"It's my line."

"Not her upstairs?" She tipped her head at the Office behind them, at the paper she had read at arm's length on her own step.

"The hand that opens a line closes it," Wren said. "Nobody finishes one they didn't see begun."

Nell thought about that. "So it's you."

"Yes."

"What's it pay?"

Wren had read the line for a journeyman baker in Pell's copy of the Schedule. She had read it more than once. She had never done the sum, because there had never been a name to do it for. She did it now, on the steps, with the lamplighters starting along the street below: so much for a year at thirty-one, so much again for each year after, forty of them. She set it beside what Pell had paid the end clerk for an hour last year, before the rate began to climb, and beside what it was now.

"Less than forty of my hours would fetch," she said. "On the floor, across a desk."

"Forty hours." Nell looked up at the clock, then down again. "The lane put three doors in for one."

"I know."

"So that's him. Forty afternoons off for somebody at a desk." She said it flatly, the way she might have read a weight off a scale. "I'll take it."

"Whoever's named?"

"Whoever. I've a man sitting on a step from first light till they light the lamps. I'll take it off anybody's name you like."

The street lamps were coming on one at a time down the hill. Wren watched two of them catch before she answered, and when she did it was the first time she had said it aloud to anyone, herself included.

"Not on what I've got."

Nell looked at her a long moment. "Then get something else."

She unfolded her arms as if to go, and didn't. She stood with her floury hands hanging and looked at the step between them.

"I'll take his coin," she said. "I'll not take it on a lie about my own house. Nobody came to our door that night. I sleep light. I'd have heard the latch." Her chin came up. "Whatever the lane says."

Wren's hour had not begun, and none of this could go on the back of the warrant. It would stay a thing said to her on the steps at six, and Nell had come up the hill and said it anyway, knowing it might lose her the coin. A caster needn't come to anybody's door. The cost fell where it fell, within its radius, on whoever lay there asleep, so Nell's latch cleared no one. But it did tell Wren one thing: the door Kit had watched a man go in at, up by the bakehouse end, was not the Varleys'.

"So whose was it?" Nell said.

Wren did not know, and her face must have said so, because Nell did not wait to hear it. She went down the steps and along the front of the Office and round the corner of it, toward the back of the hill and the lane, with the near-dark closing behind her.

The clock over the steps said a quarter past six. Wren went back in.

* * *

Her hour began on the turn of the stair, where the street door's light gave out. She stopped there and lit the candle-end she had brought from her room. The stub was thick, and she knew it from evenings of reading by its fellows: a little more than an hour in it, if she did not stand in a draught. The extra flight took her down into its own cold. At the bottom the flame laid a small bright fleck on the brass of the plate and showed her nothing else she had not seen the night before.

She took the disc out and fitted it to the slot, notched edge first.

It went in as far as the bites and stopped, the plain round of it still showing between her finger and thumb. For a moment nothing. Then, somewhere inside the thickness of the wood, something heavy moved, a long slow lift like a bar coming up out of its brackets, and settled with a knock she felt through the soles of her shoes.

She drew the disc back out. It came easily. She put the flat of her hand to the door, and it gave inward.

On its inner face, where the outside had nothing, was a plain iron ring for a handle, black and worn smooth. Just inside, on a hook in the wall, hung a lamp. She lifted it and shook it gently and heard oil in it, not much. Beside the hook ran a ledge of stone at the height of her hip, and on the ledge lay a bone folder and a hank of waxed thread.

She knew a bone folder. The end clerk creased the returns with one, sliding it down each fold with his tongue between his teeth. The thread she did not know what to do with. Heavy stuff, waxed against fraying, wound in a figure of eight round two fingers and tied off, the kind a binder used to sew sections to a spine. She could think of no reason a clerk would need it down here, and she did not try to make one up. She lit the lamp from the candle and hung it back on its hook, and it threw its light up onto a high desk standing a pace inside the door, and onto the book open on the desk.

It was a tall book, taller than a ledger, its pages ruled down in five columns. Across the head of the page, in the spurred type of her warrant, it said:

DEEP ARCHIVE · ADMISSIONS

And under that, smaller:

Every admission to be entered in the bearer's own hand, with the bearer's tally drawn as borne.

Date · Name · Tally · In · Out

She looked at the disc in her palm. Tally. It had a word, then, and the word was the Office's own, the name of the account she kept upstairs, made small enough to carry.

* * *

The ink in the well on the desk had a skin on it. The pen lay in a groove beside the well.

There was no need to turn far. The last written lines were halfway down the open page, and above them, line after line, in the hand she had read upside down across two desks for four years, was H. Teague.

She turned back one leaf, and another, and kept on. H. Teague. H. Teague. Most evenings, with gaps where she supposed he had been tired, or ill, or at home with his peppermints. His In was always a little past six, ten past, a quarter past. His Out came an hour after, or two. And every time, in the Tally column, he had drawn in ink a row of small square bites along a curved line, careful, never hurried, the same bites in the same order.

She held the disc against the drawing, its edge laid along his line.

The same bites. Every one.

She paged back to the start of his run of lines, about a year of evenings, to where the page above them belonged to some other hand and some other time, and there she stopped and did not turn further. In a year of pages there was no other name than his. Dunmore's signature, which she had looked at on the front of her warrant more often than she liked, was nowhere on them.

She came forward again to his last line.

The twenty-eighth. H. Teague. The bites. In, ten past six. Out, half past two.

Half past two in the morning, then, or there was no sense in it. And the twenty-eighth was a week before the fifth: the same week, she thought, that the stubs in his ledger stopped, where the page after the gap had been from last week.

She did not let herself do anything with that. She took the pen out of its groove and broke the skin on the ink.

Whatever she found below was going on the back of the warrant anyway, in her hand, under her slip.

The thirteenth. W. Aske. In the Tally column she drew the bites, square by square, laying the disc beside the page and copying it as she went, so that his bites stood in her hand under his own. In, a quarter past six.

She left Out empty, laid the pen back in its groove, and took up the candle.

* * *

Behind the desk the room ran back in aisles, shelves to the ceiling on either side, and on the shelves the Office's own books and bundles tied in tape: thin books, broad books, books in boards gone soft at the corners, none of them the fat calfskin of a loss ledger. Those stood upstairs on the Loss Room wall, where they had always stood. These were something else.

The dust lay on everything, fine and grey and thick as felt, on the shelves, on the tapes, on the tops of the books. It lay on the floor too, except in one place. Down the middle aisle a track had been worn through it to the stone, one person wide, and the track led away from the desk between the shelves and stopped at the head of a stair going down.

She followed it. The stair turned once, and at the foot of it was a second door, with the same brass plate set into it and the same slot.

The disc went in notched edge first, and the bar lifted in the wood the same way, and the door gave to her hand. She found the iron ring on its inner face before she let it swing to behind her.

This level was older. She could tell it before she lifted the candle to a shelf: the air was drier, and the tapes on the bundles had gone the colour of tea. When she held the light to the spines, their stamps had no bindery's mark on them at all, like the oldest ledgers at the far end of the Loss Room wall, the ones nobody took down.

The track ran on through them, through the dust, to another stair. She walked to the head of it and held the candle out. It went down and turned, and at the turn, where her light only just reached, was another door, and on it another plate.

Each one older than the one above, and each with its slot. She stood and looked at that a while.

The candle-end had burned to the width of her finger. Her hour was all but spent, and whatever she saw after it she would see in nobody's hour, on nobody's slip, where it could no more go on the sheet than Nell's latch could. The track went on down past the turn without her.

She went back the way she had come, through the second door by its ring and up the stair, along the track between the shelves to the desk and the lamp.

* * *

At the desk where Teague had stood, she wrote her Out: ten past seven.

Then she took the warrant from her coat and opened it flat on the slope of the desk beside the book, back uppermost, and under row seven, in the lamplight, she wrote row eight.

The thirteenth · one hour · initialled · seen, below the main stair, through the door at the foot of the further flight: the Deep Archive, entered on the tally of H. Teague, from his effects. In its admissions book, his name most evenings for about a year, the last the twenty-eighth, out after midnight. W. Aske entered under it, the tally drawn. Two levels, a door to each; a track in the dust going down.

She read it through once. It said what she had seen and nothing else.

She knew whose desk the front of this sheet went across, and who turned it over to read the back. Dunmore had asked her once whether a thing was on here. This was.

She waited for the ink to take, folded the warrant along its creases, and put it inside her coat. Tonight's chit would go through the slot in the morning and leave two in the tin.

* * *

The ring turned in her hand and the bar lifted, and the door let her out onto the flags. When she let go of it, it swung shut on its own weight, and she heard the bar drop back into its brackets behind the wood. She tried the door with the flat of her hand. It did not move.

She climbed the extra flight with the candle-end guttering, round the turn and through the street door onto the front steps, where the lamps were all lit now down the length of the hill.

Under the first of them she looked at her arms. Her sleeves were grey from the cuffs to the elbows, the fine grey of the shelves, worked into the weave where she had reached past the books to hold her candle up to them. She beat at one sleeve and it only spread.

Grey to the elbows, Pell had said. Teague had come up like this, and whatever else he had been doing below, he had been signing his name for it.

She turned for home with her arms held a little out from her sides, which did no good at all.
