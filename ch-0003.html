The Tallied City · Chapter 3

The Matched Column

The chits lived in the tin under Wren's bed, and on the morning of the seventh, before the light was properly up, she knelt on the cold boards and took two of them out.

Each was a square of stiff card no bigger than her palm, stamped once in the corner. The slip she had carried yesterday said, in small print under the countersignature, that chits for hours spent were to be surrendered on the morning following. She had spent two hours on the sixth. So two chits went into her coat, nine went back into the tin, and she sat on her heels a moment looking at the lid, as if it might have an opinion about the arithmetic.

The chit box stood at the end of the row of desks in the Loss Room, on a stool of its own, padlocked, with a slot in the lid worn pale by thumbs. Wren posted the two chits through it one at a time. They made no sound when they landed. The box was fuller than she would have guessed.

At desk nineteen she set out her own ledger and the plain leaf headed unmatched — by district, and began copying her column across, street by street. The total went at the foot, where it belonged. Forty-one losses at the quarter's close, matched to nothing. Forty-two with the line she had added for Teague at the bottom. Twice in the night she had caught herself running that forty-one together with the other one, the forty-one years of service on Dunmore's form, as if the losses and the man were a single sum. They were not. The losses had been forty-one and were now forty-two. His years had been forty-one and would stay so. She wrote 42 and underlined it once.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske."

Osric Pell had come down the row with a cup in each hand, which meant he wanted to stay. He set one on the corner of her desk, well clear of the ink, and kept the other.

"She's paying for the plot," he said. "The Deputy Registrar. Out of her own pocket."

Wren put her pen down. "For Mr. Teague?"

"He had nobody. Forty-one years in this room and not a soul to stand at the edge of a hole for him." Pell blew across his tea. "Day after tomorrow. I had it from the porter, who had it from the undertaker's boy, who had it from her own mouth, so you may take it as gospel or as the porter, whichever you like."

"Why would she?"

"Why would anybody?" said Pell, and went back to desk twelve with his cup, which was no answer, and which he seemed to think was one.

* * *

Dunmore came up to the Loss Room a little before ten.

She never came up of her own accord. It had taken a dead man to bring her to the second floor on the fifth, and in four years Wren could not remember another time. Now she came down the row with a cardboard box under one arm, her other hand holding the keys still at her waist so they would not ring, and the room went quiet in the particular way of a room full of clerks pretending to write.

"His effects," Dunmore said. "For the burial."

Wren stood. Dunmore did not ask her to move. She put the box on the desk beside the ledger and opened the drawers herself, one after another, the way she might have gone through a return.

There was not much. The pen he had been holding when he died. A pen-wiper stiff with years of black. From the top drawer, the string and the pumice, the two spare nibs, the candle stub. The peppermint tin, which Dunmore picked up last.

She turned it over in her hand. Something small slid inside it.

"Forty-one years," she said, "and he never offered me one either."

She put it in the box.

Wren waited for the rest: for the question again, for did he say anything. None of it came. Dunmore's hand went along the empty top drawer, front to back, and came out again with nothing.

Teague's razor was folded in the pocket of Wren's coat, over the back of her chair, where it had been since she ruled her sheet with it. Wren looked at the box and not at the coat, and said nothing. That made two things she had kept from the Deputy Registrar: what Teague had really said, and now this.

Dunmore lifted the box. "Thank you, Miss Aske."

Wren went ahead of her to hold the door. On the landing Dunmore stopped at the urn, set the box down on the rail, took a cup from the hook and poured. She tasted it. Her face did nothing at all. Then she tipped the cup out into the slop bowl, rinsed it under the last of the stream, and hung it back.

"He was right," she said. "It has gone off."

She took up the box and went down the stairs, the keys quiet under her hand, and Wren stood on the landing with the door against her shoulder and could not have said, then or afterwards, whether she had just watched a woman grieve for a man who had sat at the same desk for forty-one years, or been told, in the plainest way Dunmore had, that the tea had never been all Teague said.

* * *

At noon her second slip came back countersigned. One hour. Purpose: the inquiry. When its chit went through the slot tomorrow morning there would be eight left in the tin.

She went out by the front steps under the clock and down the street into Vellmark.

In the middle of the day the town looked like a place that was owed a holiday and meant to have it. The handbills had gone up overnight, a full quarter early, as they did every year, pasted over the old notices on every blank wall, black letters on yellow: SQUARE YOUR SLATE BY SETTLING DAY. Some of them had a picture of a slate with a cloth wiping it, done by somebody who had never drawn a cloth before. In a chandler's doorway a woman with a basket stood while the chandler took the slate down from its nail, found her name on it, and drew his thumb through two lines of chalk. She thanked him as if he'd let her off something, and he said it was early yet, and they both laughed. Further down, in the yard of a guild hall, two apprentices had the hall's bunting out on trestles, sewing up a pennant somebody had put a boot through.

Wren knew the day itself well enough. Every Settling Day she could remember had been the same: the shops put their shutters up at noon, people went round to their neighbours with what they owed in a twist of paper or a pie or a promise, and by dark the taverns were full of people who owed nothing and were celebrating it on credit. It was a festival for clean slates. Nobody she had ever met thought it was anything else, and nothing in the handbills, the chalk or the bunting suggested otherwise.

She skirted the foot of the hill to the river side, where the smell of mud and coal smoke came up to meet her, and climbed Mill Steps from the bottom again.

The Varleys' chimney was cold. Every other chimney on the lane had smoke on it, thin in the noon air. The wheatsheaf on the door was where it had been. The knots were not. There were more of them, on more doors: the doorframe to the left of the Varleys' had five bundles now where yesterday it had three, the one to the right had gone past counting, and three doors further down, which had had none yesterday, had them today, fresh twine, the pins still bright.

Wren stood on the step below the Varleys' door with her hand at her side. There was nothing to tell them. Nothing had changed except the date and the number of knots, and Nell Varley would ask her what they had bought, and she would say she was looking into it.

She did not knock.

* * *

She walked back down to the foot of Mill Steps, where the cobbles gave out onto stone stairs that dropped to the Vell, worn into dips in the middle and green at the bottom where the water reached. A young man sat halfway down them with a tray across his knees, as if the tray were the reason the stairs had been built.

He was all length, knees and elbows and a neck too long for his collar, and he was up before she had reached him.

"You want a knot," he said. "Everybody on that hill wants a knot this week. Penny a knot, any you like."

The tray held two kinds. Most were plain, twine knotted round a bent pin, the same as on the doorframes, in rows. Along one edge lay a handful of others done in red thread, wound tighter, each with a little brass pin that caught the light.

"And those?"

"Those are the proper ones." He touched one. "Four times a penny, each. Red's lucky, and the brass is brass, which counts for something."

"What do they do?"

He looked at her with real pleasure, like a man who had waited all morning for the right question. "Make you feel better," he said. "The penny ones make you feel a penny's worth better. The red ones, a good deal more, if you're the kind of person who likes red." He shrugged. "I don't tell anybody they push anything anywhere. People tell themselves that. I only sell the twine."

"And people buy them."

"Never sold so many. More every season since I started, and I started young." He tapped the tray. "Settling Day's my best trade, mind. Everyone wants to start the year owing nothing, and if they can't manage that, they'll take a knot that says they tried." He considered her coat, her cuffs, the ink on her second finger. "You're from up top."

"Yes."

"Thought so. Jory Dace," he said, and did not offer a hand, because it was full of tray.

Wren glanced back up the lane, at the knots she could see from here, and at the cold chimney she couldn't. "Who casts round here?"

"Nobody casts on Mill Steps." He said it kindly, as if she were a long way from home. "They can't afford what it lands on. You cast for a better loaf, and next week it's your neighbour's eyes, and you've got to live next door to her." Then he leaned in a little, tray and all. "If you wanted it done proper, though, and not landing on anybody? There's Rook. Does it himself and pays it himself."

Wren heard herself answer before she had decided to.

"The caster never pays," she said. "Someone else always does. That's the first law of the Tally." It was the reason there was an Office at all, and three hundred years of ledgers along the Loss Room wall. "That's how it has always worked."

Jory Dace shrugged, a long shrug, all the way down his arms. "Tell Rook."

"Where would I find him?"

"About the river stairs." He nodded at them, and at the water, and at nothing in particular. "When he's about."

She bought a penny knot without quite knowing why. She paid, and he picked her a plain one off the end of the row, and she put it in her coat pocket, where it lay against the folded razor.

The climb back up Mill Steps took the rest of the hour. She went past the Varleys' door without looking at it, round the side of the Office to the front steps, and came in under the clock as the hand went over.

* * *

She carried her sheet down the row to desk twelve. "What can they claim?" she asked Pell.

He had been sanding a line, and he stopped with the shaker in his hand. Nobody had asked Pell anything in his own field, Wren suspected, in some years, and his face showed it.

"The Varleys," she said. "The baker on Mill Steps."

"Ah." Pell put down the shaker, opened his drawer, and took out a booklet so thin it bent over his fingers. Its paper cover had been grey once. The title was printed in the old heavy type the Office used for things it did not expect to change: Schedule of Restitution.

"You'll know it," he said.

"I know it exists."

"Nobody reads it. I've read it." He did not open it. He didn't need to; he laid it on her sheet and spoke with his eyes on the windows. "Once a loss is matched to a caster, the caster is made to pay the payer, and this is what he pays. A year of life, by trade and by age. A sense, by which sense. A day of memory, flat. It's all here, very tidy. Some clerk worked it out with great care, a long time ago." He turned the booklet over with one finger to show the back cover, where the last revision was printed in a type even older than the title. "And nobody has touched it since. Generations. So a year of a journeyman baker's life, in the prime of it, is set at less than I offered you yesterday for one of your quiet hours."

Wren looked at the booklet.

"A year," she said.

"Less. Not much less. But less." Pell seemed sorry about it, in the way he might have been sorry about the weather. "And that's for a matched loss. That's for a loss with somebody on the other end of it."

"And an unattributed one?"

"Has nobody to charge." He spread his hands. "Nobody to charge, nothing to pay. The Schedule doesn't run backwards, Miss Aske. If there's no caster, there's no restitution, and if there's no restitution, the Varleys get what everybody gets who's in your column." He put a finger on the 42 at the foot of her sheet, gently, as if it might be hot. "They get written down."

Unless somebody named a caster. Wren did not say it. It sat on the desk between them with the booklet.

She looked at him instead: the cup at his elbow, the drawer full of other people's business. "Do you spend yours?" she said. "Your own hours."

"Never spent one." He said it at once, with a kind of comfortable pride. "Never sold one, either. Thirty years." He picked up the Schedule and slid it back into his drawer, under something. "Between ourselves, I like to know I could leave."

He went back to his sanding. Wren waited for the rest of it, and there was no rest of it.

* * *

The floor emptied at six. By half past there was nobody on the second floor but Wren and the dust on the windows, and the light coming through them had gone thin and brown, like the river.

The referral index stood at the far end of the ledger wall, past the last of the calfskin spines: a squat cabinet of narrow drawers, each with a brass card-frame and a letter in it. Wren had filed cards into it for four years. Every case the Loss Room sent down to the wardens went in under the caster's name. She had never once pulled a drawer to read what someone else had put there.

She pulled R.

The drawer ran out stiffly, nearly empty. The guide cards stood in their order with nothing behind most of them; behind the last was a single card, gone soft at the corners, dated eleven years back. The hand was a clerk's hand, upright and careful, and it was not one she knew.

Spell: a broken arm set whole, river stairs below Mill Steps, witnessed by a warden. Caster: the hedge-worker called Rook, taken in the act. Payer sought: none found in the Tally within radius or time. Caster states the cost was his own. Not credited. Closed: no loss on the Tally to answer for.

Something moved at the edge of the shelves. Tuppence came out from behind the ledgers, went up the side of the cabinet in two easy movements, and settled on top with her paws folded under, looking down into the open drawer as though she too had been meaning to check.

Wren read the card again.

The wardens had taken him with the arm still in his hands, by the sound of it. They had brought the case up here, and some clerk at some desk had gone through the Tally for the payer, a broken bone's worth of somebody's year or eyes or memory, in the streets round the river stairs and in the days round the casting. And found nobody. Nobody had paid. So there was nothing to charge him with. The Office could not hold a man for a cost that did not appear in its own books, and so had let him go, and written down what he said, and written underneath that it did not believe him.

She stood with the card in her fingers and saw the shape of it, turned the other way round, like a reflection in the black window. Her column was full of losses with no spell. Here was a spell with no loss.

Tuppence yawned, showing all her teeth, and did not move.

* * *

Wren put the card back where it had been, closed the drawer, and went to desk nineteen. Because the room had gone cold with the stoves banked, she put her coat on, and then sat down in it, which she knew was a foolish way to sit at a desk.

The one column she had ruled down the district sheet with the back of the razor was for the match, and it was empty from top to foot. All quarter she had not written a name in a matched column. Not one.

Three lines on the sheet were Mill Steps. The widow two doors up, her sight, in the quarter just signed. The lamplighter's winter, carried over from the quarter before and set below the total. The baker, from the new one, pencilled under that.

She dipped her pen and wrote, against all three, in the empty column: Rook (?)

Then she looked at it, and made herself put it together properly, as if it were somebody else's return and she was checking it.

A man who swore he paid for his own spells. Who had worked the stairs at the foot of Mill Steps for eleven years at least, and was still, by Jory Dace's account, about. Who had set a broken arm under a warden's eye, and the Tally had searched for the cost of it, within the radius and within the time, and found nothing. Perhaps it had searched in the wrong place. Perhaps it had searched too soon. Perhaps a man could believe, honestly, that he paid his own costs, because they never seemed to touch him, and they never touched him because they went somewhere else: up the steps behind him, a door or two at a time, one a quarter, into a lane where nobody cast because nobody could afford what it landed on. Into her column, where they were entered as matched to nothing, because nobody had thought to look at the stairs below.

It held together. That was the trouble. It held together, and she wanted it to.

If it was Rook, the baker's lost years had a caster on the other end of them. Under the Schedule a caster could be made to pay, even at a rate set before anybody's grandfather was born, and the Varleys would be owed something, not nothing. Nell Varley could be told what they had bought. And Wren would have a line that closed, by Settling Day, in her own hand.

Four years in the Loss Room had taught her what it looked like when a clerk wanted a column to come out a particular way. It looked like this. A name with a question mark after it, because the question mark let you write the name.

She did not cross it out.

Outside the back windows the lamps were coming on down Mill Steps, one and then the next, in the lane below the Office. Wren watched them until she could not tell which one hung nearest the Varleys' door. Her hand had gone into her coat pocket without her sending it. It found the penny knot, the twine rough under her thumb, the bent pin, and beside it the razor, folded shut.
