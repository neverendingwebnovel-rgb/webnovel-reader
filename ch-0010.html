The Tallied City · Chapter 10

None Found

The book lay open on desk twelve, under Pell's hand: a small black pocketbook with a soft cover, ruled by hand down the middle of every page. On the left of the rule was a quarter-day. On the right was a figure, in pencil. There were thirty years of them.

Wren had come down the row to ask him a thing she had never asked anyone, whether he would ever sell an hour, since she had one left and a great deal of quarter still to spend it in. Pell had said no gently, as he might have refused to lend his good pen, and then, instead of turning back to his ledger, he had taken this out of his inside pocket and opened it on the blotter between them.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske, I don't sell them. I write them down."

The early pages had gone soft as old cloth, and the hand on them was the hand on his ledger now, only smaller. For year after year the figure barely moved. A silver and a penny. A silver and two. A silver and a penny again, one quarter-day after another, so steady that the pencil seemed to have got bored of it. Then, twelve quarters back, it began to go up, and it went up every quarter after that without once standing still. Last year there was a silver and nine, which was what he had paid the clerk on the end desk. She had always taken it that he bought that hour while the price still sat where it had sat for years; by his own pencil it had been rising two years when he did. At the foot of the last page, against this quarter-day, he had written two silvers.

Her rent was a silver a week. She had not known, until she saw it in his pencil, how far the figure had come to get where it was.

"It's for the inquiry," she said. "If I could show the—"

Pell lifted one hand off the page, not far.

"I only write them down, Miss Aske."

He did not want to know what she would make of it. She saw that plainly, and saw too that it was not unkindness. He had kept the figure thirty years for the comfort of knowing it, the way he kept hours he never spent, and a comfort would not stand up to being asked a question. He closed the book, put it into her hand and went back to his work as if he had lent her a pencil.

She walked it up the row to desk nineteen and set it open on her blotter.

She did not need to fetch her own sheets. The unattributed figure on every return that bore her signature was in her head. Her first year had run to about a dozen a quarter, as the dead clerks' sheets had. Then, from the same quarter in which Pell's pencil had begun to climb, her column had begun too, and it had gone up every quarter since: to thirty-four, to thirty-eight, to forty-one. She put a finger on his page and ran it down beside the figures she knew by heart. Quarter for quarter, from the same quarter, the two rose together.

The reason offered itself at once, and she nearly let it stand. More losses meant more burials and more sickbeds, and more mourners wanting an hour off a bench to sit with somebody, and the more people wanted a thing, the higher its price went. Then she thought of Mill Steps, where three households had put in together for one hour. It was lanes like that one the losses were landing on. A lane that lost a baker's forty years did not come to market with more money for hours; it came with less, or it did not come. A price did not climb on buyers who were getting poorer. So the mourners were not paying two silvers. Then who was? She turned it over and back and it would not come out. There was nobody in it she could see with two silvers to put down, quarter after quarter, and still the figure in Pell's pencil went up.

Twelve quarters was three years. Three years was what the stubs in Teague's ledger covered, under the heading in his hand, unmatched — by distr—. He had been counting this. She was nearly sure of it, and nearly sure was not a row. She shut Pell's book and laid it by itself at the corner of the blotter, and pulled a slip toward her.

One hour, against one held. Purpose: Inquiry of Imbalance, warrant attached.

This was the last chit in the tin. She knew what she meant to buy with it. The Loss Room's own ledgers, read from the ninth: somewhere in them, within radius and time of the river stairs at dusk that day, would be whoever had paid for the boatman's boy's palm. A payer for the boy would prove that a hedge-worker's costs came down on the lane, as she had thought they did, and that would finish what row nine needed to say about the night of the fifth. She still believed it as she wrote the slip.

She took the book back down the row with the slip lying on top of the warrant. Pell's eyes went to it as she set the book at his elbow, to against one held, and stayed there no longer than it took to read. She watched him come to the end of a thought and decide not to say it. He put the book away in his inside pocket and dipped his pen.

* * *

She took her hour at three of the clock, at the ledger wall, in the working day, where every clerk on the row could see what she was spending it on.

The quarter's book came down off the wall heavier than it looked. She brought it to her desk and then went along the row for the clerks' ledgers, which came across the desks to her in turn without a word said, though not without looking. The ninth came first. She ran a finger down the day's column, district by district, stopping at every line that gave the river stairs, the foot of Mill Steps or the waterfront under the hill. She passed over the districts across the Vell without reading them: nothing that landed over the water had come from a boy's hand on this bank. Then the tenth, and the eleventh, and on through the week, to the lines that had been entered that morning while she sat with Pell's book, the ink on two of them still sharp.

There was nothing. Inside the radius, nobody had lost so much as a day of memory, not on the ninth or on any day after it.

Payer sought: none found in the Tally within radius or time. The card's words came up in the card's order, as she had copied them out for him, and what she had in front of her was the same nothing a clerk had had in front of him eleven years ago, the one who had looked as she had and then written Not credited under it.

Back on the seventh she had written Rook (?) against three lines of her district sheet, sure enough of her reasoning to put a name to it: that whatever he paid came down unmatched on the lane above him and reached her column that way. But a loss that matched nothing was still a loss, and still entered. If his costs fell anywhere, they would be here, in these books, under these districts, and they were not.

A man whose castings left no payer had no reason to have left one on the fifth. A child's arm need not have cost a baker anything. One row was not a rule, and she knew it. But the card made it twice, and the second of the two she had watched with her own eyes. So the baker's forty years were back where she had first found them, with nobody's name against them, and her mind had already gone past Rook to somebody else, somebody who had cast on the hill that night and left no door the lane had seen.

She turned the warrant over and wrote row nine.

The fifteenth · one hour · initialled · read, the Loss Room's ledgers, the ninth to the morning of the fifteenth: no loss entered within radius or time of the river stairs at dusk on the ninth (row seven).

The topmost door was not on it. Yesterday she had kept it off for what writing it down would do. Today there was no reason to put it down at all.

She carried the clerks' ledgers back along the row, desk by desk, and put the quarter's book up in its place on the wall. Tuppence, who had been asleep on the shelf beside it, got up and went in behind the ledgers. The clock had gone four. The hour was spent, and there was no other to come after it.

* * *

After six she went down to the river stairs anyway. No slip lay behind her, and nothing she heard below could be entered on the sheet. Going at all, with the warrant in her coat and no hour declared, was outside its terms, and she knew that as well as she knew the number on her desk. She went because she meant to. Not to take anything, she told herself. To know.

She went down the length of Mill Steps without stopping. At its foot the lane's last lamp hung on its bracket above the mouth of the river stairs, and Rook was sitting on the top step under it with a paper open on his knee.

He was reading it slowly, as a man reads his own name in a list. She knew the leaf. It was her copy, in her writing, folded to the size of a chit and opened so many times since the twelfth that the creases had gone soft. He never heard her stop. When he looked up, his gaze went to her coat and rested there.

"Clerk," he said, and that was all he had.

He turned the leaf over. On its back, in pencil, in large uneven capitals that went uphill and leaned back, a left hand's, someone had written:

ASKE. CLERK. UP TOP. SHE WROTE IT OUT.

Nothing else was on it. He took it in, and looked at her again.

"Aske."

On the ninth he had asked her name twice inside a few minutes. This was what the paper was for, then. Or it was part of what the paper was for, since she still could not say why it had to be this paper, the Office's card in her writing, and not any scrap with a name on it. Her name was on the back of it now too, in his pencil. She put that away to think about later.

"You were at the top door," he said. It was not a question. "Leave them off it."

"I haven't put them on it."

He folded the leaf once and held it on his knee. "I'll give you the fifth. Said, and you write it down, and read it back, and I'll mark it, same as the boy. I went up the lane that night. There was a little one burned, and I closed it, and the cost was mine. You can have all that under my mark." He looked at her. "Write me. Not them."

"Whose door?"

"That's theirs."

A statement that put a caster on Mill Steps on the night of the fifth, in his own words and under his own mark, was everything a referral wanted, a loss and a caster with a name and someone to say so, the very thing Dunmore had sent her to find; and the Office had never once credited mine. He was offering to go into the drawer for the baker's forty years, to keep a child out of it.

"I can't take it," she said. "I've no hour. Nothing you tell me tonight can be read back and go on the sheet."

He waited.

"And I don't need it," she said. "I had the ledgers this afternoon, from the ninth on. Nobody paid for the boy. Not on the stairs, not on the lane, not anywhere inside the radius."

He did not look surprised. If anything he looked as if she had told him the tide came in.

"No," he said. "There wouldn't be."

"No."

"Then what are you here for?"

"To know."

He considered that, and her, and did not say anything to it. She had come down with one question and she asked it.

"What took forty years off Tobin Varley?"

"Not me." He unfolded the leaf, looked at the back of it again, and folded it along her creases. "I know what I paid."

That was all there was. He put the leaf inside his coat, and as he settled the coat something under the right side of it knocked once against the stone of the step, hard. Not a hand. Metal. A tool, she thought, or a weight, or a flask. The question was at her teeth, and she let it go. Her asking was spent on Tobin Varley, and it had bought what it had bought.

She left him there on the top step under the lamp, his coat buttoned over the paper, and went up.

* * *

She passed the Varleys' door without slowing. The knots were still on its frame, and behind it there was no sound at all.

B. Hollin was at the topmost door, taking her washing in off the line under the eave, in the dark, by feel, with the basket on her hip and a sheet half over her arm. She saw who it was before Wren had reached the step, and did not turn away.

"You were at my door," she said, low.

"I've no hour tonight," Wren said. "Nothing said now goes in any book."

The woman looked down the lane before she answered, at the bakehouse half-doors with the light coming out of them, and kept her voice under the noise of the ovens. Whatever her shut door had been for yesterday, it had been for them and not for Wren. The Office's book was the lane's ear.

"I sent for him," she said. "She'd pulled the kettle over. Posy."

She unpegged the next sheet and doubled it against herself.

"He came up. He sat at my table after, till near midnight. And by the end of it he didn't know my name." She laid the sheet in the basket. "Twenty years he's known it."

Behind her, at the window, the child's head showed above the edge of the cloth, and dropped out of sight again. Neither of them spoke to her.

"You'll not put my door in your book," the woman said.

"It isn't in it."

The woman's eyes went once more down the lane, to the Varleys' door. She said nothing more about it, or about what That's ours had meant, but pulled the last of the pegs off the line, settled the basket and went in with it, and the light from the window lay across the step where she had stood.

* * *

Kit Morrow was at the head of the lane on his second round, with his pole up and the hook in the lamp, trimming a wick that had been smoking. He brought the pole down when he saw her.

"You've been down the stairs, miss." His voice cracked on stairs and he went on past it. "I saw you go. Was he there? Are you having him, then?"

"No," Wren said.

She said it as her own word and not the Office's, because it was hers to take back. It was her paper Kit had put his mark to, and from her paper the lane had got its name for the man on the fifth.

Kit stood with the pole grounded and his mouth a little open.

"Then whose?"

"I don't know."

He peered down the lane past her, past the lodging's window, to the three bakehouse doors under it, where the ovens were going for the night and the half-doors stood open on the heat. She watched him go from one to the next.

"One of them, then," he said. "Under the top. Has to be one of them."

She did not tell him he was right, and she could not tell him he was wrong. Nell Varley would have taken the Schedule's coin off any name the lane gave her, and Wren had just taken away the only name it had.

Kit shouldered the pole.

"The lane'll want somebody, miss," he said.

He went down with the pole over his shoulder and the hook swinging. At the first half-door below the lodging he stopped, where a man in his shirt was bent to the oven with the peel, and leaned in over the door to say something, and the man straightened up to hear it.
