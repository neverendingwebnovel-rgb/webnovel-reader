The Tallied City · Chapter 1

Remainder of Years

The cooper had lost his wedding day.

Wren Aske read the report twice, the way she read all of them: once for what it said and once for what it didn't. The warden's hand was blocky and impatient. Complainant, a cooper of the lower town, states that on waking he could recall nothing of his own wedding. Wife confirms the day occurred. Complainant recalls the courtship, the banns, the morning after. Not the day. Distressed. At the bottom, pressed into a thumb of brown wax, was the mark of the coopers' guild, a hoop crossed with an adze, so that nobody could later say the man had not been vouched for.

She set it on the left of her blotter and pulled the night's seizure lists toward her.

The Loss Room ran the length of the Office's second floor, forty desks in two facing rows under windows nobody had washed since before Wren was hired. Dust lay in the grooves of every desk like grain in a field. Along the inner wall, floor to ceiling, stood the ledgers: three hundred years of them in calfskin gone the colour of weak tea, each spine stamped with a year and a guild mark for whichever bindery had won the contract that decade. The oldest ones at the far end had no marks at all. Clerks walked past them the way people walk past graves of families they never knew.

The seizure lists were the wardens' work, scrawled overnight. Wren ran her finger down the column of the fourth night. A dice-weight charm, confiscated. A cure for warts that had cured nothing and so cost nothing. And on the docks, near the fish stairs, an unlicensed seller taken with a basket of lucky-catch charms: knotted cord and a scrap of scale, sold to men going out on the night tide. Three sold before the wardens came.

She checked the date against the cooper's report. The same night.

There it was. Some fisherman had wanted a full net and had paid a few coppers for the knot. The net had come up full, no doubt. And across the city a cooper had gone to bed with his wedding day and woken without it. Someone cast, someone else paid. That was the rule of the thing, older than the Office, older than the Prohibition that had made the Office necessary. Magic did not care who held the cord. It only cared that the account was settled.

Wren dipped her pen and wrote the line into the day's ledger in the small, upright hand she had been trained into.

Cooper, lower town — memory, one day (wedding). Matched to: lucky-catch charm, docks, night of the fourth. Caster unknown. Referred to wardens.

She blotted it and laid the cooper's report in the wardens' tray, where it would go downstairs with the afternoon runner and become somebody else's problem. Somewhere a fisherman had eaten well. Somewhere a wife was sitting across the table from a man who looked at her as if she had told him a story about someone else.

The tea came round at ten. It was bad, as it always was, stewed in an urn on the landing since the early bell, and Wren drank it anyway because it was hot and because it was part of the wage. The rest of the wage came at the end of the month in a buff envelope: some coin, and a slip of quiet hours, stamped and countersigned, each one good for an hour off the floor whenever a clerk chose to spend it. Wren had eleven saved in the tin under her bed. Clerks traded them like tobacco. She had never spent one. She wasn't sure what she would do with an hour that nobody was counting.

* * *

It was quarter's end, which meant the unattributed column.

Every clerk kept one. The quarterly sheet was a printed form headed The Office of the Tally, Vellmark: Quarterly Return of Losses, and the unattributed sat at its far right, past matched and referred and closed, a narrow column for losses that could be matched to no spell at all: no charm seized, no caster named, no ritual reported within a reasonable radius or time. Some losses always fell there. Memory went missing and nobody knew why. People were careless, or lied, or forgot that they had wanted to forget. The column was the Office's way of admitting that it could not catch everything. Wren had been told on her first day that a tidy clerk's unattributed column was a short one.

Hers was not short.

She totted it up with the sheet flat under her palm, then did it again from the bottom up. Forty-one losses this quarter that matched to nothing. Thirty-eight the quarter before. Thirty-four before that. She had gone down to the shelves in her first year, curious, and pulled the quarterly sheets of clerks long dead, and their unattributed columns had run to a dozen or so. Some quarters fewer. She had never seen one past twenty.

Across the desk, Halloran Teague was writing.

He had sat opposite her for the four years she had been at the Office, and at that same desk for thirty-seven years before she came. He wrote the way some old men walk, without hurry and without stopping. His cuffs were black with ink to the second button. He kept a clerk's razor in his top drawer for trimming quills, though no one used quills any more, and a tin of peppermints he never offered to anybody.

"Mr. Teague."

"Mm." He did not look up.

"My unattributed's up again."

"Mm."

"Three quarters running. It's near three times what the old sheets show." She turned the sheet round and slid it across the gap between their desks, over the little canyon of dust where the two desks met. "I've checked it twice. They're not careless reports. Most have guild marks. Most have witnesses."

Teague finished his line. He blotted it. Then he looked at her sheet without touching it, as if it might be hot.

"Some columns," he said, "aren't meant to close."

"That's not an answer."

"It wasn't a question, Aske. You told me a number." He went back to his ledger.

Wren didn't take the sheet back. She sat with her hands flat on the desk and waited, which was a thing she had learned from him, and after a while he set his pen down in the groove of the inkstand with a small, precise click.

"If you want to know who pays," Teague said, "look at who's first in the queue."

"What queue?"

He took a peppermint out of the tin, looked at it, and put it back.

"The tea's worse than usual," he said. "Somebody's been at the urn with the old leaves again. You'd think after forty-one years they'd have learned to buy new."

"Mr. Teague."

"Your sheet's due to the Deputy Registrar by morning," he said. "Best not be late with it. She notices."

And that was all she got.

* * *

He died a little after three.

Wren would think about it afterwards, how ordinary it was. The light had moved down the windows to the place it reached every afternoon, where it lit the dust on the ledger spines and made the Loss Room look briefly like a church. Somebody at the end of the row was coughing. A runner had come up with the second post and gone down again. Teague was writing. She heard his pen moving, the long scratch and the small tick at the end of each line, the sound she had heard every working day for four years.

Then she didn't hear it.

She looked up. He was sitting as he always sat, a little forward, his pen in his right hand and the nib resting on the page. His eyes were open. He was looking at what he had written. There was no expression on his face at all, not pain, not surprise. It was the face of a man who has finished reading something.

"Mr. Teague?"

She was round the desks before she had decided to move. She touched his shoulder, and it was like touching a coat on a hook. His hand stayed where it was. A bead of ink gathered at the nib and spread into the paper, a small black flower.

"Someone fetch—" she said, and didn't know whom. "Someone go down for the Deputy Registrar."

Chairs scraped. Somebody ran. Wren stood at his elbow and did not look at his face again, and so she looked at his ledger instead, because she was a clerk and that was where her eyes went.

The last line was in his own hand. The ink was still wet; it shone where the light caught it. It was written in the loss columns, properly ruled, the way he would have written any report.

The fifth. H. Teague — remainder of years.

The column headed matched to was blank.

* * *

Deputy Registrar Marguerite Dunmore came up the stairs at a pace that was not quite hurrying. She was a tall woman in grey with her keys on a chain at her waist and a habit of pressing her lips together before she spoke, as though she were checking each sentence for errors first. She looked at Teague. She looked at the clerks who had gathered in the aisle.

"Back to your desks, please," she said. "There we are. Thank you."

They went. Wren didn't.

Dunmore bent and closed Teague's eyes with two fingers, briskly, as if she were shutting a drawer. She took the pen out of his hand and laid it in the inkstand. Then she sent a runner for the porters and another for a form, and when the form came she filled it in standing up, using the corner of Wren's desk.

Wren had never seen one before. It was printed on the Office's heavy paper, with the Office's seal at the head: Report of Loss (Personal), Staff. Name. Years of service. Desk. Time. And a box, ruled more heavily than the others, marked Cause.

Dunmore wrote Halloran Teague. Forty-one. Loss Room, desk nineteen. Approx. three of the clock. She left the box empty. Then she took a stamp from her pocket, breathed on it and brought it down beside the box, and when she lifted it the word sat there in red capitals: UNATTRIBUTED.

"You can't," Wren said.

Dunmore looked at her as if noticing her for the first time. "I'm sorry?"

"You haven't looked. Nobody's looked. There's no physician, there's no warden. He was well this morning. He was complaining about the tea." She heard her voice going up and brought it down. "He wrote it himself. Look at the ledger. He wrote his own line."

Dunmore did look. She looked at the wet line for a long moment, and for that moment her lips were not pressed together.

Then they were.

"It happens in this building," she said. "Once in a long while." It was not said unkindly. It was said the way one tells a child that the river rises in spring. "The form goes into the Tally like any other loss. That's what the Tally is for, Miss Aske. We write it down."

"And then what?"

"And then we go on writing things down." She blotted the form, folded it once and put it in her inside pocket. "There we are."

The porters came, two of them, and carried Teague out between them in his chair, because it was easier than lifting him out of it. Nobody in the Loss Room stood. A few clerks lowered their eyes as he passed. By the time the door had swung shut behind the porters, the pens had started again, one and then another and then all of them, until the room sounded the way it always did, like rain on a roof.

* * *

Dunmore came back an hour before the end of shift. Wren was at her own desk, staring at her quarterly sheet without reading it, and did not see her until the Deputy Registrar's hand came down on the empty desk opposite.

"Desk nineteen is yours from tomorrow," Dunmore said. "Move your things tonight. His ledger's open; his quarter isn't closed. It'll need closing by morning along with yours."

Wren looked at the desk. Teague's pen was still in the inkstand where Dunmore had laid it. The peppermint tin sat by the blotter.

"Tonight," Wren said.

"The work doesn't stop because we do." Dunmore pressed her lips together, and then added, more quietly, "He kept a neat book. It shouldn't take you long."

* * *

It took her until the lamps.

By then the Loss Room was empty, the last clerks gone down the long stairwell with their collars up, and the only sound was the building settling and, somewhere behind the ledger shelves, the cat.

Tuppence came out when the room was quiet, as she always did: a grey, square-shouldered animal with one ear notched, who belonged to nobody and was fed by everybody. She walked the length of the aisle, inspected the place where Teague's chair had stood, and then hopped up onto desk nineteen and sat down squarely on the open ledger.

"That's not yours," Wren said.

Tuppence closed her eyes.

Wren worked round her. She took Teague's ledger page by page from the front of the current volume, and for a long while it was exactly what Dunmore had said, a neat book: forty-one years of practice in every ruled line, every match cross-referenced, every referral initialled. She found herself admiring it and then felt ashamed of admiring it, and kept turning.

She lifted the cat to turn a page, and the ledger fell open further than it should have.

She knew before she understood. The book lay too flat. She ran her thumb down the gutter and felt them: stubs. A row of narrow paper stubs standing up from the spine like the stumps of a cut hedge. Someone had razored out pages, close to the binding, with a clean straight stroke. Not torn. Cut.

She counted. She stopped counting and turned to the dates on either side of the gap instead. The page before was from three years ago, almost to the month. The page after was from last week.

Three years. Gone.

Wren bent the lamp closer. Most of the stubs were blank, a finger's width of paper with nothing on it. One was not. Near the top, the knife had come a hair too far from the spine and left a sliver of a heading, ruled in Teague's hand, the ink faded to brown:

unmatched — by distr—

The rest was gone with the page.

She sat back. Tuppence, displaced, climbed into her lap without asking and began to knead.

After a while Wren opened the top drawer of desk nineteen. The peppermints were on the desk; the drawer held what she had expected: string, a pumice, two spare nibs, the stub of a candle. And the clerk's razor, folded into its horn handle. She opened it carefully. The blade was clean, finely kept, the edge straight as a ruler. She laid it in the gutter of the ledger beside the stubs and did not need to try it to know that it fitted.

He could have done it himself. It was his razor and his book. Or anyone could have done it, anyone who had sat across from him long enough to know which drawer he kept it in.

She had sat across from him for four years.

She closed the razor and put it back.

* * *

It was past nine when she laid it all out on the desk.

On the left, her own quarterly sheet with its unattributed column: forty-one losses that matched to nothing. Beside it, Teague's ledger open at his last page, the line drying now, dull instead of shining. H. Teague — remainder of years. Beside that, the ledger opened at the gap, the stubs standing up in the lamplight, and the brown half-heading. unmatched — by distr—

She looked at them for a long time. Then she picked up her pen, turned her quarterly sheet to the unattributed column, and wrote in one more line at the bottom.

H. Teague, Loss Room — remainder of years. Matched to: —

Forty-two.

At the foot of the sheet, under the totals, there was a line for the clerk's signature and a choice of two words, printed side by side, one to be struck through. BALANCED. UNBALANCED. Every clerk in the Loss Room struck through the second word. Wren had struck it through sixteen quarters running. Teague had done it a hundred and sixty-some times. She had once asked him what happened if a clerk struck through the first, and he had said, "Paperwork," and gone back to his ledger.

Junior clerks who made paperwork got moved to the cellar records. Or docked their quiet hours. Or found, at the end of some month, that the buff envelope had a letter in it instead of coin.

Wren thought about the tin under her bed with its eleven unspent hours.

She struck through BALANCED. Underneath she wrote W. Aske in her small upright hand, and dated it. Then she closed Teague's quarter the same way and signed it W. Aske. It was his book and his column, and she would not put a balance on it that he hadn't believed in.

The Deputy Registrar's office was on the floor below, past the stairwell, and the door was locked, but the tray stood outside it on a narrow table for exactly this purpose. Wren put both sheets in it, face up. The word sat on the top one where anybody passing could read it.

On the record, then.

She went back up for her coat. Tuppence was asleep on desk nineteen with her chin on the peppermint tin.

* * *

She came in early, before the urn was lit, and moved her things across.

It was a strange thing, sitting on the other side. The light came from a different angle. Her old desk looked smaller from here, and very tidy, like a desk belonging to someone who had not yet been asked anything difficult. Teague's chair had come back from wherever the porters had taken it. Someone had set it straight.

The wardens' night runner had been up before her: the night's seizure lists were already shelved, and the tray held a report marked urgent. Wren took it out and unfolded it.

A warden's hand again, but a different warden, and this one had pressed so hard the nib had gone through the paper twice.

Complainant, a baker, states he went to bed in his own house on the night of the fifth, in health, aged thirty-one years. Woke at the usual hour aged, by his own account and by appearance, some forty years older. Hair white. Teeth gone. Hands no longer able to work dough. Wife did not know him at first. Neighbours called the warden. Complainant's guild mark verified against hall register. Identity not in question.

Below that, in the boxes the Office had printed for the purpose, the warden had written the same thing three times.

Caster: none known.

Charm or article: none found.

Witness: none.

Wren read it twice, once for what it said and once for what it didn't. Then she went to the shelves and pulled down every seizure list for the night of the fifth, and the two nights before it, and laid them on Teague's desk, and began to run her finger down the columns.

Forty years. Someone, somewhere, had wanted something so badly that it had cost forty years of a man's life to pay for it.

There was nothing on the lists. Not a charm, not a cord, not a whisper from the docks.

She sat back in Teague's chair and looked at the baker's report, at the three blank answers in their ruled boxes, and asked herself what could take forty years from a man in a single night without leaving so much as a knot of string behind.
