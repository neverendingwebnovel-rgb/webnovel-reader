The Tallied City · Chapter 5

Particulars

Rook didn't get up. He sat on the last dry step with his boots on the green and watched her come down the rest of the stairs, the way a man watches rain he has no plans to go out in. The skiff had pushed off. The boy had gone with it, his mended hand held against his chest as if somebody might ask for it back.

Wren kept two steps between them. Near enough to talk. Far enough to be the Office.

"Jory says you've got me in a column," Rook said.

"I have a good many people in columns."

"Not like me, he says." He considered her for a while. "Name?"

"Aske. Wren Aske."

He nodded once, slowly: a thing put on a high shelf, where he could still reach it.

"The boy's hand," she said. "Whose cost was it?"

"Mine."

He said it with no weight at all, the way Tobin Varley had said thirty-one. Wren had her answer ready. She had given it once already that week, to a young man with a tray of twine.

"The caster never pays. Someone else always does."

"So they tell me up top."

"It's the first law of the Tally."

"That's your book's law." There was no heat in it. He might have been telling her which side of a street she lived on.

"What have you got in your coat?"

He glanced down at the shape his right arm made under the cloth, and back up at her. "My other hand."

She waited. The Vell slapped the bottom step and drew off again. He let her wait; he had a great deal of practice at it, by the look of him.

"Your lot wrote me down once," he said at last. "Not credited." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "I'd have liked a copy."

Wren kept her face as still as she kept it for Dunmore. "You were on Mill Steps," she said, "on the night of the fifth."

He didn't say no. That was the first thing she would write down afterwards, before any of the rest of it: that he didn't say no.

"I go up the lane when I'm asked."

"Who asked you?"

"That's theirs."

"Which door?"

"That's theirs as well." He turned his left hand over on his knee and studied the palm. "You'd have them in your book by morning."

"A baker on that lane went to bed at thirty-one and got up with forty years on him."

"I heard." For the first time he looked away from her, up the stairs to the mouth of the lane, where the lowest of Kit's lamps had come on. "I never took forty years off anybody."

She waited for the rest: I was with, or ask her, or it was. It didn't come. He had put the baker down and set nothing in his place, and he seemed to know exactly how that would sit on a page.

He got to his feet with one hand flat on the wet wall. "Mind the green going up, clerk."

He had gone a step along the waterline when he stopped and turned, frowning, as if he had walked into a room and mislaid the reason. "What did you say your name was?"

"Aske," Wren said. "Wren Aske."

"Aske." He opened his left hand and looked into it, and closed it, and opened it again. Whatever he was looking for, he seemed to find that it was still there, or not yet gone. Then he went off along the edge of the water, round the foot of the stairs and out of the lamplight, with the loose, quick stride of a young man who has somewhere to be.

Wren stood on the step until the cold came up through her boots. She had a claim that cleared him, if anybody could be made to believe it, and a refusal that didn't, and no way she could see to weigh the one against the other.

* * *

She climbed the lane under Kit's lamps, keeping left on the greasy bit, and went home round the side of the Office and down the street. Her room was cold and her candle was down to a thumb's width. She sat on the bed with the tin under her and the notebook on her knee, and turned to Kit's page.

Rook (?) seen on the lane.

Under it she wrote what Rook had said, as near to his own words as she could get them. The ninth, dusk, river stairs. The boy's hand: "Mine." The first law: "So they tell me up top." The coat: "My other hand." The night of the fifth: does not deny the lane. Goes up "when I'm asked." Will not say which door, or who asked. The baker: "I never took forty years off anybody."

Below that, before she had decided to write anything more, her pencil had put down: Caster states the cost was his own.

She looked at it for a long moment. It was the card's line, word for word, the one she had read at the referral index after hours. Eleven years ago some other clerk had written it in another hand. Now it was in hers.

The page held three things and they pulled three ways. The claim said he paid for what he did. The refusal said he would not tell her where he had done it. And Kit's walk put him at a door near the top of the lane with careful hands, on the one night that mattered.

She pinched out the candle and lay down believing she had a witness, a sighting and a claim. The evening had cost her nothing. She slept better than she had all week.

* * *

Before light on the tenth she counted the tin by touch. Seven.

She had the sum straight now, after days of getting it wrong in her head: each hour was paid for on the morning after it was spent, so the tin always ran a day behind her. Yesterday she had declared no hour, so this morning nothing was owed. Seven chits, and all seven hers.

She walked up through the dark to the Office, and down the Loss Room row in her coat, and past the chit box at the end of it with nothing in her hand.

* * *

At desk nineteen she set out the notebook beside the warrant. She meant to enter it all under the inquiry, properly, before the floor filled: Kit's account, the casting on the stair, Rook's mine. She unfolded the warrant to do it.

She had carried it folded in four since the sixth, and had read Dunmore's name on it and the words Inquiry of Imbalance; the rest she had heard said aloud in Dunmore's office and had not troubled to read. Now she read past the signature: the old spurred capitals at the head, and the two close paragraphs of small type under them, which said what Dunmore had said. Then she turned it over to write, and found the back was not blank.

It was ruled. Across the top, in the same spurred capitals, it said PARTICULARS, and under that four columns:

Date · Hour declared · Slip initialled · Particulars taken

There was no fifth column. There was no margin to speak of. At the foot, in the spurred type, ran one paragraph:

All hours given to this inquiry, at whatever hour of the day or night, to be declared upon the slip and paid in the clerk's own chits. Nothing to be entered under this warrant but what was seen, heard or taken in an hour so declared. The clerk's own ledger and returns are her post and are not the inquiry.

She read it twice. The second time she read it as she would have read a complainant's report, for what it left out.

A row on this sheet was meant to have a date the Office could find again, an hour somebody had paid for, and a slip with somebody else's initials on it. A row with nothing behind it was a row anyone could have written at a kitchen table. She could see the sense in that. She could see it very clearly, which was the trouble.

She went down what she had. The neighbour on his step on the sixth, in a second hour that was paid for on the seventh but never put on the slip, which said one: not declared. The referral card, read after half past six on the seventh with the floor empty: no slip. Kit Morrow on the lane at dusk on the eighth, after the floor had emptied at six: no slip. The boy's palm closed on the stair, and mine, last night: no slip. None of it had an hour declared behind it. None of it could go in.

Her own time had cost her nothing, and nothing was what it had bought.

She read the small print on a chit slip every time she filled one in. She had sent this paper round attached to three slips, and she had never once turned it over.

For a while she sat with her hands flat on the desk. Then she did what a clerk does with a form that has gone against her, and filled in what she could.

Three hours, three rows. The sixth took the first, the one hour its slip had declared: the Varleys, Mill Steps, fifth door from the top; T. Varley's statement: thirty-one, aged in one night, wanted and bought nothing. The seventh: J. Dace, river stairs: R. "does it himself and pays it himself." The eighth: J. Dace, two fingers set by R. when nine: "Nobody on this stair lost a thing for it."

She blotted the three rows and read down them.

The district sheet stayed folded in her coat. Her own ledger and her own returns were her post, and the foot of the warrant said so. She found she was grateful to the paper for that one sentence, and for nothing else on it.

* * *

By late morning she had priced the rest.

Kit, retaken: an hour, if she could find him on the lane by daylight. Rook, found and heard again: an hour or more, if he could be found at all, and Jory would not tell her where he slept. The card in the referral index, read again with a slip behind it: an hour, though the drawer was across the room. Three hours, then, at the very least, out of seven, and the whole of the quarter still lying between her and Settling Day.

She took a slip from the end clerk and wrote it out: One hour, against seven held. Purpose: Inquiry of Imbalance, warrant attached.

"Spending again, Miss Aske?" Pell did not look up from desk twelve. "Between ourselves, the offer stands."

He would have paid her more than a week's rent for the hour she was about to spend. The same money would not buy her one back afterwards, not from anyone she knew of, and certainly not from Pell, who had never sold one in thirty years. She sent the slip round. It came back initialled after the noon bell.

* * *

She went out by the front steps, under the clock, and round the side of the Office to the top of Mill Steps, and started down.

The Varleys' chimney was still cold, the fifth along. There were more knots on the frames either side than she could have counted from the step, and she didn't try. Three doors below, another bakehouse had its upper half-door open and its chimney going, and a man in a floured apron filled the doorway with his arms folded. In front of him, in an apron that had been made for someone broader, stood Nell Varley.

She was holding something up to him between two fingers. A square of stiff card.

The master took it. Wren had come on down past the Varleys' by then, to within a step of his door, and he tipped it to the light from the lane, looked at the corner, and nodded. Nell had the apron untied before he had finished nodding. She put it into his hands with the chit and went down the lane fast, past the knots and the open doors, toward the river stairs. If she saw Wren she didn't stop.

The neighbour from the sixth was out on his own step, just above her, with his broom, as if he had never left it.

"He went down at first light," he said, before Wren had asked anything. "Tobin. Down the stairs, looking for him that does the mending. Hasn't come back up." He knocked the broom against the step. "Three doors put in for that. Hers can't. Ours did, and two more further down. Took a bit of finding."

Wren knew what it had taken to find. An hour cost more than a week's rent, and three households on Mill Steps had put in for this one, so that Nell could leave the bench and go and fetch a man back up off the stairs. It was the only leave in the city a master could not refuse. Without it a hand off the bench was a hand who had walked out, and could be fined for it, or struck from the working register, and Nell's household had one name going off that register already. What the Schedule set a baker's year at she knew too, and she did not do the sum.

When the master had held the chit up she had seen its corner close, closer than she had ever looked at her own. The stamp was a clock face, the hands standing on the hour: the same face as the one she had just walked under on the front steps, and the same as the disc in her pocket. She had posted four of her own chits through the slot that week, and seen there was a stamp on every one of them, and never once looked at what it was.

Nobody on the lane said anything about it. The master had gone in with the apron over his arm. The neighbour had gone in with his broom.

Wren stood with her hand in her coat, against the brass. It was the Office's, then. Its own mark, on its own things. That was all she could honestly say of it, and she did not take it out.

* * *

Kit Morrow was coming up the lane with a can of oil in one hand, leaning against the weight of it, his cap pushed back.

"Miss!" He set the can down on a step. "You're early. They're not lit."

"I've come for you, not the lamps. I need your account of the fifth again." She held up the notebook. "Properly, this time."

He liked properly. She watched him like it. He wiped his hand down his trousers as if he might be asked to shake on it, and told it again: the late round, the smoking lamp, the man coming up from the river stairs with a hooded lantern and careful hands, the door up by the bakehouse end near the top, out again before midnight.

"Everybody says it was Rook now," he added. "Whole lane."

"Did you see his face?"

Kit opened his mouth, and shut it, and thought about it honestly, which she had not expected. "No," he said. "His walk."

She wrote what he had seen and nothing he had been told. The fifth, late. A man came up from the river stairs carrying a hooded lantern, with careful hands. Went in at a door up by the bakehouse end, near the top of the lane; which door not known. Came out before midnight. Face not seen. Knew him by his walk, for Rook. Then she read it back to him, all of it, slowly, with her finger under each line.

"That's it," Kit said. "That's what I saw."

"Then put your mark to it."

She held out the pencil. If she was going to write a man's words down in a book that could take a name to the wardens, he ought to have heard them in her voice first and set his own hand under them, so that nobody could say afterwards the clerk had made it up at her desk. The guild's wax on a report was for the same thing, and so were somebody's initials on her slip. Kit took the pencil as if it might go off, and pressed his mark under the last line so hard it came through on the leaf below.

"Is that it in the book, then?" he said. "Proper?"

"It will be by the end of the hour."

He picked up his can and went on up with it, a little straighter than he had come.

Wren climbed after him, to the top of the lane and round the side of the Office, up the front steps and the stair, and sat down at desk nineteen with minutes of the hour to spare. She uncapped the ink and entered the fourth row.

The tenth · one hour · initialled · K. Morrow, lamplighter, statement retaken.

* * *

By late afternoon the light had come round onto the ledger wall, and she had not written anything else.

The particulars held four rows. The first three cleared Rook or said nothing about him at all: a baker who had wanted nothing, a knot-seller who said Rook paid his own, and the same knot-seller's two straight fingers. The fourth was the only row on the sheet with a time and a lane in it, and it put him there.

In her notebook, three pages back, Rook's mine sat under Kit's old entry in her own pencil. It could not be copied across. There was no hour behind it.

A referral wanted a loss, a caster with a name and someone to say so. She had all three on paper now, one of them only just entered, with a lamplighter's mark pressed under it. She did not refer him. Nobody came down the row to ask her to, and nothing on the desk would have stopped her.

In her coat the razor lay against the disc and the penny knot. Whoever had used it on Teague's book had cut close to the spine and clean, the way you cut when you want the leaves whole. She left it where it was.

Six in the tin, once tomorrow's chit went in.

She would have to go down to the river and find him again, in an hour that counted, with the slip behind it and the warrant open on her knee. And she did not think he would say it for nothing twice.
