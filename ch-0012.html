The Tallied City · Chapter 12

Volume I

Mr. Hesketh had a pen behind each ear and reached for neither. He took the slip from Wren by one corner, as though it had come up from the river, and held it off at the length of a thin arm.

"Nobody brings their own, Miss Aske," he said. "That's what the boy's for."

"Yes, Mr. Hesketh."

On the landing this morning the old habit, the slip in the tray and the boy to carry it, had very nearly turned her round. Last night, with Dunmore's square of paper on the blanket, she had not let herself ask who on this floor watched her hours, because asking was telling them she wondered; somewhere between the bed and the landing she had decided they knew she wondered already, and that she would sooner see the hand herself.

She had written for an hour after six, against one held. Hesketh read every word of it, the purpose too, and remarked on none of it. He took the right-hand pen from behind his ear, initialled the foot, and drew toward him a book that lay open at his left: long and narrow, ruled across, a line to a slip. He wrote today's at the bottom of the page. Date, desk, hours, against one held, the purpose cut down to his own short form, his initials closing the line.

Higher up the same page, among other desks' lines, stood two for nineteen. The fifteenth · nineteen · one · against one held. The sixteenth · nineteen · one · against one held. His initials at the end of each, as even as print.

"Who reads it?" she said.

He blotted the line before he answered, and did not look up from it. "The Deputy Registrar has it up every evening before she goes."

That was all. He did not ask why a clerk of the Loss Room should want to know, and he did not tell her anything she had not asked. He laid her slip on a pile at his elbow and waited, without impatience, for her to be somewhere else.

A chit carried no name, but a slip carried her desk and her count, and from two lines of against one held the Deputy Registrar could count her down to none; that was how she had known the tin was empty. Every hour Wren had declared since the sixth stood in that even hand, read on the evening it was written. The warrant's back held what she chose to put down; this book held the rest: when she had gone, for how long, and how little she had left each time.

"Thank you, Mr. Hesketh," she said, and meant it, and went up. Before she reached the top of the stair she glanced back and saw the pen go back behind his ear.

* * *

Teague's tally lifted the bar below the stair at a few minutes past six, and she let the door take its own weight shut behind her and heard the bar come down. She lit the lamp on the hook from her candle-end.

The ledge beside it was bare.

On the thirteenth a bone folder and a hank of waxed thread had lain there. They had lain long enough for the dust to settle round them, and the dust kept their shapes now: a long clean blade of stone where the folder had been, and beside it a rounded patch, two loops pinched together, where the hank had sat in its figure of eight. Nothing in either but stone. Three hanks entered against his name, Fenn had said. One on this ledge, then, and two gone into something; and now the one on the ledge gone too, and not into anything that showed.

She went to the high desk and found her place without hunting for it. Teague's last line, the twenty-eighth. Under it her own of the thirteenth, small and upright, the bites drawn square by square. Under that, where the page should have been clean down to tonight, a line that was not hers.

The fifteenth · L. Vane ·

Then the tally, drawn; then · In, a quarter to eleven · Out, ten past one.

The hand was small, upright and careful, a hand that did not blot. The ink had been dry a long while. She read the drawn tally bite by bite, as she had read Teague's on the thirteenth: small square bites set along a curved line, and the line went on round the circle past the place where Teague's stopped. She called up Dunmore's disc as she had seen it lying on the blotter beside his. These went on past that too, nearly round to where they had started. Ink could not show how deep a bite was cut. It could show how far round the bites ran, and these ran further than any she had seen.

A tally was cut to the grade; Dunmore had said so, with her own on the desk between them. So this one had been cut for somebody above the Deputy Registrar. A Deputy was deputy to somebody. The word said so, and in all her time on the floor she had never heard who, nor, she found now, once wondered. The name Vane meant nothing to her.

In at a quarter to eleven at night. Out after one in the morning. Two nights after her own line. Somebody read this book, and had come down to see.

The cold of the place got in under her collar then and stayed. The lamp hissed on its hook. The door at her back was barred, and the only other way out of the room was down.

She dipped the pen and wrote her line under the stranger's. The seventeenth · W. Aske · and Teague's bites, each in its place, the disc laid on the page beside them so she drew them as borne, and In, six minutes past six. She wrote it as plainly as she had ever written anything. Whoever came down next would read it, and she would not have it said her hand had shaken.

She left the lamp to the hook, and took the track by the candle.

* * *

It ran down the middle aisle, worn through to the stone and one person wide, and down the stair at its end; past the second level, older and drier, without her stopping; down the stair that turned, to the third door. Teague's tally went into the slot notched edge first, as it had gone into the other two. Something heavy rose inside the wood. The door gave.

The air on the other side was colder again, and so dry it caught in her throat. The shelves were not shelves. They were bays cut back into the rock itself, one beside another, each a little cave the depth of her arm, and the books in them were fewer and bigger than any above: ledgers in boards, laid flat as often as stood, stacked two and three deep. What lettering they had was by hand, in a hand older than any in the Loss Room.

The track went on through the dust between the bays and stopped at one. There the dust along the shelf edge had been rubbed away, the width of a sleeve, to the stone. A plain stool stood in front of it, and its seat was clean.

He had sat here. Evening after evening, by the book upstairs, he had come down three doors to sit on this stool in the cold with his sleeve on that edge.

She held the candle up to the bay. Three hanks of binder's thread. A bone folder. A man who came down here and sat. Whatever else he had done, he had sewn something; and thread entered not a month before he died would not look like thread that had been through three hundred winters. She went along the heads of the spines with the light. Old headbands, frayed, gone the dull brown of everything down here. Another. Another. Then, on a volume lying flat at the bottom of the bay, a few turns of something pale under the old frayed headband: waxed thread, new, wound round and tied off. The only clean thing on the shelf.

The boards were covered in leather gone hard and pale, the corners capped in brass gone green. The spine had no type on it and no bindery's mark. It had a paper label, and on the label, lettered by hand, a single upright stroke.

I.

The first of the Office's ledgers. The year it began. Nobody had needed to tell her that; there was nothing else a one could mean on a shelf this far down.

This was the Office's own record, read in a declared hour, and so it was the inquiry's. She took it down.

* * *

The book was heavy and stiff and it smelt of nothing at all. She set the candle on the shelf edge, in the clean place his sleeve had made, and opened the book from the back, where the thread was. The new gathering was thick enough that the boards fell open at the join, where his paper met the old.

To the left lay the last of the old leaves, and opposite it the first strip of new paper, sewn in. The old leaf was a short list in a long, sloping hand, faded nearly to the colour of the page: the year's last outgoings. Candles. Ink. Others she did not stop over. Then:

Milk, for the cat (Tuppence).

She stopped over that one for a breath. A joke, written into the Office's first book by a clerk who thought nobody would ever read it; or a custom she did not know; or something she had no word for at all. There was no telling, and she had no time to find out.

At the foot, under the last line, one word.

Settled.

Under it, a row of guild marks struck in ink, each in a different hand. The bakers' wheatsheaf, which she could have drawn blind by now. The hoop crossed with an adze, the same as the cooper's seal. The chandlers' wick and flame. The cobblers' last. Others after them that she could not place.

Settling Day closed the Office's books. It closed the city's slates, the chalk wiped off in every shop doorway on the hill. Nothing she knew gave a guild master's mark any part in either, and here the Office's first year had closed under theirs, struck one after another into its own book. What the masters of the guilds had had to settle in the Office's ledger, she did not know. She said so to herself, plainly, so as not to pretend otherwise later, and turned the leaf.

Loss Room paper. She would have known it in the dark: the ruling of her own desk, the weight of it in the fingers. A gathering as thick as her thumb, each leaf razored close along one edge and pasted to a narrow new strip, and the strips sewn in, through and through, with the new thread.

At the head of the first leaf, in Teague's hand, the whole of it at last:

unmatched — by district

Half of it she had carried since the fifth.

Under the heading he had written the quarter-day the leaf covered. She counted back to it without needing to: three years, twelve quarters gone. That was the quarter the column began to rise. Down the left ran a column of districts in his hand, in the order the returns list them: the lower town, Mill Steps, the docks, and the rest below. Across from each, figures, ones and twos. Ruled off at the foot, his total. Sixteen. The margins were clean.

He had begun counting in the quarter it began. Nobody had cut these out of his book but the man who wrote them; she had looked at that cut edge every day for twelve days, close along the spine, and this was its other side. None but the man who drew the thread had sewn them in here.

She turned to the back of the gathering. The last leaves were the week of the twenty-eighth. The column there had no total under it. The margins had no room for one: they were crowded with his small pencil, line on line, running down the outer edge and turning in at the foot toward the sewing.

Her one hour was somebody else's, and whatever she spent past it would go through the slot in the morning, a chit for each hour, and she had no chit and no rent left to buy one. Half an hour bent close over these margins might tell her what he had been doing down here; his pencil was too small to read at arm's length.

She shut the book on the margins unread.

She could not carry the leaves up, either. They were the Office's records, cut out of an Office ledger, and that was the reason he had put them in the one place they could not leave.

She laid Volume I back at the bottom of the bay, square on its own clean print in the dust, and drew her hand away. The candle showed her the head of the spine as she straightened. Pale thread, new and waxed, on a shelf of brown. That was the one thing in the bay that anybody holding a hank of the same thread would know to look for.

* * *

She came up the track to the first level with the candle low and her hour short, and there was a second light at the high desk.

Dunmore stood there in her coat. She had lifted the lamp off its hook and was holding it close to the book, so that her face was lit from below and the page was lit brighter than her face. She turned when Wren's candle came out of the aisle.

Under Wren's line of tonight was another, the ink still wet. The seventeenth · M. Dunmore · the drawn bites of the disc Wren had seen on her blotter, and In, ten to seven.

Once, she had said. The day it was cut. She had signed the book and come up. This was the second time.

"It was your own hour, ma'am," Wren said.

"It was."

Wren came to the desk. Between her line of the thirteenth and the one she had written tonight stood the one that was neither hers nor Dunmore's, and Dunmore had read it; the lamp had been held close for a reason. She did not look at it again now. She looked at Wren.

"Who is L. Vane, ma'am?"

The first thing Wren had ever asked her about anyone above her went out into the cold and did not come back.

"I told you I'd answer for the tin, Miss Aske."

Wren took it in. The tin had gone up to somebody whose bites ran nearly all the way round; and the next night, somebody had come down.

Dunmore set the lamp back on its hook. In the plain light her face was only tired.

"What was he sewing?"

Not a word about thread had passed from Wren to Dunmore, not the hanks, not Fenn; but the hook Dunmore had taken the light from hung over the ledge where the folder and the hank had left their shapes.

Wren had the answer in her mouth, and behind her eyes, two doors down in the dark, the clean thread at the head of Volume I.
