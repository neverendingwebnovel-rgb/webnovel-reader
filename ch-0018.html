The Tallied City · Chapter 18

The Quiet City

The Registrar's card was through the slot of the Loss Room's box, and Wren came down the one flight with nothing of his left on her but what he had written on the warrant.

Dunmore's door stood open. The window behind the desk held only the first grey of the morning, and the lamp had not been lit; its wick was trimmed square and the chimney was clean. Dunmore was on her feet beside the desk with the hours book open across both hands, tilted to the window, at last week's page. A small pencil tick stood against every line down it. At the foot, in her hand, was a figure.

"Thirty-four," Dunmore said.

Wren had seen that figure last night, in the Registrar's account, in the column headed By the book. So Mr. Hesketh's book had been right to the last slip, whatever made up the rest of a hundred and eighteen.

"Every one of them a slip I read," Dunmore said, and shut the book.

There was dust along her right cuff, from the wrist almost to the button, the fine dry kind that lies on a high desk under the street. She had not brushed it off. Wren had never known her carry a mark on her for longer than it took to find it.

"I went down at first light, Miss Aske." Dunmore laid the hours book on the blotter. "Under your line of the twentieth there's his, of last night. In at twenty to eight. Out at a quarter to one." She let that sit. "Five hours."

He had gone down within the hour of her leaving him and come up past midnight, while Wren lay in her bed with the warrant in her coat on the chair.

"I wrote my own line under it and came up again. I didn't go past the book." Dunmore looked at the dust on her cuff as if it had only now been pointed out to her, and left it. "I'll not ask you what's in those leaves. If anyone is to answer for what's in them, I'd sooner it was him."

Then she held out her hand. "The warrant."

Wren took it out of her coat and gave it her. Dunmore opened it at the back and went down row thirteen from its date to read on no hour, standing, without a finger on the page. When she reached the end she sat, for the first time since Wren had come in, drew the inkstand to her and wrote under the row in her black ink:

Inquiry answered. Closed, the twenty-third. M. Dunmore.

"I opened it," she said, "so I close it. An answered warrant comes back to the officer who gave it, and it stays with her." She folded it once along its old crease, put it into the top drawer on the right of her desk, and locked the drawer with one of the small keys from her ring.

Wren's coat hung lighter from the shoulder than it had in seventeen days. In it were her notebook and her district sheet, and nothing else. The only written record of what the head of the Office bought, and with whose silver, lay in a locked drawer under the Deputy's name now, and nothing Wren did tonight, or any night after, would be a row on anything.

"Settling Day was to have been its last day, ma'am."

"It was."

"What's done here on Settling Day? I've never seen it."

"You wouldn't have. The floor doesn't sit. The books close that day, and there's nothing to enter against them." Dunmore squared the hours book to the edge of the blotter. "At first light the masters come up the front steps. Thirty-one of them, one for every hall, in their chains. I take them up the stair to the landing at the top of the house. There's a leaf laid ready there, printed. Settled. at the head of it, and a space for each hall with the hall's name printed under. Each master strikes his hall's mark in its space, and I hold the inkstand for him. When the last one's done I put the leaf through the slot, and they go down to their dinners."

"Who prints the leaves, ma'am?"

"They come off a block. The block was in the Office before I was." She considered the window. "Fourteen times I've held that inkstand. It's the one morning in the year that landing has anyone on it but a clerk being sworn."

In Volume I the Office's first year closed on that same word, with the halls' marks inked one after another beneath it. The line stood a year at a time, Settling Day to Settling Day, and upstairs every year of the Registrar's account was ruled off and Carried to the line on the morning that leaf went through that slot.

"And if a master didn't set his mark, ma'am?"

"They always have," Dunmore said.

* * *

The dock warden's sheet came up third in the morning's bundle, in a hand that pressed so hard the letters stood up on the back of the paper.

T. Cardew, of the barge Mallow, of Brede, moored at the fish stairs: nine days of memory, found wanting on waking, the twenty-second. No charm found. Complainant of no hall in the city; not sealed. Asks who is to pay him; told the Office pays nothing.

Wren found no knot on the seizure list near the fish stairs and no caster in any return, and wrote unattributed against it in the far column, in the same small upright hand as every line before it. It was the first she had entered since the Registrar's one word, and the pen did not slow on it. That was what she minded. The Tally had found this payer at a mooring: a man who was not of the city, whose hall was nowhere in it, and who had paid for its quiet with nine days of a river.

She copied two words into her notebook in pencil, Mallow and fish stairs, and went on.

Toward eleven her nib split across the down-stroke of a seven. She took it to the end desk with the notebook still open in her other hand. Mr. Fenn had a paper of new nibs under his left palm and was laying them into the tray by fives, his lips going on each five. He took the split one from her between finger and thumb, looked at it, and dropped it into the tin he kept for them. He wrote the new one down against her name in the stores book.

He read her notebook upside down while he blotted the entry.

"Brede boat," he said. "Wool down, salt back."

"You know the boats, Mr. Fenn?"

"Grew up under them. My father worked the lighters." He gave her the new nib. "You'll not take a clock card down the fish stairs unless you mean to sell it, Miss Aske. He paid my father in one once, a captain did, for a day's lightering, and my father sent me down the stairs with it. There was a fellow at the head of them then who'd sell an hour on to anybody with a funeral. Now he only buys."

"Who does he buy for?"

"I never asked. A boy doesn't ask a man with silver." He went back to his nibs, and his lips took up the count at thirty-five.

Wren fitted the new nib at desk nineteen and entered the rest of the bundle. Somewhere in the second sheet she settled it: the evening was her own, and after six she would go down and look at the fish stairs, and at a payer from upriver, only so that she had seen them.

* * *

At six she went down the hill the long way, through the lower town and past the fish market with its shutters up, and the arch of Saltmarket Yard dark beside it, to where the hill gave out and there was nowhere lower to go but the river.

The docks smelt of tar and of the day's fish gone over, and under both of those of wet hemp. The stone was wet underfoot though it had not rained. A hoist stood out over the nearest barge on a timber arm, swinging bales up from the hold on a chain that rattled at every pull, and a man on the quay called the bales off by number as they came. The barges lay three deep along the moorings. A man going ashore from the outermost had to cross two other men's decks to do it, and did, with a sack on his shoulder, and nobody on either deck looked up. Somewhere along the row a mallet was knocking oakum into a seam, a slow double knock, over and over. A woman on an upturned tub was splitting the last of the day's catch into a bucket and throwing the heads to a dog that had long since learned to catch them.

The fish stairs went down beside the moorings, broad and shallow, their treads worn into hollows by generations of baskets and green where the water reached them. At their head, under the one lamp on its post, stood a man in a heavy coat. It was a coat for standing still in all night, thick as a blanket and done up to the ears, and a leather purse hung at his front on a strap. He was not selling anything. He was waiting.

Wren stopped by a stack of empty crates and waited too.

A bargeman came up from the moorings and climbed the last few treads. He was broad and somewhere in his fifties, and his hands were river hands, the creases in them black with tar, the knuckles thick. He held a card out between two fingers.

"Chandler put this in with the coin for the half of a load," he said. "It's no good upriver. Nobody in Brede honours a clock."

The man in the heavy coat took it and tilted its corner to the lamp. That was all the looking he gave it. He loosened the neck of the purse.

Wren stepped out from the crates. "Who are they for?"

He took her in, clerk's coat, ink on the second finger, and did not seem much surprised. "The purse comes down full of silver," he said. "It goes back full of card. Up there." His chin went toward the hill. "I'm paid by the card, miss. I don't ask a purse its business, and I'll not lose a card's pay finding out."

The buyer counted two silvers into the bargeman's palm. They were worn nearly smooth, their stamp gone soft as a river pebble, older than any coin in Wren's purse. Every card in the city had come out of a buff envelope as somebody's wage, and here was that purse buying them back at two silvers each. Why buy, when the hand that stamped them could have stamped one more for the cost of the ink? Each card it swallowed was a loss somebody in the city would be spared. The thick black-creased hand closed on the coins, and the card went down into the leather, where in the morning it would start up the hill.

* * *

The bargeman walked back along the moorings without hurrying, and Wren went after him. He stopped at the inside berth, where a squat barge lay with Mallow painted on her stern in white letters that the river had been at.

"Mr. Cardew?"

He turned with one foot on the gunwale. "Who's asking?"

"I'm a clerk at the Office of the Tally. Up the hill. I entered your nine days this morning."

"Then you'll know what your warden says. Pays nothing." He stepped down onto the deck. "You can come aboard if you like. I've a pump to work, and it doesn't work itself while I talk."

She came aboard. The deck was wet and gritty with salt from the hold, and it moved a little under her, which the stone on the hill never did. Cardew set his back to the pump and began, a long steady stroke, and the water came out of the spout and went over the side.

"Nine days," he said. "I've got casting off at Brede. I had the stern rope in my hand. Next I've got, I'm waking up tied here, and it's the twenty-second, and the load's half sold." The pump knocked. "The rest of the river's what my mate tells me. Says I was right as anything the whole way down. Took her through the narrows myself." Another stroke. "Thirty years I've worked this river. I could tell you every shoal between here and Brede with my eyes shut. Ask me how it went this time and I've nothing to tell you but what he says."

He did not sound bitter about it so much as put out, a man who had lost a tool and was going to have to work without it.

"Is your mate aboard?"

"Ashore, drinking the half of the load that's sold." He tipped his head at the open hatch as if the man might be down there after all. "He'll tell it you, if you've the evening. He's told it me four times now, and it's no better for the telling. I keep waiting to know it when I hear it, and I don't."

"What would they do in Brede," Wren said, "if it had happened there?"

"It wouldn't, not like this." He let the pump rest and took the lantern down off its hook to look at the wick. "Somebody in Brede wants a working done, it's done out past the last field. Nobody within reach of it but the stander. And the stander's been paid first, and said yes to it. Everybody else keeps well off till it's over."

"And if nobody says yes?"

"Then they go without." He trimmed the wick with his thumbnail. "We ask first."

He hung the lantern up again and looked past her at the city, at the roofs climbing behind the fish market.

"Mind, you'd not get much to take here anyway. The quiet city." It came out of him like the name of a reach he had passed a thousand times. "Everybody on the Vell knows it. Nothing much will take in Vellmark. You can't raise a wind in Vellmark. Some say it's the hill does it. Some say it's the old stone of the place."

Wren looked up the hill. From the deck it was only roofs going up on roofs into the dusk, house over house, with the clock face at the top of them too far off to read. A city built roof over roof had no last field to go out past. She heard the hill and the old stone, and let them stand.

Cardew took up the pump again. Three strokes, four.

"So who did it?" he said. "In Brede I'd know whose field. I'd know who'd stood for it, and what he'd been paid. Who's to pay me, up your hill?"

Wren had the whole of the answer, down to the column it stood in three levels under the street. It was the name she worked under, and the record of it was the Office's. She had no notion what it would start, said aloud on a deck in the dusk to a man with four days of river to carry it up, and she did not open her mouth to find out.

Cardew shrugged over the pump handle. "It'll be nobody. Your warden said as much."

The pump knocked on. Wren kept her inked hand on the wet gunwale and watched the water go over the side, and did not tell him he was wrong.
