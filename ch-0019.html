The Tallied City · Chapter 19

The One Over

The blank returns came up on the twenty-fourth, a bundle in brown paper tied with tape, and the runner dealt them down the row with the morning's losses as if each were one more loss apiece. Wren's came to rest under a warden's sheet from the lower town. She drew it out to square it against the edge of the desk, and because she had read the foot of every paper since the warrant's, she read this one's.

The Office of the Tally, Vellmark: Quarterly Return of Losses, the head said. The columns stood ruled and empty for the quarter that would close on Settling Day. Below the box where a clerk struck through one word and signed under the other, in type so small it might have passed for the edge of a rule, was a single line:

Printed at the Office of the Tally, for its own use.

The forms came up from somewhere in the house smelling of oil, and where in the house they came from had always been the runner's business. Dunmore's leaf came off a block, and a block did not cut itself: somebody had set it, letter by letter, and nobody could set a letter without reading it. If the Office printed its own, then the leaf the masters marked had been made in this house by a man who knew every word on it. Dunmore had told her what was done with that leaf. Nobody had told her what it said.

She entered the warden's sheet, blotted it, and took the blank return down the row to desk twelve.

Pell had his Schedule open on the blotter and a cup of the urn's tea beside it with the steam gone off, which was how he took it; it could not, he said, get worse once it was cold. Wren laid the return on the Schedule's open page with its foot toward him.

"Where are these printed, Mr. Pell?"

He put a finger on the small line and drew the paper toward him. "Here. In the house, I mean. The press room. Ground floor at the back: the door at the foot of the stair, not the flight that goes on down. Mr. Lusk." He gave the return back to her. "The Office sets its own and always has. Nothing with the clock on it is printed outside this house."

"You've been in."

"Once." He turned the Schedule a few leaves back, to a page of trades and ages, and tapped a figure in the third column. "Years ago. This had a letter dropped out of it, and the sum came out wrong by it. I wanted to see whether the forme had it or only my copy."

"Which was it?"

"The forme." The corner of his mouth went in. "It still has. Lusk wouldn't break a standing forme for one letter, and nobody else has ever written to say." He closed the Schedule on it. "Desk twelve is owed a quire of blanks, while you're asking. The bundle's always short at this end of the row. You can carry that down, if you're going."

She was going. It was the Office's errand, in the Office's working day, inside its walls, and wanted no slip.

Pell looked at the return in her hand, at the line along its foot.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske," he said, "I've signed one of those every quarter for thirty years, and I've never once read the foot of one."

* * *

She went down the main stair past Dunmore's landing and on to the bottom, where the street door stood open on the morning and the extra flight dropped away toward the door she had opened with a dead man's disc. At the back of the hall, in the shadow under the stair, was a second door, painted the same brown as the wainscot. She had walked past it every morning and taken it for a cupboard.

The smell met her before the door was half open. Ink, first, thick and sweetish, and under it machine oil and wet paper and something scorched from the stove in the corner. The room ran a long way back, low, lit by two windows high in the back wall, and it was full of sound: a slow iron creak, a knock, the creak again. A hand press stood in the middle of the floor, black, taller than a man, its great screw worn bright where a hand went round it. Along one wall stood the cases of type on sloped racks, rows of shallow drawers divided into little square pits. Paper hung over a line strung under the ceiling, sheets of it, drying in a row, and when the door's draught reached them they lifted and settled with a hiss.

A short man in a leather apron was at the bar of the press. He pulled, and the platen came down with the creak she had heard, and he let it up again and drew out a sheet and laid it on a board with the others. Then he looked round.

He was in his sixties, stooped from the shoulders in a way that had nothing to do with age; he had spent his life bent over the case. Ink had gone into the grain of his hands and stayed there, a blue-black in every crease of the knuckles and every line of the palm, and it ran up past the wrist before it gave out. A pair of spectacles sat on his nose.

"Desk twelve's quire," Wren said. "Mr. Pell's."

"Pell." He came round the press, wiping his hands on a rag that could not have made them worse. "Pell and his dropped letter. He'll have told you." He went to a shelf of blank stock, counted out sheets with a wetted thumb, faster than she could follow, squared them on the bench with two knocks and folded a band of waste round them. "There. And tell him it's still dropped."

She took the quire and did not go.

"Do you print the Settling leaf, Mr. Lusk?"

He looked at her with more interest than he had shown the quire. "I do. Nobody asks after that one." He pointed with his chin to a rack along the end wall, where iron frames stood upright in slots, each with type locked inside it by wedges, the faces of the letters black and shining. "Standing formes. Things the Office doesn't mean to change. You set them once and lock them up in the chase and you never break them up again. That one was standing when my master had the press from his, and it was old then." He touched the end of one, a frame a little larger than a sheet of the hours book. "The Deputy calls it a block. Block will do, upstairs."

"How many do you pull?"

"One a year. One goes up, and one over, in case a master blots. Mr. Sefton takes them up the night before." He was already at the spike by the window, where proofs hung threaded through on a long nail, and beside it a fat press-book with its boards held on by string. "The one over comes back down to me after, clean. Nobody's blotted in my time. And I keep it, because the press keeps one pull of everything it prints. Always has." He turned leaves of the press-book. "I've not pulled this year's yet. That's two months off. Here's last year's."

He put it on the stone, a flat slab of marble at the end of the bench with the ink of a hundred jobs worn into it, and stood back.

Wren read it as she read any page, from the top.

Settled. stood at the head, in the old face with the spurred capitals, the stop after it as heavy as a nailhead. Under it the leaf was ruled into spaces, four rows, the last row one short. Under each space a hall's name was printed small: Bakers under the first, then Coopers, Chandlers, Cobblers, and on along the row. She counted them. Thirty-one. Every space was empty. This was the one over; nobody had ever put a hand to it.

At the foot, under the bottom row, there was a line smaller than anything else on the leaf, smaller than the line at the foot of the warrant had been, so small that she bent to it, and Lusk pushed his spectacles up onto his forehead and bent with her, as if he too were reading it for the first time in a year.

By these marks the halls of the city, as in hall, give the line another year, from this Settling Day to the next. Wanting the mark of any hall, the line is not given.

"There's your Schedule," Lusk said, pleased with it. "You'll have seen the front leaf. As assented by the masters of the guilds, in hall. Same words, near enough. That's the halls putting their hands to the rates another year. The line's the line of rates." He tapped the foot with an inky nail. "And that's why the rates never shift. They're settled every year. You can't move a thing that's settled every year; the halls would have to agree to it first, and they never agree to anything."

Wren looked at the two sentences under his nail.

"May I copy it, Mr. Lusk?"

"Copy away. It's printed. There's nothing secret in a thing that's printed." He went back to the press, and the creak began again.

She set the quire down, took her notebook and her pencil out of her coat and copied the foot of the leaf onto a clean page, every word, every comma, the stop after next and the stop after given. Below, in the third level, with Volume I open before her, she had copied nothing. This she copied; the notebook was hers, and she meant to carry these. When she had finished she read her copy against the print, word for word, and found nothing to mend.

She was putting the notebook away when she saw the small one.

It stood on the rack beside the Settling forme, a frame no bigger than her hand, the type in it the same old face, the same spurs on the capitals. Its heading ran backwards, as all type did, but the letters were large enough to read the wrong way round, right to left: THE CLERK'S OATH. Under the heading the lines were set close, and backwards, and black, and too small to give up anything from where she stood.

"Would you pull me one of those?"

Lusk did not stop the press. "That one's the Deputy's."

"The Deputy's?"

"She sends down for a card when there's a swearing. One card, one clerk. I pull it, it goes up, and after it comes back down to me and goes in there." He nodded at the stove. "Only pull in this room I don't keep. I don't know why. It's how I had it from my master." The platen came down. "That one's the Deputy's."

So the card her oath had been read from on her first morning had come back down to this room after, and into that stove, while she went down to her desk.

She picked up the quire.

"See Pell gets those," Lusk said, "and not some thief at desk thirty."

She went up the main stair with the quire under her arm and did not open the notebook again. She had seen the line. It ran across the first leaf of Volume I under four printed heads, the last of them ruled empty all the way down, and under her row twelve the Registrar had written that it stood; thirty-one hands kept it standing, and any one of them could let it fall. The words that said so were pulled every year in a room any clerk with a quire to fetch could walk into, with a spare kept in a book tied with string, and not one master who struck his mark knew what the line was.

* * *

After six she walked down the street the front steps looked down, to the bakers' hall halfway along it.

The clerk's room off the yard was as warm as before and smelt of yeast and banked coals. Mrs. Orme sat at the high desk under the shelf of canvas-covered books with the dues open in front of her, and a man stood at her elbow going down the columns with a broad finger. He was big through the shoulders, and his sleeves were pushed up over forearms that were floury to the elbow. Behind the door, on a peg, hung a green baize bag with something heavy and shapeless in it that kept the cloth drawn down in a long point.

"It's the Office's young woman," Mrs. Orme said. "Come about the charter."

"Not the charter, Mrs. Orme." Wren looked at the man. "The master?"

"Garrod. That's me." He did not take his finger off the dues. "What does the Office want with the bakers this time?"

"Nothing. The Office didn't send me. It's my own question." She had no paper to put on the desk and put nothing on it. "What does the master set his mark to, on Settling Day?"

Garrod took his finger off the column then. He took her in from head to foot, and then turned to Mrs. Orme, who raised her eyebrows at the dues.

"A clerk," he said, "out of that house, on her own legs, asking a baker what goes on in it." He folded his arms, and flour came off them. "Well, I'll tell you, since none of them up there will. It's the hall's mark on the city's year. The morning the city squares its slates, the halls square with the Office. That's all it is. Our name on the year, with the rest. The halls have gone up that morning since the Prohibition. The first masters went up the day it came in, so the hall says, and we've gone up every year since."

"There's a line of print at the foot of the leaf," Wren said. "Very small."

"There's small print at the foot of everything the Office gives you. I've a drawer full of it." He said it without heat. "I put my mark in my space, Miss. That's what the space is for."

"And if you didn't set it?"

The heat came then. It came up his neck and into his face, and Mrs. Orme's pen stopped.

"Then the bakers would be the hall that didn't," Garrod said. "First in three hundred years. And I'll not be the man." He unfolded his arms and put both hands flat on the edge of the desk. "You don't know what a hall is, up there. A master who came down that hill without his mark on the leaf would not be master by the hall's dinner. They'd have the chain off him before the bread was cut, and they'd be right to."

Mrs. Orme's pen went on.

The working register lay open at her other elbow, at the month's-end page. Wren had not meant to look and looked. Against T. Varley the pencil rule was still there, as light as the first time, not yet gone over.

Garrod followed her eye. "I ink those myself," he said, "the month's strikes. At the month's end. It's the master's hand does it, nobody else's." He kept his eyes on the name. "Good hands, his. Were. And nobody to answer for it, the Office says." He turned back to the dues. "Well. The Office would know."

The notebook was against her ribs, and the two sentences were in it, and she had only to open her coat. She kept her hands at her sides.

On the page between them the rule against Tobin's name lay thin and grey as a hair across the paper.

* * *

The street was going blue when she came back up it, and the clock over the front steps had lost its numbers. The street door was shut. As she reached the foot of the steps it opened, and Dunmore came out, and it shut behind her, and the bar went across on the inside.

Dunmore came down the steps without the rail, her gloves in one hand and an umbrella furled in the other, taking each step with the whole of her foot. She saw Wren at the third from the bottom and stopped there.

"Miss Aske."

"Ma'am. I've read the foot of the Settling leaf."

Dunmore did not come down the last three steps.

"Mr. Lusk keeps last year's one over," Wren said.

"Does he." Dunmore looked past her down the street, and back. "On the morning I set the inkstand on the foot of that leaf, to keep it flat for them. It's a stiff paper and it curls. I lift it for each master and set it back between. That's where the Deputy before me showed me it stands, and it's where it has stood." She drew the gloves through her hand. "I read what I'm to sign, Miss Aske. Nobody signs the foot."

Wren took out the notebook and opened it at the page and held it up to her, turned so the last of the light fell on it.

Dunmore read it once. She did not take it. Her hand with the gloves in it came up as though to hold the page flat, though there was no wind, and fell short of it.

"Under my inkstand," she said.

Wren tore the leaf out along the stitching and held it out, the Office's words, printed in its own house, going no further than its own officer's hand. Dunmore took it.

Dunmore folded it once and kept it in her hand. Then she turned, and went back up the steps she had just come down, to the top, and knocked on the street door for the porter to let her in.
