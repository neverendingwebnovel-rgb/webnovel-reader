The Tallied City · Chapter 6

What's Been Paid

Before light on the eleventh the Loss Room was the colour of cold tea, and the only sound in it was Wren's boots on the boards.

She had counted the tin by touch in her room in the dark, the way she did every morning now: seven. One had come out into her glove at the door, and the lid had gone back down on six. She kept the glove on all the way up the hill and did not take it off until she stood at the end of the row.

Tuppence was sitting on the boards beside the box's stool with her tail round her feet, watching the slot. Wren posted the chit. That was the tenth paid for: one hour, the lamplighter on the lane, his mark pressed under his statement. Six at home under the bed. The cat watched the slot a moment longer, then looked up at Wren and went off behind the ledger shelves.

At desk nineteen Wren lit the lamp, unfolded the warrant and turned it over.

Four rows in her own writing, and a great deal of ruled paper under them. The Varleys on the sixth. Jory on the seventh, and Jory again on the eighth. Kit on the tenth, retaken, read back and marked. The first three had each been taken inside the hour its slip declared; what she had lost was everything done outside them, the second hour on the sixth that the slip never mentioned and the evenings after the floor emptied. She did not count it over again. She had counted it enough.

None of the four rows said what Rook had been doing on Mill Steps on the night of the fifth. One of them put him there.

She sat with her hands flat on either side of the sheet. Rook worked the river stairs when he was about. Tobin Varley had gone down them at first light yesterday, looking for the man who did the mending. At dusk on the ninth she had had Rook two steps below her, talking, and every word of it was on her own time and worth nothing to the warrant. If he was anywhere an hour could reach, he was at the bottom of those stairs, and if she went by daylight she would at least see who else was waiting for him.

One hour. She took a slip from the end clerk and wrote: One hour, against six held. Purpose: Inquiry of Imbalance, warrant attached. It went round. She did the morning's entries while it was gone, a burned hand in the lower town, a lost day on the far bank matched to a charm already on the seizure lists, and she did them carefully so that nobody could say the inquiry had cost the Office a line. The slip came back after the noon bell with initials on it.

She went out by the front steps, under the clock, and round the side of the Office to the top of Mill Steps. The lane smelt of bread and hot iron. Five doors down, the Varleys' chimney stood cold among the smoking ones, and the knots on the frames either side had begun to hang in clusters, like onions. Three doors below that the half-door was open again, and inside it, at another man's bench, with her back to the lane and her sleeves pushed up, Nell Varley was kneading dough that was not hers. Wren did not stop. Nell had spent the lane's one hour yesterday, the hour three households had put in for. There was no second one to bring her down the stair today, and a hand who left the bench without one had walked out.

Wren kept left on the greasy stretch, and at the foot the cobbles gave way to steps.

* * *

Jory was at his tray halfway down the river stairs, sitting with his knees up near his ears. He looked at the warrant in her hand and not at her.

"Afternoon," he said, to the warrant.

Below him the stairs ran down to the green and the water, and on the last dry step, where Rook had sat on the ninth, a man was sitting with his hands on his knees.

It took her a moment, though she had sat at his table. Tobin Varley was thirty-one, he had told her so and asked her to write it down, and everything about the way he sat said otherwise: the care with which his back met the wall, the knees drawn in against the damp, the hands laid on them palms down as if they had been set there by someone else and might be wanted back. His white hair lifted a little in the wind off the Vell. He did not turn round.

"Since first light," Jory said, low. "Second day running. Nell come down for him yesterday on the lane's hour and took him home, and he's back. She can't come again." He picked a knot off his tray and put it down in the same place. "His own oven's cold. No bench to walk off, no master to ask. So he can sit there as long as he likes, and he does."

"Does Rook know?"

"I told him there's a man waiting." Jory shrugged one long shoulder. "He'll come or he won't."

Wren looked down at the bent back on the last step. "Where did the lane find a chit? Yesterday."

"Took them the whole morning." Jory said it as though it still annoyed him on their behalf. "Nobody sells now. Nobody on this side of the Vell, anyway. Three doors put in, and then they had to go and find one. There's a fellow at the fish stairs buys every hour going, any rate you like for it, and asks no names." He wiped his nose on his wrist. "And I never met a soul who bought one off him."

Wren turned that over. An hour cost more than a week's rent. There was a man on the docks who would pay that or better for any hour anyone brought him, and nobody had seen him spend one or sell one back. Pell had never spent or sold his either, but he had bought the end-desk clerk's at a clerk's price so that he could feel he might leave; nobody bought every hour going, at whatever rate, to leave that often. Buying on that scale and doing neither was throwing coin into the river, and she could not make it come out, so she let it sit where it was. The Office belonged to no guild, which was why every guild took its stamp, and why a square of stamped card would let a woman off any bench in the city. She had never thought of it as something anyone would want by the sackful.

"Thank you," she said, and went on down.

* * *

She stopped three steps above Tobin. He knew she was there; his head moved a little. He did not say anything and neither did she.

Rook came along the waterline from round the foot of the stairs, the way he had gone on the ninth, not hurrying, with his right hand in his coat. He came up onto the green as if it were a floor he had laid himself. He saw Wren and nodded.

"Clerk."

Then he knelt on the wet stone in front of Tobin Varley, so that their faces were level.

"You're him," Tobin said.

Rook said nothing.

"You mended Jory's fingers." Tobin's voice was steady and quite plain. He had thought about this on the step all morning, Wren saw, and all yesterday, and had decided how to say it. "You mended a boy's hand. Mend this."

He lifted his hands off his knees and held them out.

Rook took the nearer one in his left hand. He turned it over, palm up and back again. He pressed along the knuckles with his thumb, one after the next, the way Wren had seen the bindery men run a thumb down a spine for a loose signature, and Tobin did not flinch, though his mouth went thin. Then Rook laid the hand back on Tobin's knee, gently, as if returning something he had borrowed.

"There's nothing broke in it," he said.

Tobin looked at him.

"I don't mend what's been paid."

"I never bought anything."

"No," Rook said. "I don't suppose you did."

It was kind, and it was no more than that. The river came up the bottom step and went back. Wren stood above them with the warrant against her coat and thought: that is why the Schedule pays in coin. Nobody had ever given a year back. Not a caster, not the wardens, not the Office with its three hundred years of ledgers; the Prohibition had looked at years gone in a night and settled for making someone pay for them, because there was nothing else to make anyone do.

Tobin's hands lay on his knees. When he spoke again his voice was harder.

"They say up the lane you were there. That night."

"I was up the lane."

"Which door?"

"That's theirs," Rook said, and as he said it his eyes went up the stairs, past Jory and his tray, to where the steps turned into the mouth of Mill Steps, and stayed there a moment. Wren could not have said what was in the look. A man might look like that at a house he had robbed. He might look like that at one he had sat up all night in.

Tobin got himself up. He did it with one palm flat on the wall and then the other, a little at a time, and when Wren came down a step and put out her arm he shook his head without looking at it. He went past her and started the climb, one step and then the feet together, one step and the feet together, with the whole of the river stairs and nearly all of the lane still above him. As he passed her he stopped, and said, not loudly, to her and not to Rook:

"He looked at my hands like he knew them."

Then he went on. She watched him past Jory's tray and on up. At the end of the month the guild would strike his name from the working register, and he would keep the wheatsheaf on his door. His was the name going off it. She had written his age on a sheet of the Office's paper because he had asked her to. It was the only thing anyone had given him.

* * *

When she turned back, Rook had sat down on the step Tobin had left, with his boots on the green.

She had heard all of it. She had heard it with a slip behind her and initials on the slip, and every word of it could go in: what Rook had said to Tobin Varley and what he had not. She felt the weight of that in her coat like a stone.

"You said it to him in my hour," she said. "That goes in as what I heard. I'd like the rest of it in your words. The boy's hand. Whose cost it was. You say it, I write it, I read it back to you, and you put your mark under it. As the lamplighter did."

What she heard in a declared hour was hers, the clerk's evidence, answerable to her slip and written in her words. A man's own account was another thing. It stood as his statement only when it had been read back to him and his mark was set under it, so that nobody could say afterwards the clerk had put it in his mouth. And he had to choose to.

Rook looked at the water for some time.

"And what's it buy me?"

She had no answer ready. He did not seem to need one.

"Your lot wrote me down once," he said. "I said I'd have liked a copy."

"The card."

"What it says. Written out." He turned his head and looked at her for the first time since he had sat down. "You'll have read it."

She had, by lamplight, with nobody on the floor, and she could have said it to him now, standing on the wet step, without the drawer.

"Records of the Office don't leave the Office," she said.

Nobody had had to tell her that. The referral index held the names of casters, and of the people who had seen them, and of the wardens who had taken them; a card copied out and carried down a lane could warn the one or set a street on the other. If she ever referred Rook, Kit Morrow's name would go into that drawer beside his, with the mark he had pressed through to the leaf beneath.

Rook nodded, as if that were the answer he had expected and the one he had come for.

"Then you know what it costs," he said.

He got up with one hand flat on the wet wall and went back along the waterline the way he had come, round the foot of the stairs and out of sight.

Wren stood where she was. A man who said he paid his own costs, who would not say which door, had put a price on his own word, and the price was paper. Not coin. Not a promise to leave him off a sheet. The one paper in the city that called him a caster, in the Office's hand, closed and filed under his name. She could not think what a man would want with it.

She climbed. Jory did not look up as she passed.

* * *

She was back at desk nineteen with minutes of the hour to spare, her breath still short from the hill. She uncapped the ink.

The eleventh · one hour · initialled · heard, river stairs: R. to T. Varley, "I was up the lane"; asked which door, "That's theirs"; of T. Varley's hands, "I don't mend what's been paid."

She blotted it and read down the sheet from the top. Five rows. Two of them now put Rook on Mill Steps on the night of the fifth: Kit's, marked, with a lantern and a door near the top of the lane, and her own, in his words. She had gone down to the river to find the thing that would balance the fourth row, and the one hour she had paid for had bought the fifth.

His Mine was in her notebook, in pencil, on her own time, where it had been since the ninth. Under it was the line she had found herself writing that night, one she knew by heart from the card. In her notebook it was hers; a clerk's notebook was her own, like the district sheet folded in her coat. Written out on a clean page and put into his hand, the same words would be the Office's.

She folded the warrant in four and put it in the drawer.

* * *

At six the floor emptied the way it always did, chairs back, lamps down, the urn on the landing ticking as it cooled. Pell was slow with his muffler, and Wren was slow with her coat, and they went down the stair together.

Past the Deputy Registrar's floor, past the turn where the light from the street door came up to meet them. Pell talked about the tea. At the ground floor Wren stopped on the last step, because he had stopped to get his second glove on, and she looked where the stair went on.

It went on down. She had always known that, in the way she knew the number of desks in the Loss Room, without ever having looked. Beyond the turn the stair dropped another flight into the dark below the street, and at the bottom of it was a door. There was no handle. There was no keyhole. Where a keyhole would have been, a narrow brass plate was set into the wood with a slot cut in it, and over the door, cut into the stone of the lintel, was the clock: the same face as the one over the front steps, hands standing on the hour, gone soft at the edges with age. In four years she had never seen it open.

Pell followed her look.

"Teague went down there, you know," he said. "Most evenings, this last year. Came back up grey to the elbows." He worked his fingers into the glove. "Between ourselves, I thought he was looking for somewhere quieter to die."

He laughed, a short one, at the shape of it. Then he heard it. Wren watched it reach him: the desk, a little after three, the pen still in the hand. He stood with the glove half on and looked down the dark flight at the door as if the door had said it, and not him.

"That was not well put," he said. "He was a good clerk." He pulled the glove the rest of the way on, carefully, finger by finger, and did not look at her. "Goodnight, Miss Aske."

He went out under the clock without the rest of it.

Wren did not ask how a man got through a door with no handle. She stood on the last step with her hand in her coat pocket, round the razor and the knot and the small hard edge of the disc, and looked at the brass plate a moment longer. Her mind was on a card in a drawer two floors up. Then she went out after Pell, into the street.

* * *

In her room she knelt and counted the tin. Six. Five, once tomorrow's chit went in for the hour she had spent today.

The card was on her list already. She had meant since the tenth to read it again with a slip behind her, so that what it said could stand on the particulars and not only in her head: one hour. Copying it out for Rook would take ten minutes of that same hour. Nobody at the index would look twice at a clerk writing. It was still the one thing in all of it the Office would call a breach, and she had never yet done a thing the Office would call by that name, not on purpose.

She sat on the bed with the notebook open on her knee at a clean page and the pencil in her hand. She did not need the drawer. The hedge-worker called Rook, taken in the act. Payer sought: none found in the Tally within radius or time. Caster states the cost was his own. Not credited. It was all there behind her eyes, every line, in the order it came.

The pencil stayed where it was, over the page.

She had kept the city's records for four years, and nobody had ever asked her for one.
