The Tallied City · Chapter 11

Information Laid

INFORMATION LAID, said the heading, in the plain even type the wardens printed in, without a spur on any of its letters. It lay on the tray at her elbow on top of the morning's returns, and somebody downstairs had already turned it so that the heading faced her chair.

Under it a warden had written, in a round hand that bore down hard on every stroke:

Against: S. Ewart, baker, master of the bakehouse next below the lodging, Mill Steps. Of the loss of: T. Varley, baker, forty years, the night of the fifth (clerk of the line, W. Aske). Laid by: N. Varley, of the same house. Her mark. On the informant's own knowledge.

Nell's mark stood against Laid by: a short cross, cut so hard that the nib had caught and spat.

Wren read it from the foot up, as she read anything with her own name in it, because the foot said what she had to do and the rest only said why. The foot was printed:

To be answered by the clerk of the line on the day received: matched and referred, or set aside, the reason entered and initialled. No information to be acted upon unanswered.

That day was today. The wardens would lay no hand on a man for it. A charge wanted a matched loss, and only the hand that held the line could say whether this one matched, so they had written down what they were told, taken a mark under it and sent it up the hill to be tested. Until it was, Sim Ewart stood under his neighbour's word. Nobody had read it back to Nell. Reading back was the Office's habit and not the wardens'. On the informant's own knowledge was their form of words, the line every information closed on, and it said nothing at all about what Nell had seen.

Ewart's bakehouse was the first half-door down, where Kit had stopped last night and leaned in to the man at the oven.

She could match it. Then a man went to the wardens on the lane's say-so: the very thing she had said No to for Rook, done to someone with less to stand on. Or she could set it aside, and a reason had to go in the box under it. She had nine rows on the back of her warrant, and two of them spoke to which door at all. Row four was Kit's statement retaken, and the leaf behind it said which door not known and, a line lower, that he knew the man by his walk, for Rook. Row five was Rook on the river stairs, asked which door, saying That's theirs. Set Ewart aside on either, and she handed the wardens Rook in the same stroke, with her initials under it.

Every way out of the paper went through a name. The only ground that named nobody was Nell's own word, taken down and read back to her and marked, and Nell was on the lane, at another man's bench, in the working day. That meant an hour off the floor before six. The tin under her bed was empty. The fifteenth's chit had been her last.

She had told Kit No, and this was what the lane had made of it. She did not pretend otherwise.

* * *

Mr. Fenn had the stores book open under his left hand and was counting nibs out of a paper into a tray by fives, his lips moving on each five. He had sold an hour across this desk before, to Pell.

"Morning, Miss Aske. Nibs. There's somebody on this floor eats them."

"Mr. Fenn," she said, "would you sell me an hour?"

He finished the five he was on before he looked up. "Two silvers," he said. "Rate of the day."

"Yes."

"I'll not take more off a clerk than the day's rate, and I'll not take less, so you needn't try me." He said it kindly, the way a man gives the weather.

The next two weeks' rent was in her coat, in its own fold of the purse, put by so as not to be spent. She counted it onto his blotter, one silver and then the other. The purse slid into her pocket a good deal lighter than it had come out.

He swept the coins into his drawer with the side of his hand and brought out a chit from somewhere behind them: a square of stamped card, the clock in its corner, and nothing else on either face. No name, no desk number, nothing to say it had been his when he came in this morning. She turned it over once. Whoever held it held the hour.

He did not ask what it was for. He was counting again.

"You're the second off desk nineteen," he said, "to want a thing of me that isn't on a slip." He tapped the stores book with the end of his pen. "Mr. Teague had me enter three hanks of binder's thread. Not a month before he went. Never said what for." Five more nibs went into the tray. "I don't ask. It's down against his name, and that's all the book wants of me."

Binder's thread. She saw the ledge inside the first door below the stair, at the height of her hip, and the hank lying beside the bone folder, waxed and tied off. Something sewn, then, and sewn down there, by a man who—

She set the thought down. She had one hour, bought with her rent, and a paper on her blotter that named a man and had to be answered by six. The thread would keep till tomorrow. She took a slip off Mr. Fenn while she stood there and filled it in at his desk, with his pen, against one held, and it was back on her blotter initialled before two.

* * *

Coming down onto Mill Steps from its head, past the lodging, she found Sim Ewart's the one door on the lane shut.

Both leaves of it were to, in the middle of a working afternoon, with the smell of the other ovens all down the hill and bread to be sold off every other board. The top leaf hadn't been pulled quite square. Through the gap she could see his board inside, with the morning's loaves still on it in a full row, their crusts gone dull. Farther in a man sat on a sack by the oven mouth, his forearms on his knees and his hands hanging. He never turned his head.

Either side of his door the knots on the frames were new since yesterday, the twine still pale and the pins bright. While Wren stood there a woman came out of the bakehouse below with one in her fingers and fastened it to her own frame beside the wheatsheaf. She tugged it to see it would hold and went back in without once looking at her. Nobody was hanging them for their own luck any more. They were all hung toward his door.

Tobin Varley was on his own step at the fifth door, his hands on his knees and the wheatsheaf still on the door behind him.

"Not Sim," he said, before she had reached him.

"Mrs. Varley laid it."

"I know who laid it." He did not look up the lane. "Sim Ewart's never wanted anything in his life but his oven. You could set the street alight and he'd want to know was it drawing."

She stood on the step below his. His eyes went from her to his hands, and she thought he had finished. He had had nothing to do for days but sit.

"Down the stairs," he said. "When he knelt to me."

"I was there."

"You were standing. I was looking straight at him." He moved one hand on his knee, a little, as far as it went. "His coat came open when he got down. He'd a beam in his coat."

Wren said nothing.

"Little iron beam. No pans to it. His other hand had it by the middle." He was quiet. "I've weighed every loaf I ever made on a beam. What's a man want with one that's got no pans?"

"I don't know," Wren said, and didn't. Last night something under that coat had knocked once on the step, and she had not known what it was then either.

"Nor me," he said. "I've sat here and turned it every way. You can't weigh with it. Weigh what?"

It had been said to her in a declared hour, and it could go on the sheet as what she heard. She wrote it in her notebook instead, under the day's date, because on the sheet it would lay the inside of Rook's coat on Dunmore's desk, and nothing in it answered the paper she had come about.

* * *

Nell had the upper leaf open at the third door below the Varleys' and both arms in another man's trough. The borrowed apron was wound twice round her and tied at the front. She did not stop when Wren's shadow came across the bench.

"Mrs. Varley. Your information came to my desk this morning."

"I know where it went."

"I'd like your account of what you know of Mr. Ewart and the night of the fifth. Written down, read back to you, and your mark under it if it's right."

Nell folded the dough over on itself and leaned her weight into it. "I saw nobody. I've told you that."

"Then where did his name come from?"

"The lane." She turned the dough a quarter and bore down again. "Best bread on the steps since the thaw, his. The lane says nobody comes by a loaf like that for nothing."

"And the lane had it from?"

"Kit Morrow. Last night, up and down, telling it." Nell's mouth went tight. "You'd told him no. He'd put his mark to your paper, hadn't he, and they've been at him over it ever since, and once you'd said no he'd nothing left to give them. So he gave them Sim."

"And you went to the wardens."

"I told you to get something else." The heel of her hand went into the dough. "You didn't. So I went and got it."

Wren opened her notebook to a clean leaf and wrote, in pencil, on the corner of the bench where the flour was thinnest. Then she read it back line by line, as she had read Kit's, because the leaf was nothing until Nell had heard every word of it.

"Nell Varley, of the Varleys' house, Mill Steps, the sixteenth. She saw no one go in at any door on the night of the fifth. The information against S. Ewart was laid on what the lane says."

Nell's hands kept working while she listened. At saw no one they did not slow. At laid on what the lane says they stopped, both of them, flat on the dough, and stayed there.

Wren held out the pencil and the notebook and did not say anything else. It was Nell's to choose. Marked, the leaf took the only name the Varleys had and set it aside on her own word. Unmarked, it was a clerk's notes, and the information stood.

Nell took her hands out of the trough and wiped them on the apron, front and back, slowly, getting it out from between the fingers. She took the pencil and made her cross under the last line, smaller than the one on the wardens' paper and every bit as hard.

"I'll not put my mark to a lie," she said. "Not about his house either."

"No."

"So it's nobody's again."

"Yes."

"And nobody's owes us nothing." She said it as if she were reading it off a board.

Wren let it stand. Nothing she could put beside it was true.

Nell's hands went back into the dough.

* * *

She went up past the shut door without slowing, and round to the front of the Office, and was at desk nineteen a little after three, with the light going yellow down the row. Turning the warrant over, she entered row ten under the ledgers:

The sixteenth · one hour · initialled · N. Varley, informant against S. Ewart, statement taken: she saw no one go in at any door on the night of the fifth; the information was laid on what the lane says. Read back and marked.

Then she drew the information to her, found the box under its printed foot, and set down in it:

Set aside. The informant states she saw no one go in at any door that night (warrant, row ten). W. Aske.

She blotted it and laid it in the wardens' tray for the night runner. Sim Ewart was clear on the Office's paper before four. The lane did not read the Office's paper, and the knots on his neighbours' frames would be there in the morning.

She took the district sheet out of her coat and spread it on the blotter. Rook (?), in her own ink from the seventh, against the three Mill Steps lines: the widow's sight, the lamplighter's winter, the baker's forty years. She dipped her pen and struck it through, once on each line, and the matched column against all three was empty again.

A loss reached the Tally only when somebody brought it in: the one who had paid it, or a complainant under his guild's seal, or a warden with his list. The Office had no other way to see one. It recorded; it did not go out into the streets looking for whoever had been hurt. Whatever Rook paid, he paid where nobody would ever come up the hill to report it but himself.

She blotted the three lines and folded the sheet away.

* * *

The envelope was on the boards inside her door when she let herself in after six, where it had been pushed under. Buff.

For as long as it took her to stand in the doorway, it was the other buff envelope. Wages came at the end of the month, and this was the sixteenth, and she could think of no reason for the Office to send her buff early unless it had stopped waiting for the month to end. She knew what she had done to earn it. She had copied a record out for a man on the river stairs, gone below the street on a dead man's tally, gone out last night with no hour declared, and bought one this morning across the end desk with her rent. Any clerk on the row could have told her what each of those was called.

She shut the door, picked it up and opened it standing.

No letter. There was a chit, a square of stamped card with the clock in its corner and no name anywhere on it, and with it a slip of paper, cut small, in the hand of the signature on the front of her warrant:

For Mr. Teague's line. M. Dunmore.

She sat down on the bed with the two of them.

Mr. Teague's line was hers; she had signed it. The Office doesn't pay for a clerk's doubts, Dunmore had said, so the Deputy Registrar had paid out of her own: out of her own keeping, the way Pell had said, between ourselves, that she was paying for the plot out of her own pocket. Wren had never known whether to believe that, and she did not know now. But it was the same kind of paying. And it was a chit in her keeping, which was all the slip had ever asked of an hour.

How had Dunmore known the tin was empty?

The slips went round to the floor below and came back with initials on them, and in four years Wren had never once asked whose. Either the hand that initialled them took what it read to Dunmore, against one held, yesterday and again today, or Dunmore read the slips herself, before or after somebody else's initials went on. She weighed the one against the other. Neither would settle, and she could not ask which without asking who on the floor below had been watching her hours.

The Deputy Registrar knew where she lodged, too.

She held the slip to the candle and read it again. The line went down a flight below the street, to a ledge with a hank of binder's thread lying on it, and a track in the dust going on down past it, and somebody had just paid for her to go and see what he had been sewing there.

For Mr. Teague's line.
