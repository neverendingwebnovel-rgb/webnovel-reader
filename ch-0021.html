The Tallied City · Chapter 21

If He Asks

On the twenty-sixth Wren came up the last of the stair before the early bell, and the only lamp lit along the row was on the desk opposite nineteen.

Ruddock was ruling. A quire of blanks lay squared at his left hand and the ruled sheets at his right, and the ruled pile was most of a quire already. His lamp had burned long enough to want trimming. He lifted his head when she came along the row and said, "Morning, miss," at about half the size of yesterday's voice, and put it down again to the ruler.

"How long have you been in, Mr. Ruddock?"

"Since the porter took the bar off, miss." A cloth with bread in it sat by his elbow, still knotted. He drew a line, lifted the ruler, laid it again a finger lower. "There was the quire."

On her blotter, square in the middle, lay a sheet that was not the Office's. It was plain white, not buff, and folded twice. No runner had been up the stair yet; the bundles came after the bell. She opened it standing, with her coat still on.

Desk nineteen. W. Aske. To the Deputy Registrar's room, at ten. L. Vane.

It was the hand that had written under row twelve at the top of the house, small and upright, each letter finished before the next began.

"A gentleman left it," Ruddock said. "Before the lamps were up."

She looked across at him.

"I took him for a master. From one of the halls, come early on some business. My father says a master's first into his yard and last out of it, so I thought nothing of it." He held his ruler still. "He'd no ink on him. Not anywhere. I noticed, because I'd been looking at my own." He showed her his right hand. Yesterday's quire had left the side of his second finger grey, and the nail was black at the edge. "He asked was this nineteen. I said I didn't know the numbers yet. He said it was."

"Which way did he go?" Then, because whatever came down from up there had only ever gone back up: "Up?"

"Down, miss." Ruddock pointed the ruler at the stairhead. "I went to the top of the stair to see him down, for the dark. He went past the street door. The dark flight."

In four years nothing had come down from the top of the house to this floor but a sheet slid out under its door. This morning the man behind that door had walked down every floor of the house before light, to her desk, and past it.

He had not gone back up. He had gone on down, under the street, to the door at the foot of the dark flight.

* * *

At the end desk Fenn unlocked the stores book and set out his tray. Pell came in on the bell, looked at the white sheet on her blotter and then at her, and opened his ledger without a word. The bundles came up. Wren entered what they held, matched what she could and carried the rest to the far column, and the morning came round to ten.

She went down to the Deputy Registrar's floor and knocked, and Dunmore's voice said, "Come in."

Vane stood at the window in his plain dark coat, with nothing drawn on over his sleeves this morning, and the chair behind the desk was empty, pushed in. He had not taken it. By daylight he was greyer than he had been under his own lamp, and the coat was worn shiny at the cuffs. On the blotter Dunmore's note lay beside the oath card, and the torn leaf was still under the inkstand, one corner showing.

Dunmore was on her feet behind her desk and clear of it, a hand's breadth of air between her fingers and the wood. She stood straight, heels together, her face up and to the front, the way Ruddock had stood on the top landing yesterday. Wren had never seen her stand so for anyone.

"Miss Aske," Vane said.

He took the note up off the blotter. He did not read it; he held it a little toward Dunmore, so that she could see which paper he meant.

"You'll not read it to him till you can answer for it."

"No, sir."

He laid it down again beside the card.

He looked at the card. "You've both read it. What the line would take, it takes from the sworn clerk first, before the city. You were sworn on those boards yourself, the year you came on Teague's floor. So was I."

Dunmore's eyes went to the card and came up again.

"Did Mr. Teague know?"

"He worked it out."

Wren had read how far he had worked it, on the last of his leaves, in the list with his initials under it and nothing after. She kept her eyes on the window.

Somewhere below the window a cart went down the hill with its brake on, and the noise of it came up through the glass and went away.

"Will you read it to the boy yourself, sir?" Dunmore said.

"It's the Deputy's to read."

"Sworn by the month's end," Vane said, "or there's a letter in his envelope. The floor doesn't carry a hand that enters nothing."

Upstairs the boy was ruling the last of his quire. Wren could see the pile at his right hand without being there.

"He'll not say it without knowing what it is, sir," Dunmore said. "I'll not have him. That I can't answer for."

Vane looked at her for some time. Then he said, "Then he reads it before it's read to him." He let a breath go by. "If he asks."

He turned his head to Wren. "You'll tell him he may ask."

"Yes, sir." She had the words ready behind that, and she let them come. "And if he asks what the line is, sir?"

"It isn't on the card."

Dunmore had put her hand down on the oath card where it lay, flat, the fingers spread. It stayed there.

"Come down, Miss Aske," Vane said.

He went out past her, and she went after him.

* * *

They went down the main stair, past the turn and the street door's grey, and on down the dark flight, with no slip in her pocket and none wanted when the Registrar went in front. His disc went into the slot without his looking for it, and the door gave inward.

He took the lamp off its hook himself and set it on the corner of the high desk. The ledge beside the hook was bare stone.

The book lay open. Near the foot of the page, in the same ink as the sheet on her blotter, stood his line of this morning:

The twenty-sixth · L. Vane · the circle of his bites, · In, a quarter to six · Out, twenty past six.

He dipped the pen and wrote the date and his name under it, drew the circle and the long run of its bites from memory, and wrote In, half past ten. Then he held the pen out to her.

She wrote The twenty-sixth · W. Aske ·, and in the Tally column, in words, as she had once before, none borne; with L. Vane, and her In under his. A page or two back, under the leaves she did not turn, Teague's year of evenings lay where he had left it.

"Sir," she said. "Why did you come down on the fifteenth?"

"On the fourteenth the Deputy wrote up the stair." He took the pen back from her and held it. "A senior clerk's tally was in a junior's coat. She would answer for it."

"Yes, sir."

"I came down the next night to see what a dead man had been at."

"How did you know where to look?"

"I read this book."

The lamp had a draught on it from somewhere in the aisles, and the flame leaned and stood up again.

"A senior clerk below on his own tally is at his work," Vane said. "I read his lines for a year and let him be."

"And mine, sir?"

"A junior on a dead man's tally isn't at hers."

He lifted the pen an inch toward the ledge without going to it.

"There was a bone folder on that. And a hank of binder's thread." He put the pen down across the book's gutter and did not let go of it. "Binding has no business below. I took them up."

"Did you look for anything else, sir?"

"Loose leaves. I went along the second level and the third. There were none."

"And the first book?"

"I didn't take it down. Nobody had opened it in a hundred years."

Teague had known that as well; on the seventeenth she had found his leaves sewn in under its headband.

"How does a man come to sit up there, sir?"

"From up there. Never from the floor."

"Who chose you, sir?"

"The one before me."

He said no more than that, and she did not ask who the one before had been. She looked down at the two lines of his on the page, the morning's and the one still wet, and at her own under them with no circle in it.

"You let me go on," she said. "From the fifteenth. You'd read my line."

"I wanted to see what you'd find."

"And now?"

"Now I'll see what you do with it."

Whatever she did next would be read, in one book or another.

The flame leaned again. Out in the aisles the dust lay on everything as it had lain before any of them came down, and would lie after.

At his table on the twenty-second she had let one question go by unasked. She asked it now.

"Why is the line dearer, sir?"

"It's old."

He looked at her over the book. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of the head of the house at a clerk off the second floor who has asked her Registrar a question, and he added nothing to it.

"And if I tell the boy more than's on the card, sir?"

Vane dipped the pen and wrote Out, five past eleven. He held it off the page a moment and then laid it in the gutter of the book, where the leaves met, and left it there.

He gave her neither leave nor a refusal, nor any word of what would follow.

* * *

At six Ruddock put his coat on, and Wren put hers on when he did, and went down the front steps at his side. The floor was no place for it, with the row listening and Pell watching them go over the top of his ledger, and there was nothing she had to tell him that could not be said in a street.

"I'll walk down with you, Mr. Ruddock."

He looked at her and then down the street, where the lamps were not lit yet. "It's a long way down, miss. My father says nobody from up the hill comes down to us but the wardens and the rain."

"Then I'll come down with the rain."

He took her down the hill by turnings she did not know, off the street at the chandlers' wick and flame and through a passage between two of their back walls, and the ground went lower and the doors went older, their marks worn shallow in the lintels, until the air smelt of tallow and then of the river. On her sheets it was a name far down the column, with its twos and threes. Down here it was washing strung across a passage, a dog asleep on a step, a woman calling a child in, and a mark over every door.

The yard was behind the chandlers', as he had said. A gate of grey boards stood open with the coopers' hoop and adze cut in the beam over it, and inside, staves lay stacked against the wall in their bundles and hoops hung from pegs, large to small. A shed open on one side ran the length of the yard, and in it, under a lamp on a hook, a man was driving a hoop down on a cask. By the gate the finished casks stood two high, and every head had the same word chalked on it: OSTRY.

Ruddock went into the shed ahead of her, took the black pen out of his top pocket and laid it on the bench among the shavings, as a man puts down his tools at the end of the day.

The cooper was broad, and grey at the temples, and he did not stop.

"Sworn, then?" he said, between strokes.

"Not yet."

"Holds the same when it's said." The driver came down, and the hoop went another finger's width down the staves. "This'll be the miss."

"Miss Aske," Ruddock said. "Nineteen."

"Nineteen. We've heard about nineteen." The cooper sighted at her once along the cask, as he would along a stave for true, and went back to it. "He says you match a thing quicker than he can rule a line. I said, so would anybody, he rules like a man ploughing."

Ruddock said nothing to that. Yesterday he would have said a great deal.

The cooper's eye went to the pen on the bench, and back to his hoop. "His mother'll have that in his pocket again by morning," he said.

Wren looked at the casks by the gate. "Ostry?"

"Herring," the cooper said. "They go down to Ostry on the ebb, empty, and come back up full. Ostry's at the mouth, where the Vell gives out into the salt. We've made their casks in this yard since before I was bound." He struck, and turned the cask a quarter on the block. "I went down with a load once, about his age. Thought I'd see something."

"Did you?"

"Saw herring." He struck again. "And they've a bell down there, on a post on the quay. They ring it before anybody works anything. Whatever it is, a fair wind or a sick child or a mended net. They ring, and whoever won't stand for it goes off out of hearing till it's done."

"And whoever stays?"

"Said yes, haven't they." He drove the hoop the last of the way home and laid the driver flat on the cask head. "That's how they had it, anyhow. I was there two days and they rang it once, and I went up the hill out of it with the barge lads. Who stayed, I don't know."

He hung the driver on its nail, wiped his hands down his apron, and went across the yard toward the house. "Don't keep the miss standing in the cold," he said to his son, and went in.

Ruddock walked her as far as the gate and stopped under the beam.

"When the Deputy brings the card up," Wren said, "you can ask to read it first. Before she reads it to you. She'll let you."

He looked at her.

"Read every line," she said. "If there's one you'd not stand under, don't say it."

"Which line?"

She had sworn to keep what was the Office's, and she kept it, though her mouth had gone dry on it.

"It's on the card," she said.

He stood with his back to the yard and the lamp behind him, so that she could not see much of his face.

"Would you say it again?" he said. "If you'd read it first?"

"I've said it."

From the house a woman called his name, the whole of it, loud enough for the street. He said goodnight, miss, and went in to his supper, and did not say what he would do.

On the bench in the shed, among the shavings under the lamp, his mother's pen lay capped where he had set it down.
