The Tallied City · Chapter 17

By the Book

Inquiry of Imbalance: the line to be shown to the Registrar of the Office, at six.

Wren wrote it standing at the end desk on the morning of the twenty-second, with Mr. Fenn's pen, and the purpose ran so long that its last two words went down the margin of the slip.

Mr. Fenn had a paper of nibs open under his left hand and had got as far as thirty. He turned the slip round on his blotter with one finger to read it. His lips went over the lines as they went over his fives, and on Registrar they stopped and stayed stopped, as if the word had more letters in it than it showed.

"There's not much I've not had on a slip one time or another," he said. "Burials. Teeth. A man's sister's wedding, twice." He set it on the pile for the round. "Never that one."

She went back up the row to desk nineteen. The morning's losses came up with the runner in their usual bundle: a ferryman's wife on the far bank who had woken without the feeling in her left hand, which matched to a knot on the wardens' seizure list, and a widow in the lower town who had lost a winter out of her memory, the winter her husband died, which matched nothing. Wren wrote unattributed against the widow, and the district after it, as she had written it against every one before her, and carried the line to the far column. The slip was on her blotter again, initialled, before noon.

The chit that paid for the hour lay in the fold of the warrant in her coat, where it had lain all night, and the hour it paid for was the Registrar's. She turned that over between entries. His chit would go through the slot at the end of the row tomorrow morning, and on the next seventh night back up to him, spent, into his own count.

* * *

At a quarter to six she closed her ledger and went down to the floor below. Dunmore's name was under row twelve, and it would go up the stair at six; she ought to hear that before it went, not after.

The door was shut but not locked, and the lamp on the desk was already lit. Dunmore was at her desk with the hours book open in front of her at the day's page, and nineteen's line stood in the middle of it in Mr. Hesketh's abbreviations, the newest ink on the leaf.

Wren took the summons out of the fold and laid it on the desk beside the book, the right way up for Dunmore.

"It came out under the door last night, ma'am. I'd not got to the turn."

Dunmore read the signature first, and only then the lines above it.

"I've answered to that hand fourteen years, Miss Aske," she said. "Since the day I was made Deputy. In writing, through the slot." She put one finger on the margin of the sheet, clear of the ink. "I've stood on that landing for every clerk sworn in those years, and that door has never opened. I've never had an answer out of it in the time it takes to start down a stair."

She folded the summons on its own folds and held it out, and Wren put it back where it had been, at the foot of row twelve, with the chit.

"Whatever he writes on that warrant comes to this desk after," Dunmore said. "I'll be here."

* * *

She climbed while the six bell was still going, against the clerks coming down, and took the last flight with the sound of them thinning away below her.

This time her knuckles reached the wood. She knocked once, and the door Mr. Sefton had never seen open opened inward, away from her, and a man stood holding it.

"Miss Aske."

He was older than Dunmore, spare and clean-shaven, in a plain dark coat, with black cotton oversleeves drawn on to the elbow. They were clean. So were the fingers on the edge of the door: no ink on them anywhere, not even at the side of the second finger, where every clerk on the floor had it.

"The line," he said, quietly, with no good evening in front of it, and stood back for her.

The room ran the length of the house under the roof, long and low, the ceiling coming down on one side nearly to the boards. One lamp stood on a long counting table in the middle of it, and its light did not reach either end. Inside the door, under the slot, a wide wooden trough stood on the floor with last night's hours heaped in it, stiff cards and soft ones together; two or three had gone over the side and lay where they had fallen. Along the far wall ran a shelf of narrow books, a year to each by the figures on their spines, going back along the wall into the dark further than she could see. There was one chair, pushed in under the table. There was an inkstand and a tray of pens, and an account book lying open, and no blotter anywhere.

She had been sworn facing the outside of this door on her first morning; she was standing on the inside of it now, with the slot at her back, and she noticed that.

She unfolded the warrant and gave it to him as it was, the summons and the chit still lying in the fold where she had left them.

He took it to the lamp and read the back from the top. He went down the rows without hurrying and without leaving any out, stopped at row eight long enough for Dunmore's note against it, did not stop at row eleven, and at row twelve read the Charter's line through and then Dunmore's under it. Below them the house had gone quiet, floor under floor, and there was no sound in the room but the leaf when he moved it and, once, one of the soft cards sliding on the heap in the trough and settling. He finished, and laid the warrant flat under the lamp, and waited.

She had carried one question since the twentieth, and she did not go round it.

"Sir. Is the Office the caster of every loss I enter unattributed?"

"Yes."

It was one word, and he gave it no more than one. Sixteen quarters running she had signed BALANCED under that column, and every line in it had had a caster all along, and the head of it stood across a counting table from her in oversleeves, with her warrant under his hand. The widow's lost winter that morning was the Office's too, and Wren had written unattributed against it.

He took a pen from the tray, dipped it, and wrote under Dunmore's line. Wren read it upside down as it went, which every clerk on the floor could do.

Shown to the Registrar of the Office, at the top of the house, the twenty-second. The line stands. L. Vane.

He did not blot it. There was nothing on the table to blot it with. He held the pen off the paper until the ink had gone from wet to dull, and then he turned the warrant round on the table so that the line faced her.

* * *

The hour was hers till seven.

"Last night, sir," she said. "I was on the landing when the bag went through. It was a long time going."

He looked at her for a moment. Then he turned the account book round on the table to face her, took his hand off it, and waited to see what she would do.

She read it as she read any ledger, from the head down. Across the top of the leaf: Hours returned, carried. Under it four columns, ruled, their heads written in: The week · By the book · In the bag · Carried. The last line was last night's, the twenty-first: By the book, 34 · In the bag, 118 · Carried, 118.

She turned back a leaf, and then another. The week before, the bag stood higher than the book. The week before that, the same, and every week behind it, all down the leaves; and the further back she went, the narrower the gap, a little narrower at each quarter's end. When she came to the first leaf of the year he took the year before down from the end of the shelf and opened it beside the other, and then the two before that, without a word. She went on back. Twelve quarters behind last night the two columns gave the same figure, and so did the week before that, and the weeks before it, the bag and the book agreeing to the card.

The book came up with the bag so that the cards could be counted against the slips; a bag that came up light would be known by morning, and only one man carried it. This bag had come up heavy. For three years somebody had been putting hours into the Office's boxes that no slip had ever spent.

"Who brings them in?"

"I do."

"Bought, sir?"

"At the rate of the day. From whoever will sell."

"And put through the boxes?"

"At night." He moved the lamp an inch so that it stood square to the leaf. "An hour is returned when it goes through a box."

"What for?"

"An hour given back under the stamp stands against the line. What the hours don't cover, the line takes where the Tally finds it."

"Whose silver?"

"The Office's."

"And before three years back, sir? The columns are the same."

"The line has been dearer than it was these three years."

He did not say why it was dearer, and she found she had no question ready for that, and let it go by.

Twelve quarters. Pell's pencil, her own column and Teague's first cut leaf had all started there, at the quarter-day three years back. One buyer who never spent and never sold, buying in hours that only the Office could stamp, and the rate had had nowhere to go but up. The fellow at the fish stairs Jory had told her of, who bought every hour going and was never seen to spend one: she would have put her wage on where his hours went. Vane named no dealer, and she did not ask him to.

The soft cards in the trough had not come off any clerk's floor; they had been bought in from wherever they had got to, and so had the one in her warrant.

Eighty-four over the book, in a week. At two silvers each, which was what Mr. Fenn had taken off her on the sixteenth, that was a hundred and sixty-eight silvers; at a silver a week, more than three years of her rent, gone through the slot in one night.

At the foot of each finished year a rule ran across the four columns, and under it, written small: Settling Day. Carried to the line. The same words each year. She had carried figures to a foot all her working life and never to a line, and she could not see what was done with a year's hours to carry them there. She put it with the things she was keeping.

She looked up from the books.

"What would it do, sir, if it closed?"

"There'd be nothing to keep a working to a hand's."

She had the two sides of it now. On the one, every line she had ever carried to the far column, the widow's winter among them, and Tobin Varley on his step under the Office's back wall. On the other, whatever the city would be on the morning nothing kept a working small. Both of them were Vellmark.

He drew the warrant back across the table and put one finger to row eleven, and read it out. "The last not read." He looked up. "Is that still so?"

"No, sir." She had read them on the eighteenth, on no hour.

"What do they say?"

"They're the Office's records, sir, and they're where Mr. Teague put them." Her mouth had gone dry, and she said the rest anyway. "I'll not carry them up the stair in my mouth. You've a tally that goes that far."

She heard, while she said it, what she had already given him. Not what the leaves said, but where they were: that was in row eleven, in her writing, and he had his finger on it.

At the end of the table, beside the account, where she had not looked while she read, lay a bone folder with its blade toward the lamp, and by it a hank of waxed thread wound in a figure of eight. The last she had seen of them they had been on the ledge on the first level below, on the thirteenth. By the seventeenth the ledge had held nothing but dust, and the admissions book had held a line of the fifteenth, in the hand that had just written under row twelve.

He saw where she was looking. He left them where they lay.

"Then I'll read them myself," he said.

* * *

A little after seven she came down the last flight and the main stair, past the dark of the Loss Room's floor, where the urn on the landing had long since stopped ticking, to the floor below. Dunmore was where she had said she would be. The hours book lay shut at her elbow, and the lamp on the desk was lit.

Wren laid the warrant open on the blotter, the back up, row twelve and what stood under it toward Dunmore.

Dunmore read the Registrar's line under her own. She put her pen down across the shut book, the nib clear of the cover, and left it there.

"There's a row to write, ma'am."

Dunmore turned the inkstand round to her. Wren wrote row thirteen standing at Dunmore's desk, under the Registrar's line, all of it. It ran the width of the sheet and a good way down, in Dunmore's ink, which was blacker than her own, and the pen was too broad for her and she wrote with it anyway.

The twenty-second · one hour · initialled · the line shown to the Registrar of the Office, at the top of the house; read by him, and written under row twelve in his hand. Heard, the Registrar, asked whether the Office is the caster of the losses entered unattributed: "Yes." Seen, his account of hours returned: in the bag more than by the book, every week for twelve quarters; before that, the same. Heard: the hours over the book are bought, at the rate of the day, and posted in the Office's boxes; an hour returned stands against the line; what the hours do not cover, the line takes where the Tally finds it. Asked what closing the line would do: nothing would keep a working to a hand's. Asked what the last leaves of row eleven say: not given; read on no hour.

She turned it round to Dunmore and Dunmore read it, a line at a time. At in the bag more than by the book she stopped.

Vane had told her all of it, and never once said to keep it.

Dunmore drew the hours book to her across the desk. She opened it at the week that went up with the bag, at the first leaf of it, and put her finger to the first line, the first desk's, and began to count: that line, and the one under it, and the one under that, down Mr. Hesketh's week under the lamp.
