The Tallied City · Chapter 23

Off One Knot

The first. The dock warden's return came up on its own behind the morning's bundle, by a warden's runner and marked urgent, in the hand that went through to the back of the paper, and its head ran to four lines:

Article: a wind-cord, three knots, one loosed. Bought at Brede.

Caster: J. Pask, bargeman, of the barge Heron, of Brede; taken at the moorings, the thirtieth, late.

Caster states it was loosed for luck, nothing being known to take in the city.

Payers: eleven, within the reach of the wind; each, the thirtieth, a day of memory, found wanting on waking the first.

Under the head the warden had set them out a line apiece, with where each had lain: the watchman at the fish-stairs lamp; two lightermen asleep aboard their lighter at the foot of the stairs; the mate of the Heron; the boy of the Heron; five of one family aboard a barge lying outside at the moorings, a man, his wife, her mother and two grown sons, one under the other; and last, a woman who split fish on the market's edge, with no barge against her name and no more particulars than where she sat.

Wren went down it name, place and loss, and at the word eleven lifted her pen off the ledger. She had matched a charm to its payer more times than she could count, and it had always been one charm and one payer. The night of the thirtieth had been dead still: she had walked home from the envelope under chimney smoke that went straight up off every roof. Yet a wind had filled sails along the moorings and nowhere else, off a length of Brede twine.

She took the return down the row to the end desk. The row was settling to its morning, pens going into wells, somebody's chair complaining two desks along, and Mr. Fenn was ruling off the month under the stores book's last entries. He finished the line with the ruler held flat before he looked up.

"Mr. Fenn," Wren said. "Your father worked the lighters."

"He did," Fenn said.

"What's a wind-cord?" Wren said.

He held out his hand for the return. She gave it him, and he read the article line and the one under it with the ruler still in his other hand.

"Brede twine," Fenn said. "Three knots in it. You loose one for a breeze and two for a blow, and the third you don't loose, not for anything. They make them at Brede for the upper river, where a man can want a wind and get one." He laid the return on the corner of his desk. "Down here they're for the nail. My father had one over the stove forty years and never got a breath off it."

"One took on the thirtieth," Wren said.

Fenn kept his eyes on the return. He set the ruler down, reached for his ink and put the cap on it, with the month not half ruled off, and he said no more. Wren took the return back up the row.

At nineteen she opened the day's ledger at the clean page. Across from her Ruddock was entering with his mother's black pen, slowly, his blotter going down after every line. She entered the eleven as matched lines, each under its own number, payer and place and loss, J. Pask against each of them, the Heron, the cord with its one knot, and under each, as clerk of the line, the award in words, owed by J. Pask to the one above it. The woman who split fish came eleventh, because the warden had found her last.

Under the eleventh she wrote it out, as she had under the ten before it.

Memory, one day, flat.

* * *

She went down after six by the long way, through the lower town and past the fish market, where they were putting the shutters across, and on to where the hill gave out at the moorings.

The barges lay three deep as before, with a stranger at the inside berth where the Mallow had been. The hoist stood idle on its timber arm, its chain hanging slack, and the mallet that had knocked oakum along the row was quiet. A lantern was coming along the quay toward her at a walking pace, held low, so that it lit the edge of the stone and the boots of the man who carried it. He was a man of middle years in a coat that had had the rain worked into it so many seasons the cloth had gone stiff as sailcloth at the shoulders, and he stopped when he saw she meant to stop him.

"I'm the clerk of the line for your eleven," Wren said. "From the Office."

He lifted the lantern a little to see her by. "Kerridge," he said. "Dock warden. I've been writing up that hill since before you'd a pen to hold, miss, and this is the first time it's come down to read me in person. I'd always heard my sheets went up a stair and came down an award, and nobody up there knew the river from a gutter."

"They do come down an award," Wren said. "I wrote eleven this morning. I'd like to see where they lay."

Kerridge looked along the moorings, then back at her. A clerk who wanted to walk his sheet in the dark seemed to strike him as a thing he had not had before and would not mind having once.

"The cord first," Wren said. "Do you take many?"

"A dozen a year, off Brede boats," Kerridge said. "For the form of it. The ban's after the article, not what it does, so we have them off whoever's carrying, and I never knew one do more than hang off a man's belt." He set off along the quay, and she fell in beside him. "Thirtieth, the river was flat as a slate, and the lamps up on the fish market never stirred. Along here the Heron's sail went out like washing, and three more beside her, and nothing else on the water so much as rocked. She came off the mud against the flood. I was at the stairs' head, a little off from the lamp, and I saw her do it." He shook his head once. "We had him off her deck with the cord in his hand, and he stood there telling me it was for luck."

"For luck," Wren said.

"A load late for the market. So he loosed one. He'd have knocked on the mast for the same reason, if he'd thought of it first." Kerridge's mouth went down at one corner. "He's where we keep them now, till your award's paid or it isn't. He's asked me twice who the eleven are. I told him he knows two of them."

"And the rest," Wren said.

"No payer, no charge," Kerridge said. "So at first light I went along as far as it blew, and knocked." He swung the lantern toward the barges, the dark hulls and the gangplanks laid across between them. "Knocked on hatches, mostly. You'd be surprised what a man will say to a warden before he's had his tea. Most of them didn't know what they'd lost till I asked them what they'd done the day before. Then they knew."

"Show me where each one lay," Wren said.

He showed her. The watchman's stool stood under the one lamp at the head of the fish stairs, with his stick across it and nobody on it yet. "He sat there the whole of the thirtieth, he tells me," Kerridge said, "and can't give me one thing he saw." At the foot of the stairs a lighter rode low on her line with a tarpaulin over her well. "Two of them under that, asleep. They'd been working the night before and slept the day round, and now they've no day to have slept." He took her out along the moorings by the planks laid from deck to deck, holding the lantern back for her feet. Nobody aboard looked up from their suppers to see a warden go over with a clerk behind him. Outermost but one in her tier lay a barge narrower than her neighbours, her sail furled tight to the yard and a warden's chalk mark on her hatch.

"The Heron," Kerridge said. "Pask's mate slept aboard her, and his boy. Pask was up on deck with his luck." He held the lantern toward the hatch and let it fall again. "He never lost an hour of it. They did."

The family's barge lay outermost in the next tier along, with washing strung between her mast and her tiller and a stove going in the stern. An old woman sat beside the stove with a cup in both hands, and as they passed she called to somebody below, asking what she had said to him on the thirtieth, and a man's voice told her, patiently, that it didn't signify.

"She's asked that all day," Kerridge said. "Five of them aboard, and that one's the only one minds."

They came back across the decks to the quay. He did not stop at the end of the moorings. He went up past the top of the stairs, past the watchman's empty stool, to where the quay ran into the fish market's edge, and there on her upturned tub was the woman Wren had seen on the twenty-third, working down the last of the catch with her bucket at her knee. A head went over her shoulder and the dog took it out of the air.

"Her," Kerridge said. "Her bucket was in the gutter at first light, and the dog wouldn't come out from behind the stalls for her. That's how far it blew."

The woman did not look up. She had no thirtieth, and she was splitting fish.

"You'll be from up the hill," she said to the bucket. "He says I'm owed a day. I've told him I'd a whole day of gutting I'd no wish to remember, and if the hill wants to pay me for forgetting it, the hill can." She threw another head. "Nobody's said who's paying."

"A bargeman," Wren said. "Of Brede."

"Brede," the woman said, as if that answered it, and went on splitting.

Wren looked back down the stairs, past the lamp, along the tiers of barges to the Heron's furled sail. From the tub to that hatch was further than any radius she had ever searched for a payer, and the wind had gone every yard of it. The Charter allowed nothing in the city greater than a hand's; on the thirtieth something greater had gone along these moorings, and taken.

Kerridge was watching her, not unkindly.

"Whatever you make of that, miss," he said, "that's for up the hill to say. I only walk it."

He put his hand inside his coat and brought out the cord and held it up to the lantern for her. It was brown twine, an arm's length of it, the loosed knot still kinked into a crook where it had been tied. Beyond it the two tied knots sat pulled tight, small and hard in the lamplight.

* * *

The second. Pell was at the corner where his way came in, and Wren went round to his left without a word, and he fell in beside her.

"Good morning, Mr. Pell," she said, a little louder than the street wanted.

He answered it a step later. "Miss Aske."

The street was opening up for the day, shutters coming down and a cart backing into the bakers' hall's yard. Pell walked with his hands behind him, and for a while he said nothing. A boy went down past them with a tray of loaves on his head, calling them, and a woman in a doorway was beating a mat against the step, and Pell kept his eyes on the clock over the Office's door as it came nearer up the hill.

"The row's had my form," he said. "It's come to a view. Desk nine's grandmother had it that an old house takes its rent off whoever's sat in it longest. The row's taken that up."

Wren looked at him. He did not look back at her.

"It saves them asking," Pell said.

They went up the front steps. On the main stair, past the Deputy Registrar's floor, he spoke again, dry as the rest. "They've a wind on the fish stairs as well, since yesterday's returns. His grandmother hasn't covered that."

The eleven were on the ledger at desk nineteen, and they stayed there.

Wren had carried the Registrar's sheet in her coat two nights. It saved her the asking too, every hour she kept it there. Kept long enough, it would reach the Deputy on the landing, with the masters behind her on the stair, and the first Dunmore heard of it would be a clerk's hand going out for her inkstand. Wren would not be that clerk.

At the stairhead the row was coming in, coats going onto pegs and someone laughing at the urn. She stopped with her hand on the pocket of her coat.

"I'm going down to the Deputy, Mr. Pell," she said. "Before the bell."

Pell looked down at her hand on the pocket, and up again.

"Left side," he said.

She left him at the stairhead and went down to the Deputy's floor.

* * *

The tray stood empty on its table outside the door. Wren knocked, and Dunmore said "Come," and she went in.

The Deputy Registrar was writing by what the window gave her, and Wren's torn leaf lay under her inkstand. Dunmore laid down her pen. She did not ask what brought a clerk of the Loss Room down before the bell. She sat back and waited, her hands together in her lap.

Wren took the sheet out of her coat, opened it out, and set it on the blotter in front of her.

"It was in my envelope on the thirtieth, ma'am," she said. "With the coin and the slip."

Dunmore read it. It was ten words, and she took longer over them than they needed. Then she turned the sheet over. The back was blank, plain white to the edges. She turned it face up again and laid it down square on the blotter.

"Who else has seen it?" Dunmore said.

"Nobody, ma'am," Wren said.

"Did you ask him for this?" Dunmore said.

"No, ma'am," Wren said.

Dunmore looked at the inkstand.

"It's this one," she said.

She took it up off the leaf. Wren had not seen it lifted since the twenty-fourth. Under it, the torn leaf, held flat a week and more, rose a little along its stitched edge, where it had come out of the notebook, and stayed curled there. Dunmore held the inkstand a moment over the blotter, by its base, at the height of a hand. Then she set it back on the leaf, square, and the stitched edge went down under it.

"That's all there is to it," she said.

She pushed the Registrar's sheet back across the blotter with two fingers. "It's yours," she said, and Wren took it up and put it in her coat.

"What will you do with it, Miss Aske?" Dunmore said.

"There were eleven on the fish stairs on the thirtieth, ma'am," Wren said. "Off one knot."

Dunmore held her look a moment, then lowered it to the leaf under the inkstand, and raised it again. Out on the stair someone went up two at a time, late for the bell.

"He's named you to the inkstand," Dunmore said. "He's named nobody else to the masters. Those are mine to answer for, from the front steps up. I take them up the stair, Miss Aske. Whatever's done with that inkstand, I'll be on the landing to see it done."
