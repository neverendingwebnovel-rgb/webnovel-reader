The Tallied City · Chapter 14

Under the Signature

The first of the quarters' books came off the wall a little after the early bell, and by ten there were four of them on desk nineteen, two open, two shut under her left elbow, and a clean district sheet between them in her own hand. Nobody asked what she wanted with three years of ledgers. The clerk at the end desk shifted his stool to let her reach the shelf, and the next time she went up for a book he had shifted it already.

It was her own return she was checking, at her own desk, in the working day. She took every unattributed line on this bank, twelve quarters of them, district by district as the ledgers gave them, and passed over whatever lay across the Vell. Then she wrote the districts out again, not in the printed order but by how far their streets lay from the hill: the nearest at the top of the sheet, the river's edge at the bottom. It was slow. The tea came round at ten and she drank it with a quarter open in front of her and her finger keeping the place.

By the middle of the afternoon the sheet was done, and it came out as his had. Mill Steps stood at the head of the districts. Eight, in twelve quarters. The lower town and the docks sat far down, where the hill had flattened into the river, with their twos and threes.

She did not know how many people lived on Mill Steps. A great many more than forty: bakehouse after bakehouse down the back of the hill, a family behind every door. Eight was a great deal for one district. Among so many, it was not the head of the column.

Teague had kept the Office's own losses apart, at the foot of his. She wrote them at the head.

Nearest the hill of all was the hill. The Office stood on top of it, and the ledgers carried its losses as they carried any street's, because the Deputy Registrar's forms went into the Tally like any other loss. She had read past them a hundred times on her way to the next district. Now she went back through the twelve quarters for nothing else, and found them where they had always been, one to a quarter where there was one at all, entered by desk number and not by name.

desk thirty-four · the sight of the left eye · form, unattributed.

desk six · a season of memory · form, unattributed.

desk twenty-seven · the sense of smell · form, unattributed.

desk thirty-one · four years of life · form, unattributed.

desk nineteen · remainder of years · form, unattributed.

The last was dated the fifth.

She wrote the Office's own address above Mill Steps, and beside it the figure: five, in twelve quarters, against forty desks. Once in a long while, Dunmore had said. Five in three years was not a long while. Against forty, it put the head of the column on the floor she was sitting on.

She looked up.

Thirty-four, six, twenty-seven, thirty-one. Four desks along the two rows, a clerk at each, pens going at three of them; the fourth clerk was reaching for his blotter. The fifth desk was the one under her elbows.

A list kept by desk number was the kind of list somebody kept who meant to go on down it. She thought of the stranger's line in the admissions book below, and looked at her own sheet instead of at the rows.

The warrant said she must find the line that would not close, and show it. She had found lines enough. Shown to whom, and in what words, nobody had ever told her.

She took the warrant out of the drawer at her knee and opened it face up across the count. She had not read the front since the tenth. Opened, it would not lie flat; the quarters stood up off the desk in a shallow roof, and the lowest crease, which had ridden against her hip every day since, had worn as soft as an old cuff.

She read it all, top to bottom, past what she remembered, to Dunmore's name. Under the name, in type smaller than anything above it, a single line ran across the bottom crease, worn so thin along the fold that she had to tilt the sheet toward the window before the letters would come up out of the paper. On the tenth she had taken it, if she had seen it at all, for the printer's line.

Given under the ninth article of the Charter of the Office of the Tally, as entered at the head of the Office's first book.

The first book: I, on the paper label. She had had it in her hands two nights running, and both nights she had opened it from the back. The disc that opened the third door had gone onto Dunmore's ring last night, to hang with Dunmore's disc.

She tried to hear the word Charter said aloud on this floor. By Pell, who said everything. By Teague across the two desks. By Dunmore, by the porters, by anyone at the urn at ten, in sixteen quarters of tea. Nothing came. Her inquiry stood on the ninth article of a thing she could not remember anyone naming, and it was written under the signature that had opened it.

She put the twelve books back on the wall herself, one at a time, each in its own gap. The ledgers stayed where the Office kept them; the sheet was hers. She laid the warrant face up inside the sheet, folded the sheet once round it, and did not put either in the drawer.

* * *

A little before six she went down the row to desk twelve.

Pell was blotting his last line of the day. He looked up when she stopped at his desk and not before. Yesterday he had come up the row to her, and she had sent him back down it.

"Mr. Pell," she said. "If the hour's still not for sale, I'd—"

His hand was already in his inside pocket. It came out with the pocketbook and the card together, and he slid the card off the pocketbook with his thumb and put it into her hand, and the pocketbook went back where it had been. The card was warm from a day and a night against him. Hers now; nothing on it said otherwise.

She drew breath to tell him what it was for. He lifted his hand an inch off the desk, palm toward her, and set it down again, and she let the breath go. It was not that he didn't care what she did with it. He would sooner not be the man who had known.

"Mr. Pell. Have you ever read the Charter of the Office?"

He took his time. He always did when a question was worth something.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske, I've spent thirty years turning back to the front of every book on this floor that would open," he said. "I never met it at the front of any of them."

He looked at the folded sheet under her arm, and for once in all her quarters on the floor he looked like a man who would like to ask. He shut his ledger instead.

"Good night, Miss Aske."

On her way back up the row she took a slip from the end desk and wrote it standing, the purpose in her smallest hand:

Inquiry of Imbalance: to read the Charter of the Office, at the head of its first book.

She did not leave it out to be taken round. Mr. Hesketh would cut it down to his own short form, and the Deputy Registrar would read it tonight as one line among the day's. Wren wanted her to read it whole, from Wren's hand.

* * *

She reached the bakers' hall after six. It stood halfway down the street the front steps looked down, set back behind a yard, with the wheatsheaf cut deep in the stone over its door. The warrant put the Charter in the Office's first book, and the first mark struck under Settled. in that book was this hall's. A hall that had put its mark to a thing might have kept its own copy, and a copy in a hall would be no record of the Office's and want no key.

She had no hour declared. She could ask; she could enter nothing. That would do for a question.

A boy was stacking trestles against the yard wall. He looked her up and down and pointed with his chin at a door off the yard. "Mrs. Orme keeps the books."

The room behind it was small and warm and smelt of the yard's last bread. A woman in her fifties, sleeves pinned back above the elbow, was ruling a page at a high desk under a shelf of books in canvas covers. She finished the rule and looked up.

"Mrs. Orme? Wren Aske, from the Office of the Tally."

"Then you'll want the register." Mrs. Orme had already turned on her stool toward the shelf, where a fat book stood at the end with its spine worn pale from being taken down. "Whose mark is it?"

"Nobody's, ma'am. I'm asking whether the hall keeps a copy of the Charter of the Office."

Mrs. Orme turned back. "A charter."

"The Office's. It would be an old one."

"We've the Schedule." She said it kindly, as to someone a little lost. "Every hall has the Schedule. We've the hall register there, and the working register, and the day-books and the dues. I never heard of a charter in my life." She tapped the edge of her desk. "If it's the Office's, the Office has it. The Office keeps its own. It always has. We keep the hall's, and nobody's had cause to muddle the two."

The working register lay open at her elbow, at the month's end page. Against T. Varley a rule had been drawn in pencil, light, ready to be inked. Wren looked at the shelf.

"What does the hall do on Settling Day, Mrs. Orme?"

"Eats," said Mrs. Orme, "mostly." But she got down off her stool and took two books from the shelf, one in good canvas and one in canvas gone brown at the edges, and opened the newer on the desk between them.

It was the hall's day-book, a line or three to a day in a dozen different hands: a dinner and who had paid for it, dues in and dues owing, an apprentice bound, a journeyman buried. She turned to the year's end and put her finger on it.

Settling Day. The master to the Office.

Nothing after it. She turned back a year, and back another. The same line, in another hand, in a third. Mrs. Orme opened the older book and Wren found it there too, in ink gone the brown of the cover.

"That's as far as we go," Mrs. Orme said. "Near a hundred years. Anything older went to the damp."

"What does he do there?"

"He goes up the hill in his chain at first light, and he's back for the hall's dinner." Mrs. Orme closed the older book and smoothed its cover with her palm. "What he does up there I couldn't tell you."

"Has nobody asked him?"

"You don't ask the master what he puts his chain on for," Mrs. Orme said. "You brush it." She slid both books back into their places on the shelf, and patted the newer one level with the rest, and that was plainly how she liked it.

Three hundred years of masters, then, since the hall first set its mark in an Office book: up the same street on the same morning in their chains, past this yard and up the front steps, and down again in time for dinner. She did not say any of it. To say it would be to tell a hall what was in an Office book.

"Thank you, Mrs. Orme."

"Mind the yard," said Mrs. Orme, already ruling her next line. "They've had the trestles out."

* * *

She went back up the street with the clock in front of her the whole way, in at the street door, and up the main stair to Dunmore's floor.

The door was shut tonight. There was light under it. She knocked.

"Come."

Dunmore was standing behind her desk with her coat on, buttoned to the throat. The hours book lay on the blotter, shut. She had read it. There would be no line in it for nineteen today either.

"Miss Aske."

Wren laid three things on the blotter in front of her, in order. The slip. The warrant, turned to its front, with her finger under the line in the fold until she was sure Dunmore had seen where it was. Her own district sheet, opened out.

An evening below, after six, would cost her nothing and let her enter nothing; only a declared hour would put what she read on the warrant. The inquiry stood under this Charter, so reading it was the inquiry's own work. The Office's law, not a dead clerk's pages. And no way to that book that did not go through this desk.

"One declared hour, ma'am, at the head of the first book," she said. "Whatever it says goes on the back of the warrant in my hand. For that I need Mr. Teague's disc off your ring. Or you, with your own."

Dunmore picked up the slip first and read it. She set it down and took up the warrant, and tilted it toward the lamp, as Wren had tilted it toward the window, until the worn line came up out of the fold. She read it twice.

"I've signed one of these in my life, Miss Aske," she said. "This one." Her eyes went up to her own name, and down again. "I could answer for every word above my signature."

She put the warrant down and drew the district sheet to her. Her eyes stopped on the top line, the Office's own address and the five beside it, and stayed there. Then they went down past the edge of the desk to the drawer beneath it. She did not open it. Wren knew what lay at the top of the stack in there: five forms in Dunmore's hand, his the first of them. Dunmore had stood at the edge of his grave.

"Those five desks," Dunmore said. "How many of them have a clerk at them tonight?"

"All five, ma'am."

Dunmore said nothing to that. Wren kept her hands at her sides. Whatever stood at the head of the first book, Dunmore would have to answer for knowing it, to whoever read what her desk sent up, and so would the clerk whose slip had asked.

Dunmore laid her hand flat on the slip, over the purpose line, and kept it there. The keys at her waist hung still on their chain, the long one and the ring, Teague's disc and her own on it together. Wren took up her sheet and the warrant. The slip stayed where it was.

Dunmore did not reach for her pen, and she did not take off her coat.
