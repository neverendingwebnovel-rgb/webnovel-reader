The Tallied City · Chapter 20

Not With You

The twenty-fifth began with somebody at her old desk.

Wren saw him from the head of the row at the early bell, before she had her coat off: a young man in the chair opposite nineteen. Mr. Fenn had been there before him. A tray of nibs stood on the desk, and a ruler had been laid square beside it, and that was all there was on a desk that had stood empty since the fifth.

He got up when she came down the row, and kept standing.

"Ruddock," he said. "Mr. Ruddock, I'm to say, they told me at the door. I'm from the lower town. My father's a cooper, hoops and staves, the yard behind the chandlers', you'll not know it. The Deputy's to fetch me up this morning to be sworn. I was told to sit here till she did, so I'm sitting." He was not.

He was nineteen or near it, with a collar so new it stood off his neck, and a red line already rubbed along the skin above it. A pen lay across the top of Fenn's tray that was none of Fenn's, black and new and too good for the desk. His mother had bought it him, he said, when he saw it had been seen. He had not used it yet. He was waiting for something worth the ink.

Wren hung her coat and sat down at nineteen. The first bundle had not come up. Down the row the floor was doing what it did before the bundle, which was uncapping ink and not talking, and Ruddock had not yet learned that the not talking was part of it.

"My father says it's the same as being bound apprentice," he said, across the two desks. "You say it the once and it holds you all your days. He was bound at twelve, in the same yard he's in now, and he says he's never once been unbound, not even when the old man died, and that's how you know a thing's been said properly. What do you remember of yours, miss?"

"What would be in my envelope at the month's end," Wren said.

He laughed, too loud for the floor, and looked down the row to see whether anyone minded. Pell, at twelve, turned a page. Ruddock sat down.

"It's a thing, though," he said, lower. "To be in here. Three hundred years since the city voted the magic out, my father says, and the Office came in the same day to count what it cost." He lifted his chin on it, as if some of those years were his now. "My mother's told the whole street. She's told the chandlers twice." He looked along the wall of ledgers. "Is it right they go back to the first day?"

"They do," Wren said.

He nodded at them, slowly.

Dunmore came up the row from the stairhead. She had a card in her hand, stiff and white, held by its edges so as not to mark it, and Wren saw the heading on it before she saw anything else: capitals with spurs on them, the old face of the Settling leaf, the same face that stood backwards in the little frame on Lusk's rack.

"Mr. Ruddock," Dunmore said.

He was on his feet before she had finished the name, and the chair went back against the desk behind and he caught it and set it straight and said he was sorry to it, or to her.

It was all the Deputy said, and she did not look at nineteen.

Dunmore had read the words on that card aloud a line at a time to every clerk sworn in her Deputyship, Wren among them, and each had said them after her on the landing and signed nothing. For as many years the same woman had kept her inkstand on the foot of the Settling leaf and never read what lay under it. Words read aloud and never signed could hold as much as a foot line.

Wren had put her pen down. Ruddock went up the stair after the Deputy, a step behind.

* * *

She got up.

The row saw her go. Pell's head came up at twelve; at the end desk Fenn stopped with a nib between his fingers. Nobody spoke, because there was nothing in the custom of the floor to say to a clerk who got up at the early bell with no paper in her hand and went the wrong way. She went out past the urn on the landing and up the stair above the Loss Room, turn after turn past the shut doors, and up the last flight behind them close enough to hear Ruddock breathing through his mouth. The boards at the top were the Deputy's and the sworn clerk's this morning and nobody else's, and she came up onto them.

Dunmore had him facing the door. His hands were at his sides, as somebody must have told him to hold them. She stood at his left with the card lifted to the light that came up the well, and her mouth was already open on the first line. Nobody had come up the stair behind Wren on her own first morning, and the boards had not creaked under anyone else.

"Ma'am," Wren said. "Read it before you read it to him."

Dunmore looked round at her. Whatever her face had to say, she did not say it. She looked down at the card instead, and her eyes went down it.

At the sixth line the card came down an inch. At the last it came down to the height of her waist, to the height of the slot, and hung there.

Then she held it out to Wren. "Read it."

Ruddock looked at the two of them, and back at the door.

The card was heavier than a chit. The ink stood up off it, and she could feel the letters under her thumb.

THE CLERK'S OATH.

I take my desk in the Office of the Tally,

and I will keep the Tally true:

every loss entered as it is brought, and none made.

What is the Office's I will keep in the Office.

I stand under the line, before the city;

what the line would have of the city, let it find first in me,

until my account is closed.

On the floor a clerk's line was the one she entered and answered for, the clerk of the line, and a clerk who heard I stand under the line heard that; Wren would have heard it so four years ago if she had been listening and not reckoning her rent. She knew the other line now, run across the first leaf of Volume I with the Office under Caster and the city under Payer, wheresoever the Tally shall find it, and the card had each clerk ask it to find first in me. Teague's Staff. list held the Office's own losses, and among them four of his own never brought in, the oldest from the year he came on the floor and was sworn; in twelve quarters, while the line got dearer, five desks below her feet had paid. On the fifth he had written remainder of years and left the matched column blank, because he had known the caster and there was no column to write it in. He had said where to look for who pays: first in the queue. She had sworn herself into it on these boards with her mind on the buff envelope, and so had Pell.

"Is something wrong with it?" Ruddock said, low, to her. He had turned his head from the door without turning the rest of him, and the collar had cut a fresh line under his ear doing it.

Wren gave the card back to Dunmore. She could have read him the sixth line. It was his as much as anybody's, and in a minute he would have been saying it. She could have told him what Teague had written on the fifth, and why, and he would have gone down the stair with it and sat opposite nineteen with it all his days.

"Not with you," she said.

Dunmore took the card. Her thumb went over the face of it once, where Wren's had been. "Go down to your desk, Mr. Ruddock," she said. "You'll be sworn another morning."

He looked from one of them to the other, and the questions came up into his face all at once, and he asked none of them. Then he went, slowly, because the treads were narrow. They heard him reach the turn, and the next, and then the stair took the sound.

Nobody knocked. Nothing came out through the slot or from under the door, and Dunmore did not raise a hand to it. Wren had sworn to that door, and so had Pell, and it had been told nothing this morning and gave no sign of wanting to be. Down in the press room Lusk would be expecting the card back by the runner, for his stove; a card that said that was no card to keep in a press-book tied with string.

Dunmore put the card inside her coat.

* * *

Ruddock was at the desk opposite when Wren came back down to the floor, sitting straight, with his hands on the desk and nothing under them. Fenn came along from the end desk with a quire of blanks, laid it in front of him, showed him with one finger where the ruling went, and went back. Twice in the morning Ruddock lifted his head as if to ask her something, and put it down again. The bundle came up. Wren entered what was in it and carried each line where it went, and her hand was steady all the while, and she watched it be steady.

Toward noon she went down to the floor below the Loss Room. She had no paper to bring and nothing she had been sent for, and she knocked on the Deputy's door anyway, and was told to come in.

The leaf she had torn out of her notebook lay flat on the blotter, the stitching holes along its edge, and Dunmore's inkstand stood on it.

Dunmore was on her feet behind the desk. She asked before Wren had said what she came for.

"What was in his last leaves, Miss Aske?"

"A list, ma'am," Wren said. "Headed Staff. The Office's own losses, a line to each, back further than you've had that drawer. Every one form, unattributed."

Dunmore waited.

"And four of his own he never brought in. The oldest is the year he was sworn." Wren kept her hands still. "Under them he left a line ready. His initials, and nothing after."

Dunmore's hand went to the front of her coat, where the card was, and stopped there.

She did not ask anything else. She bent and unlocked the deep drawer and drew it out to its stop. The block of forms lay as Wren had seen it on the sixth, Teague's on top, the red of the stamp on it gone darker already; under it her stamp went down a long way before the writing changed to another Deputy's, and went on down after that. Dunmore put her hand flat on the block, the fingers spread, as though it might lift if she didn't.

"I've been stamping receipts," she said.

The hand did not move. Wren did not look away from it. That hand had brought the red down on every form of hers in the block, and between them had gone up the stair with a card in it to read the next clerk onto the floor.

"Have you written up the stair about any of this before, ma'am?"

"Once." Dunmore took her hand off the forms and pushed the drawer home. "The fourteenth, about the tin. I said I'd answer for it, and I did. In writing, through the slot."

The fourteenth; and on the next night L. Vane stood in the admissions book below the street, in at a quarter to eleven.

Dunmore took a half-sheet from the rack. She wrote standing, the pen going once into the inkstand where it stood on Wren's leaf, and blotted it, and Wren read it across the desk as it lay.

To the Registrar of the Office. The clerk taken on this morning is not sworn. I read the card first. I will not read it to him until I can answer for it. M. Dunmore.

* * *

By late afternoon Ruddock had ruled most of the quire.

He had no other work. The afternoon's bundle went to nineteen and to the desks either side, and none of it to him, and he ruled. He ruled with one of Fenn's nibs, his mother's pen still in his top pocket, and at the end of each sheet he looked across at the lines Wren was entering and then down at his own, which had nothing between them. He had stopped talking some time after noon. Wren found she missed it.

Pell came up the row to nineteen himself, with no paper in his hand to account for it, and stood at the corner of her desk.

"He'll not enter a line till he's sworn," he said, low, as if she had asked. "Fenn's given him the blanks to keep his hands busy." He looked at the boy. "I saw him come down this morning. Knees still good." Something moved at the side of his face that was nearly a smile. "I came down from there remembering my waistcoat. I couldn't have told you a word I'd said."

The windows had gone dull and the lamps were not lit yet, and down the row the floor had come to the slow part of the afternoon, when a clerk could look up from a line and not be the only one. Wren put her pen in the tray.

"Did you ever lose something you didn't bring in?"

Pell did not answer at once. Across the aisle the ruler ticked against the desk and moved down a line.

"The taste of oranges," he said. "Nine years back. The winter quarter-day; I've it pencilled, the rate and the day, because I write them down. I'd an orange that evening, and it was cold and wet and nothing else." He considered it. "Only oranges. A lemon's still a lemon. Very particular, I call it."

"What did you do?"

"Ate the rest of it. It seemed a waste not to." He turned the corner of her blotter square with one finger. "I've not bought one since."

"You never brought it in."

"And have a form with desk twelve on it?" Pell said. "Red stamp by the box, and the row reading it at the urn before the tea's poured." He shook his head. "It was only oranges. Nobody else was going to miss them."

She kept the sixth line of the card behind her teeth, and it ached there.

"If you lose anything else," she said, "bring it in."

Pell looked at her, and then at the boy ruling his blanks.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske," he said, "I'd sooner not be paperwork."
