The Tallied City · Chapter 7

Not Credited

The eleventh's chit went through the slot before anyone had lit a lamp. That was the river stairs paid for: one hour, Rook on his knees in front of Tobin Varley, and every word he had said there. Five left in the tin under her bed.

She did the first of the morning's entries with her coat still on, because the stove had not caught yet, and she had got as far as a scalded foot on the far bank when a runner came up the row and stopped at the end of her desk.

"Deputy Registrar, miss."

The warrant was in her drawer, folded in four. She did not know why she took it, except that her hand went to the drawer before she had decided anything, and she put it inside her coat with the rest.

* * *

The floor below was quieter than the Loss Room and smelt of floor polish instead of ink. Past the stairwell the narrow table stood outside Dunmore's door with its tray, empty. Wren knocked and was told to come in.

Dunmore was at her desk, writing, and did not ask Wren to sit; Wren had not expected her to. She finished the line before she looked up.

"You have the warrant."

"Yes, ma'am."

"May I see it."

It was not quite a question. Wren took it out and held it a moment longer than she needed to, and Dunmore pressed her lips together.

"It's my name on the front of it, Miss Aske."

Wren gave it to her. Dunmore unfolded it, turned it over without looking at the front at all, and read the back.

She read it the way a clerk reads, which Wren had not thought of her doing: down the first column and then across, row by row, with no finger and no hurry, as if each line had to be passed before the next one was let in. The Varleys on the sixth. Jory on the seventh and on the eighth. Kit on the tenth, marked. Rook to Tobin Varley on the eleventh, in Wren's hand from yesterday. Dunmore said nothing. Somewhere above them a chair went back on the boards of the Loss Room.

Dunmore laid the sheet flat and put one finger on the fourth row.

"Retaken," she said. "Saying what?"

"That a man came up from the river stairs late on the night of the fifth, ma'am, with a hooded lantern, and went in at a door near the top of Mill Steps, and came out before midnight. The lamplighter didn't see his face. He knew him by his walk." Said aloud in this room it sounded thinner than it had on the lane. "Read back to him and marked."

The finger moved down one row. "R."

An initial was no use against the Deputy Registrar with the sheet under her hand. "Rook, ma'am. A hedge-worker. He's about the river stairs."

"Two of these put a man on Mill Steps on the night of the fifth," Dunmore said. "A named man. Your lamplighter marked it." She lifted her finger off the sheet. "A loss. A name. A witness who put his mark to it. Why hasn't he been referred?"

"He says the cost was his own, ma'am."

"Is that on here?"

"No, ma'am."

"Then it isn't anywhere."

She said it without weight, the way she would have said a column was short. Dunmore could not refer him herself; the baker's line was in Wren's hand, and the hand that opened a line closed it. She could only ask. She had asked.

"As it stands," Dunmore said, "the Varleys are owed nothing. They get written down. Name a caster and the Schedule owes them." She looked at the sheet and not at Wren. "It isn't much, the Schedule. It's more than written down."

Wren said nothing. There was nothing on the desk she could have said it with.

"Settling Day, Miss Aske."

Dunmore folded the warrant along its creases and rose to hand it back across the desk, and as she leaned the chain at her waist swung out and struck the desk's edge. Iron keys, a long one and a ring of small ones. Among them, threaded on the same ring, was a small brass disc no bigger than a thumbnail, stamped with the clock. Its edge was notched. There were more bites in it than in the one in Wren's pocket, and they were cut deeper.

Wren's hand closed on her own disc inside her coat.

Dunmore set the warrant in Wren's other hand, sat down, and took up her pen. "That's all," she said, and went back to the line she had been writing.

* * *

The slip took her less time than the walk back up the stair.

Two hours, against five held. Purpose: Inquiry of Imbalance, warrant attached.

The end clerk took it without a word and sent it round. Two was a great many out of five, and she had known since the stair that it would be two, because there were two things to be done and neither could be done in the other's hour. While the slip was gone she finished the scalded foot and did the rest of the morning's entries, a lost sense of smell in the lower town and a day of memory matched to a charm on the seizure lists, and she checked each one twice. Soon after the noon bell it was back on her desk, initialled.

The referral index was at the far end of the ledger wall, where it had always been. This time what she read there would stand.

The drawer marked R ran out stiffly. Behind the last guide card the single card was where she had left it, soft at the corners, in the upright, careful hand she did not know. Around her the floor went on with its afternoon: pens, a cough, a drawer shutting somewhere down the row. Nobody at the index looked twice at a clerk with a card in her hand.

She read it standing, every line.

Spell: a broken arm set whole, river stairs below Mill Steps, witnessed by a warden. Caster: the hedge-worker called Rook, taken in the act. Payer sought: none found in the Tally within radius or time. Caster states the cost was his own. Not credited. Closed: no loss on the Tally to answer for.

Caster states the cost was his own. The same words were in her notebook in her own pencil, under Rook's Mine, and here they were in another clerk's hand, eleven years older than hers.

She took out her notebook, opened it at the back and tore out a clean leaf along the fold.

The card named no one but Rook. It said a warden and not which warden, and it named nobody on the lane, so there was nobody in it a copy could warn him against and no street it could set on anyone; the harm the rule was there for was not in this card. It was a breach all the same. She took out her pencil.

She copied it at the end of the index with the drawer open, the date at the head and then every line in the order it came, Spell and Caster and Payer sought, the line that was already in her notebook, Not credited, Closed. Her hand was smaller than the clerk's. It took less than the ten minutes she had allowed. When it was done she read it against the card, line by line, and found nothing to correct.

She slid the card back behind the guide card and pushed the drawer home. The leaf she folded in half and in half again, until it was the size of a chit, and put it in her coat pocket with the razor and the disc. She did not write down anywhere that she had made it.

* * *

By the time she reached the top of Mill Steps the bakehouses were well into their afternoon and the lane was warm with it.

No smoke came from the Varleys' chimney. The wheatsheaf was still on their door, though the working register was the list of hands who could work the trade and sell under the guild's mark, and a man who could not knead came off it; the mark only said who lived behind it. Three doors down, through the open half-door, Wren could see the back of Nell Varley at another man's bench, the apron strings tied twice round. Nobody on Mill Steps had a desk in the Office to buy an hour across, the way Pell had bought the end-desk clerk's last year, at the rate of the day, before the rate began to climb.

She went on down to where the cobbles turned to steps.

* * *

Jory was halfway down the river stairs with his tray on his knees. He saw her at the top and did not look away, which was new, and when she reached his step he moved the tray an inch, as if to make room for her, though there was room already.

"Kit's been up and down the lane," he said. "Telling people."

"Telling them what?"

"That he put his mark to a paper. At the Office." Jory picked at the twine on a knot. "He's proud of it. He would be. And now they ask me." He kept his eyes on the water, on the brown run of the Vell below them and the far side. He did not look up the stairs at all, toward the lane, and Wren saw that he was taking some care not to. "When the Office is coming for Rook. They ask me, because I know him. Like I'd have it written down somewhere." He turned the knot over. "Is it?"

Wren had the warrant against her coat and the leaf in her pocket, and Dunmore's voice in her ear, and she did not know.

"I can't tell you that," she said.

He nodded at the river, as though that were what he had expected the river to say.

"Is he coming today?"

"Don't know." Jory put the knot back on the tray. "I told him there's a clerk asking. He comes when he comes."

She went on down.

* * *

The last dry step was empty. Below it the stone went green and the Vell came up it and drew off and came up again, and there was nobody on the waterline in either direction. She stood with her back to the wall and her hand in her pocket, round the folded leaf, and let the hour go.

It went. A skiff came downriver, well out, and did not come in. Gulls worked the far bank. She could not see the clock from here, but she could count, and she had counted a good deal more than half the hour away when there were boots on the wet stone along the waterline, and it was Rook, his right hand in his coat.

He looked at her and then at the empty step beside her.

"Clerk."

She held out the leaf.

He took it in his left hand. He sat down on the step to do it, and opened it against his knee, one fold and then the other, and read it slowly. Then he started again from the top. On the second reading his lips moved, very slightly, on two words near the foot: Not credited.

"Neat," he said.

He folded it along her creases and put it inside his coat.

"The boy," she said. "The ninth. In your words."

She opened her notebook on her knee, at a page of its own, and wrote it as he gave it to her, which was not much: that at dusk on the ninth, on the river stairs, he had closed a boatman's boy's palm, torn on a hook; that the cost was his own. He said nothing he had not said before, and she meant to write nothing he did not say. Then she read it back to him line by line, with her finger under each one, as she had for Kit on the lane.

He stopped her at the last line.

"Not his own," he said. "Mine."

She crossed out the two words and wrote the one above them, and read the line again: the cost was mine. He nodded.

"Then put your mark to it."

She gave him the pencil. He took it left-handed, the right staying where it was, and set the notebook flat on his knee. He made a short level stroke, with a shorter upright stroke standing under the middle of it. He did not press. It did not come through to the leaf below. He gave her back the pencil.

"And the fifth?" she said.

Rook looked at her for a moment.

"You bought the boy," he said.

He got up and left her the step.

Wren closed the notebook on the mark. Tobin Varley's hands came into her mind, lying on his knees on this step yesterday. A man who could close a torn palm had turned them over and laid them down. Whether nobody could give a year back, or nobody could pay what giving it back would cost, the ledgers had never said.

* * *

She came back up the stairs and the lane to the Office with a little of the second hour still left in it, and at desk nineteen she uncapped the ink and unfolded the warrant.

The twelfth · first of two hours · initialled · read, referral index: the card under R, eleven years old, in its words: "Spell: a broken arm set whole…" and on, word for word, to the card's last line.

The twelfth · second of two hours · initialled · statement, river stairs: R., hedge-worker, of the ninth, at dusk, a boatman's boy's palm torn on a hook, closed by him; "the cost was mine"; read back and marked.

She blotted them both and read down from the top. Seven rows. Two put him on Mill Steps on the night of the fifth, Kit's with its mark and her own with his words in it. One, his own and marked, said a boy's hand was his cost. Nothing on the sheet said what he had done on the fifth. The row she had paid a breach for did not go near it.

Dunmore's question still stood. So did Then it isn't anywhere. It was not true any more. It had stopped being the point.

* * *

By a few minutes past six the Loss Room was empty. Pell had gone ahead, his muffler on before the bell had finished, and she went down the stair alone, past Dunmore's floor and the turn.

At the ground floor she did not stop on the last step. She went on down.

The extra flight was steeper than the rest of the stair, and darker with every step of it, and colder; the street door's light came round the turn behind her and gave out halfway. She stood at the bottom on flags that were not worn in the middle, close enough to touch the door, and did not touch it. The clock in the lintel was over her head. The brass plate was at the height of her chest, and the slot in it was a thin black line.

She took the disc out of her pocket. Not since the hill had it been in her hand.

She held it up beside the plate, flat, edge to edge with the slot, not in it. It was the width of the slot. If it went in at all, it would go in notched edge first, and the bites would be the last thing to show.

She thought of Dunmore's chain swinging against the desk, and the disc on it with its deeper bites. She thought of Teague coming up these steps at night grey to the elbows.

It was after six. There was no slip behind her and no initials on one, and whatever she found below this door tonight, she could not write a word of it on the back of the warrant. She had paid for that lesson once.

She put the disc back in her pocket. The door stayed as it was. She climbed up into the light and went out under the clock.

* * *

The tin came out from under her bed with five in it. Three, once tomorrow's two went in for the hours she had spent today.

She sat back on her heels with the lid in her hand.

Somewhere along the waterline, in a coat with one hand always inside it, was a page in her handwriting with the Office's words on it, and the Office had a name for that.

She put the lid back on the tin.

It was a good copy. There was not a word wrong in it.
