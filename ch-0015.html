The Tallied City · Chapter 15

The Head of the First Book

Dunmore did not come up to the Loss Room. Her papers came up, and her runners with them, and the floor had not forgotten the two days she had come up herself: the afternoon Mr. Teague died, and the morning of his effects. So when her step came off the stair a little after the early bell and turned along the row between the two lines of desks, the pens went quiet ahead of it, a desk at a time. At desk twelve Mr. Pell's stopped in the middle of a stroke and stayed stopped.

She had the slip in her hand. She laid it on desk nineteen, face up, beside the morning's first entry, and took Wren's pen out of its stand without asking for it. She bent and initialled the slip at the foot, where Mr. Hesketh's initials would have gone, and beside the hour she wrote after six. The nib was too fine for her hand. The D came out thin as a hair.

Wren looked at the slip and not at her. An initialled slip went down to Mr. Hesketh's book like any other, and this one would go with the Deputy Registrar's initials on it, put there in front of the whole row.

"It was given under my name, Miss Aske," Dunmore said. "I'll see what I gave it under." She put the pen back in the stand. "I'll bring my own disc. You bring the warrant."

"After six, ma'am."

Dunmore went back down the row at the same pace she had come up it, and the pens took up again behind her as they had stopped, one desk after another, Pell's last of all; for a while after the rest he sat looking at his own page with great attention and writing nothing on it. Nobody came up the row to ask. Twice before tea the clerk at the end desk found a reason to walk the length of it and back, and twice he looked at the corner of Wren's blotter, where nothing lay now, and went on. The slip was sent down with the morning's papers. The day's losses went into their columns until six as if it were any day, a year of life off a carter across the Vell, a month of memory in the lower town, the sense of taste by the docks, and she matched the carter's and carried the other two to the far column.

* * *

Dunmore was on the further flight before her, a step above the door, with the disc already off her ring. She fitted it, and the door gave, and inside she took the lamp off its hook herself and carried it to the high desk.

She dipped the pen and wrote The twentieth · M. Dunmore ·, laid her disc on the page and drew its bites round it, then wrote her In. She turned the pen and held it out, handle first.

Wren had nothing to lay on the page. The printed head over the columns said what the third one was for. She would not draw bites she did not carry, and a gap where they ought to be would read as bites forgotten, a line somebody would come back to. She wrote The twentieth · W. Aske ·, and in the Tally column, in words, none borne; with M. Dunmore, and after it In, twelve minutes past six. Of all the lines on that page, and on every page she had turned in that book, it was the first with no bites in it.

Five lines above her own stood the line of the fifteenth. Her eye crossed it on the way down. She had asked about it once and been given the tin for an answer, and she did not ask again.

Dunmore said nothing about the book. She went down the middle aisle with the lamp, along the track, and Wren walked in the back edge of her light. At the second door, and at the third where the stair turned, Dunmore's disc went into the slot and the door gave for it as it had given for Teague's, and neither of them said anything about that either.

On the third level the air was dry enough to catch at the back of the throat. The stool stood at the bay. Volume I lay at the bottom of the shelf where Wren had left it on the eighteenth. She drew it out with both hands and sat with it across her knees, and Dunmore stood at her shoulder with the lamp held low.

She had opened it twice, both times from the back, at Teague's leaves. She turned it over now. The front board was hard as horn under her palm, and she lifted it.

The leaf inside was black.

Not the paper. The paper had gone the yellow of everything else down here. The ink was black, as black as ink in a well filled that morning. She turned two leaves, then three, to where the founding year's accounts began, leaves she had come to before from the other end, and there the old hand had faded to a pale rust-brown, nearly the colour of the paper. She turned back. Black. Three hundred years of ink stood along the Loss Room wall, and none of it had kept like this. She held the leaf nearer the lamp, and it stayed black, and she let it lie.

Across the top, in the old hand:

The Charter of the Office of the Tally, given in hall by the city of Vellmark, and entered at the head of the Office's first book.

Given in hall. The Schedule's front leaf had the same two words for the masters who had assented to its rates.

Under the heading the leaf was ruled in four columns, and their heads were printed, the letters squat and worn into the paper:

Working · Caster · Payer · Closed

One line ran across them.

Under Working: The quiet of Vellmark: that no working within the city be greater than a hand's. To stand from Settling Day to Settling Day.

Under Caster: The Office of the Tally.

Under Payer: The city of Vellmark, wheresoever the Tally shall find it.

Under Closed the column was ruled down its whole length, and there was nothing in it.

Wren read it as she read any line. She had read ten thousand set out like it, one before tea on a slow morning and twenty on a bad one. A working. A caster, with a name. A payer. It was a matched line: the kind she was paid to make and made least often.

The caster was the Office, whose stamp was on every chit she had held and whose ledgers she had kept since the morning she came on the floor.

The payer was the city, wherever the Tally found it, and the Tally found it every morning: in the lower town and across the Vell and on Mill Steps, a year of life here and a sense there and a day of memory flat, and each time with no caster beside it, so she wrote unattributed and carried it to the far column. Every one of those lines had a caster after all. Forty-one in her column this quarter. Forty years off a baker in one night.

Nothing in Closed. She ran her eye down the empty column to the foot of its ruling and found no date, no initials, no word at all. Somebody had ruled it with care, the line straight and even all the way down, for something that was never to be written there.

Behind her the lamp did not move. Dunmore had seen the columns; she said nothing.

This was the line that would not close.

She went back up to the working. That no working within the city be greater than a hand's. Every casting she had ever met on paper had been small enough to hold in one: an arm set whole, a lucky-catch charm, a boy's palm closed on the river stairs. Nothing larger had ever come across her desk, and now she had the reason the city was quiet, entered as a line.

The articles began under the line in a smaller hand, numbered, and ran over onto the next leaf. She read all of them while the lamp hissed. Most were what any office might set at its head: that it keep the Tally of every loss in the city, that the wardens bring it what they found, that its books close on Settling Day.

The fourth she read twice.

Fourth. Hours of quiet to be stamped by the Office and by no other; and the hours returned to be carried.

At the end of the founding year, among its last outgoings, the same words stood over the cat's milk: Hours returned, carried. Here they were before there had been any outgoings at all. And the same word as the working above. The quiet of the city at the top of the leaf; the hours of quiet four articles down. Every hour she had ever held had been the Charter's before it was hers. Carried, the article said, as the old leaf said, and it said no more than that leaf about where.

From Settling Day to Settling Day, the working stood, and the founding year had closed on Settled. She read on.

Ninth. Where a clerk of the Office shall sign that the Tally will not close, the clerk shall find the line and show it to the Registrar of the Office, on or before Settling Day; or withdraw.

Dunmore had bent over her shoulder to the lamp. She read the ninth under her breath, so low it was hardly a voice, as far as show it to the Registrar, and there she stopped, short of of the Office, and read no further.

So that was who the warrant's show it meant: the Registrar of the Office, by Settling Day.

A working's cost fell heaviest nearest the working. This book lay under the hill, under the Office, and over it, up through the stair and the floors, sat the forty desks. Nearer the hill, more.

She closed the front board over the black leaf and put the book back at the bottom of the bay.

* * *

At the high desk Dunmore set the lamp on its hook again, and Wren laid the warrant open beside the admissions book, its back up, and found the foot of row eleven. The pen was the one Dunmore had used for her line. Wren wiped the nib on the edge of the well and dipped it.

She wrote all of it. It had been read in a declared hour, at the head of the Office's own first book, and reading it was what the hour had been for. To leave any of it off would make it the one kind of line she had never written, one that was less than what was there.

The twentieth · one hour · initialled · read, the Deep Archive, the head of the first book: the Charter of the Office of the Tally, entered as a line. Working, the quiet of Vellmark. Caster, the Office of the Tally. Payer, the city of Vellmark, wheresoever the Tally shall find it. Closed: not closed. Article nine: the line to be shown to the Registrar of the Office, on or before Settling Day.

Row twelve took more of the sheet than any row above it. She blotted it and turned the warrant on the desk so that it faced the lamp, and stood back from it.

Dunmore took it slowly, a finger's width at a time, and Wren watched her get to Caster and go on past it without stopping. When she reached the end she held out her hand for the pen. Wren gave it to her. Under the row Dunmore wrote Read with the clerk. M. Dunmore.

Wren knew the weight of it: a name under a line made the line that name's word, and nobody could say afterwards the clerk had written it up herself; the Deputy Registrar's hand stood on the same line as hers now, and it would go wherever the warrant went.

"Who is the Registrar, ma'am?"

"The one I answer to."

Dunmore gave her back the pen. She looked at the wet ink of her own name and not at Wren.

"It's your signature on the return, Miss Aske," she said. "I've put mine under what you read. Taking it up is yours." She drew the admissions book toward her. "By Settling Day. That's the end of it."

They wrote their Outs, Dunmore on her line and Wren on hers, Out, ten past seven, and went out, and the door barred itself behind them. Pell's hour was spent. His card would go through the slot in the morning, and there would be nothing in the tin behind it.

* * *

Tobin Varley was on his step with a cup held between the heels of both hands, since the fingers would not go through the handle. The only light on that stretch of the lane came up from the bench three doors below, where somebody was still at the dough, and it reached as far as his boots.

Wren had come round from the front of the Office and down from the head of the lane, and he watched her the last few steps without getting up. She sat on the step below his.

"Nell wants the chandler's slate squared by Settling Day," he said. "She asked me how long that gives her. I said I'd ask up the hill. You keep the year up there."

"The end of the quarter."

"That's what the handbills say." He drank a little. "I'd hoped you'd know better than a handbill."

She did not answer that.

"I'll not ask what you've been at," Tobin said. "If it was anything for this step you'd have had it on your face at the top of the lane."

The warrant was in her coat, folded inside her sheet, and on its back in ink not an hour old was the name of his caster. Under the Schedule a named caster owed its payer, and this caster was the Office of the Tally, which paid nothing to anyone. What stood on a warrant was the Office's record and did not go down a lane in a clerk's mouth; the ninth article said to whom it was to be shown, and that was not a baker on his step.

"Mr. Varley," she said.

He waited. The light from below lay across his knuckles.

"It's cold to be sitting out," she said.

"It's cold in," he said. "Out here there's the street going by. In there there's me."

Nobody was going by. Down at the bench below, the dough slapped onto the board and somebody there said a word to someone else, and a laugh came up the steps and was gone.

He had asked her once to have his age written down somewhere. Tonight she had written down what it was he had paid for, under the city's name, on the back of an Office warrant, and that was the one thing she could not read back to him, though she could have said it on this step in a breath, and he would have known where to look when he looked up.

He did look up, after a while, but only at her.

"You're welcome to sit," he said. "You've sat. I'm only saying."

"Thank you, Mr. Varley."

She stayed where she was. Above the topmost door the Office's back wall stood over the head of Mill Steps, and along it the one row of windows that was the Loss Room's was dark from end to end. Tobin held his cup in the heels of his hands and drank from it a little at a time, and she sat on the step below him until it was empty.
