The Tallied City · Chapter 13

By the Middle

"His own pages, ma'am," Wren said.

She had the other answers ready too, the smaller ones that were true as far as they went, and she let them go. She had gone down in Dunmore's hour, on Dunmore's chit, and what she had seen in it belonged on the row.

"The ones out of his ledger. He sewed them into the back of the first of the Office's ledgers. It's on the third level, at the end of the track, lying flat in a bay."

Dunmore looked down the middle aisle to where the track went into the dark at the head of the stair. She did not take the lamp off its hook again.

"Have you read them?"

"The first leaf. Not the last."

"Write it here."

Wren took the warrant out of her coat and opened it flat on the high desk beside the book. The archive's pen was stiff and the ink thick; she wrote under row ten by the lamp, with Dunmore watching the nib and not her hand:

The seventeenth · one hour · initialled · seen, the Deep Archive, the third level, at the end of the track: in the first volume of the Office's ledgers, leaves of H. Teague's cut from his ledger and sewn into its back. The first read: unmatched — by district, the quarter-day three years back, sixteen. The last not read.

Dunmore read it through. Then she looked at the admissions book, once, at the stranger's line that stood between Wren's two.

"Bring it to my desk tomorrow," she said. "With the warrant. Before I go."

Wren did not have to ask what it was. The disc was in her coat, against the razor.

"It came onto that paper at my desk," Dunmore said. "It comes off it there." She buttoned her coat to the throat. "I'd sooner it was on my ring than in your coat the next time somebody reads this book."

"Yes, ma'am."

Dunmore's hands were empty. She could have held one out across the desk, and Wren would have put the disc into it, and that would have been the end of the Deep Archive for desk nineteen tonight instead of tomorrow. She didn't hold it out.

Wren wrote her Out, five past seven. Dunmore wrote hers on her own line, took hold of the iron ring and went out, and the door swung to behind her on its weight and barred itself.

The ledge beside the hook was as bare as it had been an hour ago. Whoever had lifted that hank would know the same thread on a spine, and sooner or later they would come down to look for it. And tomorrow: Wren went up the stair with the word. Dunmore had said it as she might say a date she had set herself. Or as a clerk passes on a date somebody above her has set, and doesn't say so.

* * *

Dunmore's chit went through the slot of the box at first light, and by the noon bell desk nineteen looked like any desk on the floor: the ledger open, the morning's entries matched or carried, her pen going. The corner of the blotter where a slip had lain squared on the warrant most days since the sixth was bare, and the warrant stayed in the drawer. Nobody came up the row for anything.

At a little after three Pell did.

He came with nothing in his hands that she could see, stopped at her blotter, and laid something on it face down. She knew it before she turned it over, an hour with no more name on it than Fenn's, hers once it was in her pocket. The card was stiff, the edges still sharp; it had never been bent in a purse.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske," Pell said, "you told me on the fifteenth you had one left. You've written a slip every day since, and there isn't one today." He took his hand away from the card. "It's not for sale. I'm not buying."

He had turned to go back down the row before she found anything to say. He did not mean to be thanked, or to be told what it was for.

"Mr. Pell."

He stopped.

One hour, tonight, declared. She held the card between two fingers and it weighed nothing. She could write the slip now, have it on Mr. Hesketh's desk before four, go below after six with Teague's disc still in her coat, sit on the stool in the bay and read the margins to the last word with the candle as close as she liked. And every word would go on the back of the warrant in her hand, because that was what a declared hour was for. The warrant went to Dunmore's desk tonight. From Dunmore's desk it went wherever Dunmore answered.

She put the card back on the blotter and pushed it across to the edge nearest him.

"You like to know you could leave, Mr. Pell," she said. "Keep knowing it."

He looked at the card, and then at the shut drawer at her knee, where the warrant was. He had sat on this floor thirty years and heard everything said on it.

"I'd thought it was the hour you were short of," he said. "Between ourselves, I see it isn't." He picked the card up and put it in his inside pocket, with the pocketbook. "Good afternoon, Miss Aske."

He went back to desk twelve and did not look up the row again before six.

* * *

When the floor had emptied she went down past the ground floor and opened the door at the foot of the further flight with Teague's disc for the last time.

At the high desk she wrote:

The eighteenth · W. Aske ·

and drew Teague's bites, the disc laid on the page beside them, and In, ten past six, in her own hand.

She knew what the line was: an admission with nothing behind it in Mr. Hesketh's book, as anyone who set the two books side by side would see, but Teague's year of lines had nothing behind them either, since no clerk bought hours by the year at more than a week's rent apiece, and nor, she reckoned, had the stranger's of the fifteenth. An evening below was the bearer's own, and this book said who had come down and when, not what they had opened, which only the dust had kept, whole over Volume I until Teague; it was the warrant's work that had to be paid for, and tonight she would enter nothing, so she would owe nothing, and nothing she saw could go on it.

She signed because the book asked for the bearer's own hand, and she would not leave a lie in it, not even a missing one.

She took the track down. The third door gave to the disc as it had the evening before. At the bay the stool stood where it was, and Volume I lay on its own print.

She sat on the stool this time and turned to the back of the volume.

The old leaf lay where it had lain, the long sloping hand gone pale. Last night she had passed over the others on her way to the cat. Tonight she read them, and among them, a line she had passed over above the Milk:

Hours returned, carried.

Beside it, in pencil, a tick. Small and quick, with a hook at its foot. She had watched that pencil go down the margins of Teague's ledger for four years. It was the only mark of his on that leaf. No account was named against the line, no figure, no word of where.

She thought of the padlocked box on its stool at the end of the row, and of every chit she had put through its slot, the one this morning among them. Carried. Carried where, the line didn't say.

She turned the leaves on to the week of the twenty-eighth and held the candle close.

The pencil ran down the outer edge first. It was the districts again, in his small hand, but not in the order the returns list them. Mill Steps stood at the head. The lower town and the docks came lower down, among names she knew from the returns, and the order had nothing to do with the printed sheet: it went by how far each lay from the Office's hill, the nearest first. Beside each he had put his three years in one sum, and the sums were larger toward the top. Over the column, a single pencilled line:

Nearer the hill, more.

She had sorted her own sheet by street since the sixth. She had not once thought to sort it by how far a street lay from the hill.

At the foot the pencil turned in toward the sewing, and there was a heading, underlined once:

Staff.

Under it, one to a line, losses. Not names: desk numbers, as the floor knew its clerks. Each line had a quarter-day, the loss in a few words, and after it the same two words every time: form, unattributed. The quarter-days went back decades. She knew those forms. There was a block of them in the deep drawer at Dunmore's knee, and the top one was his.

Over twenty years back by the quarter-day against it:

desk thirty · remainder of years · form, unattributed.

The words he had used for himself on the fifth.

Four of the lines had no desk number. They had his initials instead, and they had no form after them. He had never brought them in. A loss reached the Tally when somebody carried it there, and these he had carried nowhere but here.

H. T. · the hearing of the left ear.

That was the oldest of his. She reckoned the quarter-day against it: forty-one years back. The year he had come on the floor. For four years he had turned his right side to her whenever she spoke to him across the two desks, and she had thought it was his way of listening.

H. T. · the taste of salt.

H. T. · three days of a spring.

H. T. · the names of the streets I was born in.

And the last line of the list, on the last leaf, in the week of the twenty-eighth:

H. T. ·

Nothing after it. No loss, no form. A line left ready.

Wren sat with the candle and did not move it. The floor she worked on, forty desks. The block of forms in that drawer, every one with its box stamped red. It happens in this building. Once in a long while. The desk numbers going back down the years, a line at a time, as far as the pencil went, and his own four among them with—

She stopped there. She made herself stop, as she would have stopped a clerk adding a column before the figures were in.

She copied nothing. They were the Office's records, cut out of an Office ledger, and they would stay where he had put them. But every figure on these leaves had come out of the Loss Room's own ledgers, and every desk-numbered loss on that list had gone into the Tally like any other loss, on its form, in its quarter. They were all upstairs. She could count them again herself, at desk nineteen, by quarter and by street and by how far each street lay from the hill, and that was her own return and her post, and nobody's warrant. She would carry the method up the stair. The record could stay down here.

Before she laid the volume back she put her thumb to the shelf beside it and rubbed the dust into the new thread at its head, round and round, until the pale was gone and the turns were the same brown as the headband under them and the shelf around them.

Then she put Volume I down square on its own print and went up the track, and wrote her Out, twenty past seven.

* * *

Nobody had sent for her. She went up the stair to Dunmore's floor with the warrant in one hand and the disc in the other.

The door was open and the lamp lit. On the desk, turned toward Dunmore's chair, lay a long narrow book ruled across, a line to a slip. Wren knew it upside down. Today's page was short. There was no line on it for nineteen, and Dunmore's finger was resting at the foot of the day's lines, where one would have been. She had been looking at the place where it wasn't.

Dunmore's eyes went to Wren's cuffs. Wren did not look down. She knew what was on them.

She laid the disc on the blotter.

Dunmore drew up her chain, found the ring of small keys and worked it open with her thumbnail. She threaded Teague's disc on beside her own and let the ring close. Two discs on one ring now, the lesser and the greater, and when she let the chain drop they touched once and hung still.

"The warrant."

Wren gave it to her. Dunmore found row eight and wrote against it in her own hand, a short line, and her initials: Returned to the Deputy Registrar, the eighteenth. M. D. She read row eleven again below it, and then she looked up.

"Have you seen anything, Miss Aske, that isn't written on this?"

"Yes, ma'am."

Dunmore held her eyes. The lamp ticked. Downstairs a door shut somewhere and somebody went out into the street.

She did not ask what.

"Then it's nothing I'll be asked to answer for," she said, and held the warrant out across the desk.

Wren took it.

* * *

Rook was on the river stairs a little short of the bottom, sitting sideways across a step with his back to the wall and his boots drawn up out of the wet, and he did not turn his head when she came down out of the end of Mill Steps. It was late. The tide was in and quiet.

"Clerk."

"I've no hour," Wren said. She sat on the step above him. "And no key, as of tonight. There's nothing I could put on paper if I wanted to."

"Never stopped you before."

She let that go. She had come down the hill with a question, and it was not about Teague. Teague she kept.

"When you say a cost was yours," she said, "how do you make it yours? The caster never pays. You told me you did. How?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he would get up and go. Then he said, "You kept the top door off your paper."

"It's still off."

"Most up top say things." He looked out at the water. "There's nights I take it out and have to sit and think what it's for. That's new." He said it as he might have said the step was wet.

He moved for the first time then. His right hand came out of his coat.

It was pale, paler than the rest of him, and stiff; the fingers did not close all the way. They were not holding as fingers hold. They were set round the thing they held as a clamp is set, and the thing was a beam: iron gone dark, about the length of his hand from the heel to the fingertips, a plain bar with a small empty eye at each end where pans would hang, and none hung. At the middle, where his hand had it, a boss worn bright. Tobin's beam. She had carried it in her notebook in his words, no pans to it, and she had heard it knock under that coat.

"Whoever's got it by the middle when the working's done," Rook said, "says who pays."

"And you say—"

"Me."

Wren looked at the bar in that hand. "You could say anyone."

"Anyone." He did not smile. "That's why it lives in there."

"Where did it come from?"

"Older than your Office. I had it off somebody who had it off somebody."

"Who?"

"That's theirs." He turned the beam a little in the clamp of his fingers. "Who made it I couldn't tell you. It does the one thing."

He took it out of the right hand with his left, which took some doing, and held it out to her. She took it by the middle because that was where his hand had been.

It was cold, and heavier than it looked, and it did nothing at all. Nothing was being worked. It was a bar of old iron in a clerk's hand at the bottom of a lane.

Payer sought: none found in the Tally within radius or time. A clerk had written that eleven years ago, and three days ago she had written the same nothing into row nine, and here was why. She believed him. She found she had believed him since the boy.

And something that could say who pays, and a list of desk numbers in a dead man's pencil, going back—

She gave him the beam back.

She did not ask him whether there was a bigger one. She was not ready for either answer.

Rook returned the beam to the right hand and the right hand to his coat. When the hand came out again it had her copy of his card in it, folded to the size of a chit. He opened it to the back. His own pencil stood there in its big capitals going uphill: ASKE. CLERK. UP TOP. SHE WROTE IT OUT.

"Write it under," he said. "What it does. In case."

They went up the steps to where the lane's last lamp reached them. She sat with the leaf on her knee and wrote it in her own small hand under his, his words and not hers:

The beam. Held by the middle when I work. Whoever holds it says who pays. I say me.

Then she read it back to him a line at a time, with her finger under each, as she would have read a statement back for the Office, and he listened to every line as if he might need it later, which he might.

"That's it," he said.

She gave him the pencil. He took it left-handed and set the leaf flat on his knee and made his mark under the four lines: a short level stroke, and a shorter upright standing under the middle of it.

She had watched him make it under the statement that became row seven, and taken it for a mark.

It was the beam, held by the middle.
