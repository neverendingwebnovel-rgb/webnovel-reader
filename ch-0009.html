The Tallied City · Chapter 9

Cut to the Grade

The runner came down the row before Wren had her coat off. He was a boy she knew by his boots, which were a size too big and announced him from the landing, and he stopped at desk nineteen with the look of someone who had been told to fetch and not why.

"Deputy Registrar wants you, miss. With the warrant."

She put her arm back into the sleeve she had half slipped out of. A faint grey still showed in the weave of the cuff from the night before, and she tucked it under as she went.

The tray on the narrow table outside Dunmore's door held a cup this morning, untouched. Dunmore did not look up until Wren was at the desk.

"The warrant, Miss Aske."

Wren gave it to her. Dunmore turned it to the back without a glance at the front and laid it flat on the blotter.

"I sent for you about your hedge-worker," she said, and began to read.

Row six was the card under R in its own words. Row seven was the man himself saying much the same under his mark, and at read back and marked Dunmore's mouth went thin, as though she had an answer ready for it and was saving it. She went on to row eight, and did not come back up.

Somewhere down the hill a cart took the corner badly and someone swore at the horse.

"Where did you come by a tally, Miss Aske?"

"In his tin, ma'am. Under the peppermints." Wren kept her voice level. "You gave it to me at the arch of Saltmarket Yard."

Dunmore said nothing. This was not the silence of a woman waiting to hear more. She looked at the sheet for a while, at nothing on it in particular, and then she put her hand to her waist and drew up the chain, past the long key, and turned the ring of small ones until the disc came round. She put it on the desk with one finger on it, the way a clerk holds down a slip in a draught.

Wren took Teague's out of her coat and put it beside hers.

Side by side there was no mistaking them for a pair. Hers had more bites, cut deeper, round more of the edge.

"They're cut to the grade," Dunmore said. "His was a senior clerk's."

Wren waited.

"Mine was cut when I was made Deputy. When a clerk dies it comes back with his keys. It isn't cut again. Not for anyone." She glanced at the smaller disc. "His keys came back. It wasn't on them. I thought it had gone in the ground with him."

So the bites were not a pattern at all, but a rank, and the deeper set belonged to the woman in front of her. The lesser of the two had already been through two doors in Wren's coat.

Dunmore's eyes went back to the last row. "W. Aske entered under it, the tally drawn." She read it out flat, the way she would have read a district's name. "You signed your own name."

"His wasn't mine to write, ma'am."

That was the plain truth, and Wren had not thought of it as anything else. The book asked for the bearer's own hand and then trusted it to be one. Anybody holding the disc could have drawn the bites and written H. Teague under the twenty-eighth, and the book would have taken it without a murmur, and it would have been a lie in a clerk's own hand, which was the one kind of line the Office had never made a column for.

"Have you been down, ma'am?"

"Once. The day it was cut. I signed the book and I came up."

"Who reads it?"

"I've never asked."

Wren had supposed somebody did; she had not supposed the Deputy Registrar would not know who.

"You went down on a dead man's tally," Dunmore said, "and wrote it on my warrant."

"The tally opened the door, ma'am. The slip's what put it on there."

Dunmore looked at the two discs for a long moment. Then she let her own fall back on its chain, where it knocked once against the long key. Teague's she picked up between finger and thumb and set down on the warrant, on row eight, and she pushed the sheet and the disc together back across the desk.

"I'd sooner it went down on a slip than sat in somebody's pocket," she said. "Everything you do with it goes on the back of this. All of it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"I'll answer for the tin."

Wren took up the warrant, and the disc with it. Dunmore had already reached for her pen, and then she did not pick it up.

"What was he doing down there, Miss Aske?"

The question was not put as an order. Wren had asked herself the same thing on the stair last night, and on the walk home, and once in the dark with her boots off.

"I don't know yet, ma'am."

Dunmore looked at the bare place on the blotter where his disc had lain, a while longer than there was anything there to see.

"And the hedge-worker," she said.

"I mean to find whose door it was, ma'am."

Dunmore picked up the pen. "Then find it," she said, and was writing again before Wren reached the door.

* * *

She wrote the slip before she had properly sat down: One hour, against two held. Purpose: Inquiry of Imbalance, warrant attached. She meant to take it after six. She pinned the warrant behind it and left the two of them squared at her elbow for the runner, and then she opened Teague's ledger at the gap.

She had looked at the stubs often enough, but never beside a date she trusted. They ran from three years back, and the page after them was written in the week of the twenty-eighth, his last night below. Whatever had been on the cut pages had ended there, and not a day past it.

She sat with the book open until the stove behind her ticked as it caught, and then she shut it.

Pell came up the row a little before ten. He did not stop to ask what the Deputy Registrar had wanted, which in Pell was as loud as a question. He had his grey Schedule in his hand, and he laid it on her blotter open, turned back to the very front, and kept two fingers on the cover so it would not spring shut.

"Between ourselves, Miss Aske," he said, "everyone reads the rates. Nobody turns back to the front."

The front leaf was printed in the old heavy type, heavier than the title on the cover, with a rule above and below. The rates herein, as assented by the masters of the guilds, in hall. Under that was a line of revision, with a date in it that nobody now living had been alive for.

Wren read it twice. No master would put his hand up in hall to raise what his own journeymen might one day be ordered to pay, and so nobody had ever asked, and nobody was going to.

Pell took the booklet back, closed it on his thumb and went back down the row to desk twelve. If he had seen against two held on the slip at her elbow, this time he let it lie.

By the time she next looked up, it was on her blotter, initialled.

* * *

Her hour began at the head of Mill Steps, a little after six, with the light going brown over the roofs and the lamps coming on up from the river end.

Kit Morrow had got as far as the middle of the lane, where the stairs gave way to the slope, and she went down to him past the Varleys' door. He lit the lamp there in one movement and lowered the pole, and saw her, and wiped the hook on his trouser leg as if she might want to inspect it.

"Evening, miss. Are you after somebody?"

"I'm after you. I want you to stand where you stood on the night of the fifth, when you saw him. And show me what you could see from there."

He looked pleased, then careful, then pleased again. This was her hour, and what he showed her now she could set down as seen, not only as said.

Kit had no need to lead her anywhere. That was the one, he said, the lamp he had been trimming that night because it smoked. He stood under it with the pole grounded and his back to the wall, the way he must have stood then, and turned his face up the lane.

"He came up past me. From the stairs." His voice went down on stairs and came up again on its own. "I never looked round proper, not till he was above me. Then he was up there."

"Which door?"

"I didn't see which, miss. I told you." He lifted the pole and held it out at arm's length up the lane, the hook a little above the line of the doors. "But not theirs. There's the lamp outside the Varleys', and he was in the light of it, and then he was past it. I'd swear to that. Above it. One of them."

Wren followed the hook. Above the Varleys' door there were four more on that side, the last of them the topmost on the lane, standing under the Office's back wall so close that its roof seemed to lean on it.

"One of those four," she said.

"Could be the top one. It's the top one or it's one of the three under." He brought the pole down. He was looking at her, not up the lane, and he had not looked at her quite like that before. "Is it him, then, miss? Are you coming for him?"

She gave him no answer. She thanked him, and he took the pole and went on up the lane to his next lamp, and twice on the way he looked back.

* * *

She walked the four doors from the fourth up.

At the fourth, the third and the second the ovens were going for the night's bake, and the men at the half-doors gave her a word apiece and nothing else. Nobody at any of them remembered the fifth for anything but going to bed.

The topmost was not a bakehouse any longer. It had the wheatsheaf on it, worn down nearly to the grain of the wood, but what came off its chimney was thin and pale, more steam than smoke, the breath of a washing copper and not of an oven. Washing hung on a line under the eave, sheets that were not all one household's, and a card tacked beside the latch in a careful hand: B. Hollin. Rooms. Washing taken in. Somebody had pasted SQUARE YOUR SLATE BY SETTLING DAY on the frame, over an older bill whose corners still showed round it like a margin.

A child sat on the step. She was perhaps five, in a pinafore too short for her, with her sleeve pushed up past the elbow, and she was looking at the inside of her own forearm the way a child looks at a thing she has been told to leave alone.

A patch of new skin lay there, as long as Wren's thumb. Pinker than the rest of the arm and shinier, it was smooth all over, with no scab on it, no pucker, and nothing at its edge where it met the old skin, no line or ridge or whitening. It looked like the skin under a blister that had never been one.

Wren's eye went to it and stayed. She had entered four years of what things cost. A burn closed and left a mark; a cost was paid and left a payer. In the Tally she had seen a great many hurts, and never one that looked as though nothing at all had been paid for it.

The child held the arm up, the way children show things.

"It doesn't hurt now," she said.

"What doesn't?"

"The kettle." She considered the patch. "I'm not to scratch it."

"Who said?"

The child did not look at Wren. She looked at the door.

It opened before Wren could ask anything else. The woman in it had her sleeves rolled and her hands red from water, and she took in Wren's coat and the warrant's corner at its pocket in one look, and put the child behind her skirt with her free hand before the look was finished.

"Yes?"

"I'm from the Office," Wren said. "The night of the fifth. Late. A man came up the lane with a hooded lantern and went in at a door up here."

"That's ours," the woman said.

Her eyes went once down the lane to the Varleys' door and the knots hanging in their clusters on the frames either side of it. Behind her skirt the child said something, and the woman said, "Posy. In," without looking round. Then she shut the door.

Wren did not knock again. A knock would not make a statement out of a woman who did not choose to give one, and there would be nobody's mark under anything. What she had heard was her own, in her own hour: two words, and the door shut on them.

* * *

She went back to the lamp Kit had lit at the head of the lane and stood under it. The hour was nearly spent.

Kit had finished his round while she was at the doors, and there was no pole on the lane now. Four doors down from the lodging stood the one chimney on Mill Steps with no smoke going up from it.

She added it up, because adding was what she was for.

A child's scald, closed at the top of the lane on the night of the fifth, closed so clean there was nothing left to show it had happened. The same night, under that cold chimney, a baker of thirty-one had gone to bed and woken with forty years gone out of him. Four doors was inside any radius the Tally had ever used, and one night was inside any time. Kit had watched a man go in at that door. On Mill Steps nobody cast, because the cost landed next door and you had to live beside it afterwards; and here was the house at the very top, and it had had a caster in.

It was a loss, a caster with a name, and a door, which was all the lane had ever asked her for. Written as a row, it would let her refer the man, and the Schedule would owe the Varleys their coin.

And the door would go in with it. The card would say where, and whose, and a card went into the drawer under the caster's name with every name on it. B. Hollin and the child called Posy would sit in the Office's books beside the hedge-worker, and by the end of the week the lane would know whose door had bought the baker's forty years.

She did not write row nine. The hour was gone, and what she had seen would keep until there was a desk under it.

The lodging's window had a light in it. While she watched, someone drew a cloth across it.
