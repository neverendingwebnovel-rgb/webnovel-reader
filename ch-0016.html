The Tallied City · Chapter 16

The Top of the House

The night porter took the bar off the Office's front door from the inside, an hour yet before light. Wren heard it before anything moved: iron drawn back through iron, a grunt, the knock of the bar's end set down on the boards. Then the door came open a foot and stopped, and he put his head round it to look at the street, and found her on the top step instead.

"You're early, miss."

"I am."

Behind her the street ran down the hill into Vellmark, still dark, with nothing on it but a cart and a lamplighter going along the other way with his pole, trimming the lamps that had burned low. The clock over the door stood at a quarter to the early bell.

He opened the door the rest of the way and stood aside for her. She had walked past this man most mornings for four years, coming in before light, soon after the bar was off, and going home at six as he came on, and in all that time she had given him good morning and good night and he had given them back. By the lamp over the door she saw that he was younger than she had taken him for, not much past thirty-five, with a front tooth gone at the top, so that his words came out round a small dark gap.

She did not go in. The fourth article said the hours returned were to be carried, and a thing that was carried had somebody under it.

"Who keeps the key to the chit box?"

He looked at her a moment. Then he unhooked a ring from his belt, turned it through his fingers past a dozen keys she could put no door to, and held up a short one, plain and dull, no bigger than the first joint of his thumb.

"I do, miss. That's the Loss Room's. The others are the others."

"When is it opened?"

He answered at once, and in good order, as if he had had the answer by him a long while and nobody had come for it.

"Every seventh night, once the floors are dark. Every box in the house goes into one bag, and Mr. Hesketh's book with it. I carry the bag up to the top of the house and tip it through the door, and I come down. The book's back on Mr. Hesketh's desk in the morning."

"Whose door?"

"The top of the house, miss. That's all anybody's ever called it to me."

"Who opens it?"

"Never seen it open."

"And you don't ask what's done with them?"

He hooked the ring back on his belt. "I carry it," he said. "Counting's up there."

There was no edge in it. He had settled long ago which part of the thing was his, and it was the stair. She thanked him. She had a foot on the threshold when he spoke again.

"Mr. Teague asked me that. A year back, about. I told him what I've told you."

Wren stood where she was.

"Tonight's a seventh night," he said, "as it falls."

"What's your name?"

"Sefton, miss."

"Thank you, Mr. Sefton."

He nodded and went out down the steps toward the street, done with the house till six, and Wren went in and up to the Loss Room, where Pell's card went through the slot of the box at the end of the row before anyone else had come up the stair.

* * *

The day went into its columns.

Toward the end of it she put her question to Pell across the corner of desk twelve, low, while he was ruling off a form: had he ever been up the last flight, above this floor?

"Once," he said. "The day I was sworn."

She waited, and Pell, who heard everything on the floor and could tell a clerk waiting from a clerk standing, blotted his line and set the blotter square.

"And I've kept it to once," he said. "Between ourselves, Miss Aske, I like to know where a door is. I've never needed it to be a door I use."

He turned the pen over in his fingers, and she saw him go back thirty years and come up again with something.

"The last flight is narrower than the rest," he said, "and steeper than it has any call to be. Keep to the wall. I was twenty, and I remember my knees." The corner of his mouth went in. "I'd a new waistcoat on and the buttons done right. I remember that too."

He drew the next form toward him, and she went back to her own.

* * *

When the six bell went, chairs scraped back down both lines of desks and the clerks went toward the stair in twos and threes, coats over their arms, and down. Wren put her coat on and went up.

Above the Loss Room's floor the main stair carried on as it did below it, broad and worn in the middle of every tread, and turned, and turned again. It passed doors on its landings, shut, with dark under them and nobody's step behind, rooms she had worked under every day and could not have named. What light there was came up the well from the lamps below and thinned as she climbed. Then the stair gave out on a landing no wider than itself, and from the back of it went the last flight: steep, so that she could have put a hand on the tread above her knee without bending, and so narrow her shoulder brushed the wall. She kept to the wall. Pell was right about the knees.

The landing at the top was small and bare, boards with nothing on them, and one door. Over it the Office's clock was cut into the lintel, the same face as over the front steps. At the height of her waist a slot ran across the door, wide, its lips faced with brass worn bright along the bottom edge, broad enough to take the mouth of a sack. There was no name on the door and no plate. Under it lay a line of lamplight, steady and yellow along the boards.

She knew the place: this was Pell's once, the day he was sworn. She had stood on these boards on her first morning with her hands at her sides, facing this shut door, and the Deputy Registrar had stood beside her with a card and read from it a line at a time, and Wren had said each line after her. She had been thinking about her wage: what would be in the envelope at the month's end, and whether it would cover the room. She could not now give back one line of what she had said. She had sworn it to a door that had stayed shut the whole time, and it had not seemed strange then, and she noticed it now and set it by.

Pell had stood here thirty years ago, in his new waistcoat. Teague, she supposed, forty-one years ago, the year he came on the floor.

She could not make the two halves of the Charter lie down in the one line, not on this landing: it allowed no working in the city greater than a hand's, and it was itself a working laid over the whole city, cast by the Office that kept this door.

Somebody was in there. A lamp burning at this hour on a floor nobody climbed to had somebody beside it. She could knock. She could put the warrant into whatever hand opened the door and show the line tonight, and it would be done.

Her hand came up off her side, knuckles toward the wood, and stopped short of it. With the other she had the warrant half out of her coat, the district sheet round it, before she knew she had reached.

All hours given to this inquiry, at whatever hour of the day or night, to be declared upon the slip and paid in the clerk's own chits. Showing the line was the inquiry's work, the last of it. A showing done tonight would be done in nobody's hour and stand on no slip, and of every line she had, this was the one she would not have standing on nothing. She had no chit. Her rent had gone on Fenn's hour, and the wage came at the month's end, so there was no buying one either, not at two silvers the hour.

She put her hand down. She slid the warrant back into her coat.

Then she sat down on the top step of the last flight with her shoulder to the wall, to wait for the bag. She wanted to see the hours carried. Taking it up is yours, Dunmore had said, and this was as far up as the house went.

Below her, a long way down, a door shut, and some time after that another. The cold came up the narrow flight and found her ankles. Later, much further down, a light went along a landing and stopped, and she heard a key, small and clear in the empty well, and the lid of a box, and the light moved on and came back the other way and paused again. Sefton, at his boxes, floor by floor. She lost him for a long while after that.

The line of light under the door did not move. Nobody crossed it; nothing was dragged or set down on the other side; it lay along the boards as still as a ruled line, and she found she could not look away from it, as she could not from a column that wanted adding.

A year back, about. Teague had asked Sefton where the bag went, and been told, and had gone on sitting opposite her through the year after it and said nothing of it. She looked at the boards of the landing, the bare middle of them and the dust in the corners, and there was nothing in them to say whether he had ever come up after his answer and sat where she was sitting.

She took out her notebook and her pencil and, on her knee, by that light, she wrote, and tore the leaf out along its stitching, and folded it once.

* * *

The lantern came first, a swing of yellow on the wall of the turn below, and then Sefton under it, climbing the last flight sideways with the wall at his shoulder and a bag over the other shoulder that bent him to it. It was a canvas bag, grey, the neck drawn shut with cord, and it hung heavy and shifting against his back.

He saw her on the step and was not surprised. He set the lantern on the boards.

"You're on my step, miss," he said. "I'll want it a minute."

She stood and went to the wall. He shrugged the bag round to his chest, loosened the cord, and set the mouth of it to the brass lip of the slot. Then he took the bag by its bottom corners and lifted.

The card went through. It went with a dry, close sound, like grain into a bin, card on card on card, sliding off the brass and away into the room beyond, and it went on. She had thought she knew what an hour sounded like going into a box. This was every box in the house, and it kept coming; he lifted the bag higher and shook it once, and still it came, and slowed, and thinned, and the last of it went through in a patter. Then something heavier slid out after it and went over with a flat knock: the book.

Sefton shook the empty bag out and folded it over his arm and picked up the lantern.

Wren took the folded leaf from her coat. She had written it on the step:

To the Registrar of the Office. W. Aske, desk nineteen, under the ninth article: a line to be shown. The clerk holds no hour to show it in.

She put it to the slot and let it go. It made no sound at all after the hours.

Sefton watched her do it. He held the lantern out over the stair for her without a word, and they went down, she in front with her hand on the wall and he behind with the light. They were three treads short of the turn when, above and behind them, paper slid on the boards.

They both stopped. Sefton lifted the lantern. On the landing, just out from under the door, in that same yellow, lay a folded sheet.

"That's not happened before," he said.

"Never?"

"Not to me, miss. Things go in." He kept the lantern up. "They don't come out."

It had come back in the time it took them to start down. Whoever sat behind that door had not had to be fetched to it, or wait for a lamp to be lit, or go looking for a pen.

She went back up for it. It was plain paper, white, not the Office's buff, and folded twice. She came down to the lantern before she opened it, and he held the light up for her without being asked, and looked down the stair while she read.

Desk nineteen. W. Aske. The line to be shown at six on the twenty-second, at this door, with the warrant. The hour herewith. L. Vane, Registrar of the Office.

She knew the hand. It was small, upright and careful, a hand that did not blot, and she had last seen it below the street in the admissions book, on the line of the fifteenth, with a tally drawn beside it that went nearly all the way round. Now it had an office written under it, and the one who had gone down after her on the fifteenth was the one the line was to be shown to.

Something was folded inside the sheet. She tipped it into her palm.

It was a chit, with the clock stamped in its corner as on every chit and no name on it. But every chit she had ever had, out of her own envelope or across a desk on the floor, had been stiff and sharp, the corners hard enough to mark a thumb. This one had gone soft. Its corners were rounded off. A crease ran across the middle of it and the crease had gone furry, and the card was grey down in the grain where fingers had been at it. It had lived in a great many pockets. It had gone across more than one desk, and more than one hand, before it came out under that door.

The fourth article named who might stamp an hour, not who might hold one; whoever had a chit in hand had its hour, and this one was in hers, so The hour herewith was an hour she could declare, at six tomorrow, at that door.

Sefton was still looking down the stair.

Wren took the warrant out of her coat and unfolded it from the district sheet and turned it to the particulars. In the light from the lantern she laid the summons along the foot of row twelve, under Read with the clerk. M. Dunmore., and laid the soft chit inside the summons, and folded the warrant shut over both, so that Dunmore's name would go up the last flight tomorrow in the same fold. She put the warrant back in her coat.

"Ready, miss?" said Sefton.

"Yes."

He went first this time, the lantern low, and she followed its light down.
